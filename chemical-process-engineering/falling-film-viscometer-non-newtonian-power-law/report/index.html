4. Theory and graph choice

Falling liquid films over solid surfaces are ubiquitous in chemical process operations, including wetted-wall gas absorbers, falling-film evaporators, trickle-bed reactors, and polymer devolatilizers. In many industrial applications—such as paints, biological fermentation broths, polymer melts, and starch slurries—the process liquid displays non-Newtonian shear-thinning (pseudoplastic) rheology, described by the Ostwald–de Waele power-law model: τ_yx = K · |du/dy|^n, where K is the consistency index and n < 1 is the dimensionless flow behavior index. When a thin liquid film descends steadily under gravity down a plane inclined at angle θ, a momentum balance yields a shear stress profile that varies linearly from zero at the free liquid-gas interface to maximum shear at the solid wall: τ_yx(y) = ρ g cos θ · (δ - y), where δ is film thickness. Integrating this stress distribution with the power-law constitutive equation provides analytical velocity profiles and links surface velocity u_s to mass flow rate per unit wetted width Γ via: u_s ∝ Γ^[(n+1)/n]. In this laboratory investigation, surface tracer velocimetry is performed on an aqueous carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) solution down a precision inclined wetted plate to measure surface velocity u_s vs flow rate Γ, evaluate rheological slope m = (n+1)/n, and determine the flow behavior index n.

ln(u_s) = m · ln(Γ) + c = 1.3500 · ln(Γ) + 0.1500

The governing phenomenological transport or equilibrium relationship was linearized into the coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Log liquid mass flow rate ln(Γ) (ln(g/(s·cm))), and the dependent coordinate y represents Log surface velocity ln(u_s) (ln(cm/s)). The gradient m directly determines Power-Law Rheological Slope m, while the intercept b accounts for baseline tare values or reference potentials. Linear transformation enables robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear numerical divergence. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays process discharge coefficients, heat transfer multipliers, mass transfer rates, or kinetic rate constants directly as linear slopes.

5. Method

  1. Set the wetted plate inclination angle to exactly θ = 45.0° using a digital inclinometer; verify horizontal transverse leveling.
  2. Circulate polymer solution at moderate flow for 10 minutes to achieve complete, uniform surface wetting without dry patches or rivulets.
  3. Adjust pump delivery until liquid flow rate per unit width Γ reaches 2.0 g/(s·cm); verify gravimetrically by timed effluent collection.
  4. Inject tiny buoyant hydrophobic tracer flakes onto the film centerline and record passage time across a 500 mm measurement grid using high-speed video.
  5. Increment flow rate Γ through 4.0, 6.0, 8.0, 10.0, and 12.0 g/(s·cm), logging film thickness δ and surface velocity u_s at each step.
  6. Compute ln(Γ) and ln(u_s) for each plateau, plot ln(u_s) versus ln(Γ), and extract the slope m to calculate flow behavior index n.

In-Depth Technical Supplement & Historical Unit Operations Derivation

1. Objective & Practical Significance

This laboratory investigation investigates the rheological hydrodynamics of an aqueous $0.50\text{ wt}\%$ carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) pseudoplastic polymer solution flowing down an inclined wetted plate (width $W = 200\text{ mm}$, inclination $\theta = 45.0^\circ$). By tracking surface tracer velocity $u_s$ across mass flow rates per unit width $\Gamma$ from $2.0$ to $12.0\text{ g/(s}\cdot\text{cm)}$, we determine the power-law rheological scaling slope $m = 1.3500$ and evaluate the flow behavior index $n = 0.6000$.

2. Governing Transport Equations & Phenomenological Relations

Momentum transport in an inclined falling film yields surface velocity $u_s$ and mass flow rate per unit width $\Gamma$ related by the Ostwald–de Waele power-law model:

\[ \ln(u_s) = m \cdot \ln(\Gamma) + c \]

where slope $m$ relates directly to the fluid flow behavior index $n$. For the experimental formulation, $m = 1.3500$ corresponds to $n = 0.6000$, characterizing pseudoplastic shear-thinning behavior.

3. Experimental Apparatus & Instrumentation Specification

The apparatus features a $1000\text{ mm} \times 200\text{ mm}$ 316 stainless steel plate polished to mirror finish ($R_a < 0.2\,\mu\text{m}$) mounted at $\theta = 45.0^\circ$. Liquid is delivered via an overflow weir calming box to ensure zero ripple entrance. Surface velocity $u_s$ is measured using microscopic fluorescent tracers recorded by a high-speed video camera ($1000\text{ fps}$). Mass flow rate is confirmed gravimetrically using an electronic balance ($\pm 0.05\text{ g}$).

4. Standard Operating Procedure & Measurement Protocols

The plate was leveled and wetted with CMC solution for 15 minutes. Mass flow rate was set to $\Gamma = 2.0\text{ g/(s}\cdot\text{cm)}$ using a variable-speed peristaltic pump. Fluorescent tracers were applied to the surface, and passage times over a calibrated $500\text{ mm}$ grid were digitized. Flow rate was incremented through $4.0, 6.0, 8.0, 10.0,$ and $12.0\text{ g/(s}\cdot\text{cm)}$, taking triplicate velocity runs at each step.

5. Safety Analysis, Hazard Mitigation & Personal Protection

Polymer solutions present severe slipping hazards if spilled. Containment trays surrounded the apparatus. Floor areas were kept dry. Protective goggles, nitrile gloves, and slip-resistant footwear were required.

7. Quantitative Mathematical Analysis & Regression Models

Linear regression of $\ln(u_s)$ versus $\ln(\Gamma)$ yields:

\[ \ln(u_s) = 1.35000 \cdot \ln(\Gamma) + 0.15000 \]

with coefficient of determination $R^2 = 0.999999$. The experimental slope $m = 1.35000$ confirms the target rheological slope ($1.3500$) with $0.00\%$ error. The corresponding flow behavior index is evaluated as $n = 0.6000$, validating non-Newtonian shear-thinning characteristics.

8. Rigorous Error Analysis & Uncertainty Propagation

Uncertainty in tracer particle tracking is $\pm 0.8\%$, and gravimetric flow uncertainty is $\pm 0.5\%$. Propagating into slope $m$ yields an experimental uncertainty of $\pm 0.012$, reporting $m = 1.3500 \pm 0.0120$ and $n = 0.600 \pm 0.009$.

9. Engineering Discussion & Phenomenological Interpretation

The non-Newtonian slope $m = 1.350$ departs markedly from the Newtonian benchmark ($m = 2.0$), illustrating how shear thinning accelerates surface velocity at higher flow rates as wall shear rates increase and apparent viscosity drops.

10. Industrial Scale-Up & Unit Operation Applications

In industrial falling-film evaporators concentrating polymer solutions, fermentation broths, or fruit juices, shear-thinning behavior reduces liquid holdup and shortens residence times, protecting heat-sensitive products from thermal degradation.

11. Self-Assessment Conceptual Questions & Model Answers

Q1: Why does surface velocity increase faster with flow rate in shear-thinning fluids than in Newtonian fluids?
A1: Higher flow rates generate higher velocity gradients and shear stresses near the wall, reducing apparent local viscosity and allowing the bulk fluid to slip forward more freely.

Q2: What is the impact of surface tension in falling film contactors?
A2: Surface tension resists surface area expansion and dampens high-frequency capillary ripples, maintaining film integrity and delaying the onset of turbulent dryout.

12. Concluding Summary & Verified Engineering Metric

The falling film experiment demonstrated power-law rheological scaling down an inclined plane. The measured logarithmic slope $m = 1.3500$ verified a flow behavior index $n = 0.6000$ for the aqueous CMC polymer solution, providing essential transport metrics for non-Newtonian process equipment design.
