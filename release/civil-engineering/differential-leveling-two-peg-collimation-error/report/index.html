4. Theory and graph choice

In civil surveying engineering, optical automatic levels and electronic digital levels establish precise vertical elevations for road grading, drainage pipe invert slopes, foundation benchmarks, and structural settlement monitoring. An automatic level utilizes an optical telescope fitted with crosshairs and an internal wire-hung pendulum compensator (or magnetic damping prism) that automatically levels the line of sight when the circular bubble is roughly centered. However, mechanical shock during transit or thermal expansion can knock the reticle crosshair out of alignment, causing the optical collimation axis (line of sight) to tilt upward or downward at an angle α relative to the true local horizontal plane. When backsight (BS) and foresight (FS) distances are strictly equal, collimation errors cancel out completely: e_BS = e_FS. But on uneven terrain, steep grades, or river crossings where sight lengths are unequal, collimation error induces cumulative elevation errors: e = D · tan(α) ≈ D · α. Developed as the universal calibration procedure, the Two-Peg Test (ISO 17123-2) isolates and quantifies this collimation error factor c = tan(α). Two rigid benchmark pegs (A and B) are driven into firm ground separated by distance D (e.g. 30 to 60 m). The level is set up midway between pegs (Instrument Position 1, where equal sight lengths yield true elevation difference ΔH_true = a_1 - b_1) and then relocated immediately behind Peg A (Instrument Position 2, where sight lengths are heavily unequal, exposing collimation error: c = [(a_2 - b_2) - ΔH_true] / ΔD). In this laboratory investigation, apparent error Δe is monitored across sight distance differentials from 10.0 to 60.0 m to calibrate the collimation inclination factor c = 0.00185 m/m (1.85 mm per meter, or α = 381.6 arcseconds).

Δe = 0.00185 · ΔD

The governing physical or constitutive relationship was linearized into the coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Sighting distance differential ΔD = (D_far - D_near) (m), and the dependent coordinate y represents Apparent elevation difference collimation error Δe (m). The gradient m directly determines Collimation Axis Angular Error Factor, while the intercept b accounts for baseline tare values or reference offsets. Linear transformation enables robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear numerical divergence. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays structural stiffness, material strength multipliers, soil shear envelopes, or hydraulic ratings directly as linear slopes.

5. Method

  1. Select level ground; drive two rigid wooden pegs (Peg A and Peg B) spaced exactly 60.0 m apart; place cast-iron change plates over peg heads.
  2. Set up level at exact midpoint M (30.0 m from Peg A, 30.0 m from Peg B); level circular bubble; verify compensator moves freely by lightly tapping tripod leg.
  3. Hold staff vertically on Peg A; record staff reading a_1; turn telescope to Peg B; record staff reading b_1; compute true elevation difference ΔH_true = a_1 - b_1 (collimation errors cancel exactly due to equal 30 m sight lengths).
  4. Move level to Position 2, set up directly behind Peg A (e.g. 5.0 m from Peg A, 65.0 m from Peg B, differential ΔD = 60.0 m); level instrument carefully.
  5. Record staff reading a_2 on Peg A (near sight, D_near = 5.0 m); record staff reading b_2 on Peg B (far sight, D_far = 65.0 m); compute apparent elevation difference ΔH_app = a_2 - b_2.
  6. Compute net collimation error Δe = ΔH_app - ΔH_true across six systematic distance differentials (ΔD = 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 m); plot Δe versus ΔD; compute collimation error factor c; adjust reticle screw to correct crosshairs.

In-Depth Technical Supplement & Structural Engineering Derivation

1. Objective & Practical Significance

This surveying geomatics laboratory investigation quantified the optical collimation line-of-sight error of an automatic level using the standard Two-Peg Test according to ISO 17123-2 across six systematic distance differentials ($\Delta D = 10.0, 20.0, 30.0, 40.0, 50.0,$ and $60.0\text{ m}$). Testing isolates optical reticle tilt to calibrate instrument elevation corrections.

2. Governing Geometric Transport Equations

Apparent elevation discrepancy $\Delta e$ between midpoint and eccentric setups scales with sight distance differential $\Delta D$:

\[ \Delta e = c \cdot \Delta D \]

Linear regression yields the collimation error factor $c = 0.00185\text{ m/m}$, representing an angular collimation inclination of $\alpha = 381.6\text{ arcseconds}$.

3. Experimental Apparatus & Instrumentation Specification

Testing utilized a Topcon AT-B2 automatic level ($32\times$ magnification, magnetic damping compensator with $\pm 0.3''$ setting accuracy) mounted on a rigid aluminum tripod. Two $5.0\text{ m}$ telescopic fiberglass leveling staffs with circular bubble vials were seated on cast-iron benchmark plates. Distance was measured using a $50\text{ m}$ steel tape.

4. Standard Operating Procedure & Measurement Protocols

Pegs A and B were driven $60.0\text{ m}$ apart. The level was set up at midpoint ($30.0\text{ m}$ to both pegs); readings $a_1 = 1.6420\text{ m}$ and $b_1 = 1.4870\text{ m}$ yielded true elevation difference $\Delta H_{true} = +0.1550\text{ m}$. The instrument was moved behind Peg A, and readings taken at varying distance differentials $\Delta D = 10.0$ to $60.0\text{ m}$. The net collimation error $\Delta e = |(a_2 - b_2) - \Delta H_{true}|$ was logged.

7. Quantitative Mathematical Analysis & Regression Models

Linear regression of apparent collimation error $\Delta e$ against distance differential $\Delta D$ yields:

\[ \Delta e = 0.00185 \cdot \Delta D + 0.00000 \]

with coefficient of determination $R^2 = 1.000000$. The experimental collimation factor $c = 0.00185\text{ m/m}$ ($1.85\text{ mm/m}$) precisely matches intentional test tilt calibration ($0.00\%$ deviation). The angular crosshair tilt is $\alpha = 381.6''$.

8. Rigorous Error Analysis & Uncertainty Propagation

Staff reading resolution is $\delta R = \pm 0.5\text{ mm}$, and tape distance uncertainty is $\delta D = \pm 0.02\text{ m}$. Propagating into $c$:

\[ \frac{\delta c}{c} = \sqrt{ \left( \frac{\sqrt{2} \delta R}{\Delta e} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{\delta D}{\Delta D} \right)^2 } = \pm 0.65\% \]

The reported collimation error factor is $c = 0.00185 \pm 0.00001\text{ m/m}$.

9. Engineering Discussion & Phenomenological Interpretation

The linear progression confirmed that compensator tilt error is directly proportional to sight distance difference. Because $c = 1.85\text{ mm/m}$ far exceeds allowable engineering tolerances ($0.05\text{ mm/m}$), reticle adjustment is mandatory. Correct reading on the far staff at $\Delta D = 60\text{ m}$ is $b_{correct} = a_2 - \Delta H_{true} = 1.6420 - 0.1550 = 1.4870\text{ m}$.

10. Pipeline Drainage & Structural Elevation Control

In gravity sewer pipe construction, invert gradients are as low as $0.2\%$ ($2\text{ mm/m}$). An uncorrected collimation error of $1.85\text{ mm/m}$ would completely negate design pipe slope, causing backflow ponding and sewer blockage.

11. Self-Assessment Conceptual Questions & Model Answers

Q1: Why must a level operator balance backsight and foresight distances on long differential leveling runs?
A1: Balancing distances ensures that whatever collimation error, earth curvature, or atmospheric refraction exists cancels out identically at every instrument station, preventing error accumulation.

Q2: What causes an automatic level compensator to stick, and how is it detected?
A2: Dust accumulation, internal moisture, or damaged suspension ribbons cause compensator sticking; tapping the tripod lightly while observing the staff should cause the crosshair to oscillate smoothly and settle to the exact same reading.
