Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of SLE Eutectic Phase Diagram.

Solid-liquid equilibrium (SLE) phase diagrams are foundational to chemical engineering crystallization, melt purification, pharmaceutical co-crystal formulation, metallurgy, and thermal energy storage phase-change materials (PCMs). When two chemical species are completely miscible in the liquid melt but mutually immiscible in the solid crystalline state, they form a simple binary eutectic system. The classic chemical engineering model system is the binary mixture of naphthalene (component A, pure melting point T_mA = 80.20 °C) and biphenyl (component B, pure melting point T_mB = 69.20 °C). In this system, adding solute B to pure liquid A depresses the liquidus temperature—the temperature at which the first pure crystal of component A nucleates from the melt. According to the Schröder–van Laar equation derived from chemical potential equality between pure solid A and liquid solution A: ln(γ_A · x_A) = -[ΔH_fus,A / R] · [1/T - 1/T_mA], where ΔH_fus,A is the standard enthalpy of fusion (18.98 kJ/mol for naphthalene) and γ_A is the liquid-phase activity coefficient. In the dilute solute regime (x_B = 1 - x_A << 1), the liquid behaves nearly ideally (γ_A ≈ 1.00), and expanding the reciprocal temperature term in a first-order series yields the linear cryoscopic freezing point depression relation: ΔT_f = T_mA - T = S · x_B, where the cryoscopic limiting slope is given by S = R · T_mA^2 / ΔH_fus,A = 58.5000 °C (K). Continued addition of solute B depresses the liquidus until it intersects the second liquidus curve of component B at the eutectic point (T_eutectic ≈ 39.4 °C, x_B,eutectic ≈ 0.45), where both solid A and solid B crystallize simultaneously at a unique invariant temperature. In this laboratory experiment, an instrumented thermal analysis freezing point apparatus records time-temperature cooling curves across biphenyl mole fractions from 0.04 to 0.24 to identify the liquidus break temperatures, confirm the linear cryoscopic slope S = 58.5000 °C, and construct the complete SLE phase boundary.

Solid-Liquid Equilibrium & Chemical Potential Equality

Solid-liquid equilibrium (SLE) describes phase coexistence between a liquid solution and solid crystalline phases. When a binary liquid mixture of species $A$ and $B$ cools to its liquidus curve, the chemical potential of component $A$ in the liquid solution equals the chemical potential of pure crystalline solid $A$ (assuming negligible solid solubility):

\[ \mu_A^L(T, P, x_A) = \mu_A^S(T, P) \]

Expressing liquid chemical potential through activity $a_A = \gamma_A x_A$ relative to pure liquid standard state $\mu_A^{\circ,L}(T, P)$:

\[ \mu_A^{\circ,L}(T, P) + R T \ln(\gamma_A x_A) = \mu_A^S(T, P) \] \[ \ln(\gamma_A x_A) = \frac{\mu_A^S(T, P) - \mu_A^{\circ,L}(T, P)}{R T} = -\frac{\Delta G_{\text{fus},A}(T)}{R T} \]

Differentiating with respect to temperature using the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation $\frac{\partial(\Delta G/T)}{\partial T} = -\frac{\Delta H}{T^2}$ and integrating from the pure component melting point $T_{mA}$ where $\Delta G_{\text{fus},A}(T_{mA}) = 0$ yields the rigorous Schröder–van Laar equation:

\[ \ln(\gamma_A x_A) = -\frac{\Delta H_{\text{fus},A}}{R} \left( \frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_{mA}} \right) + \frac{\Delta C_{p,A}}{R} \left( \frac{T_{mA} - T}{T} - \ln\frac{T_{mA}}{T} \right) \]

Dilute Solution Cryoscopy & Linear Limiting Slope

In dilute solutions ($x_B = 1 - x_A \ll 1$), the heat capacity correction term is negligible, the liquid phase behaves ideally ($\gamma_A \approx 1.00$), and the natural logarithm expands as $\ln(1 - x_B) \approx -x_B$. Furthermore:

\[ \frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_{mA}} = \frac{T_{mA} - T}{T \, T_{mA}} \approx \frac{\Delta T_f}{T_{mA}^2} \]

Substituting into the Schröder–van Laar relation yields the classical linear cryoscopic freezing point depression equation:

\[ \Delta T_f = T_{mA} - T = S \cdot x_B = \left( \frac{R \, T_{mA}^2}{\Delta H_{\text{fus},A}} \right) \cdot x_B \]

For naphthalene ($T_{mA} = 80.20^\circ\text{C} = 353.35\text{ K}$, $\Delta H_{\text{fus},A} = 18.98\text{ kJ/mol}$):

\[ S = \frac{8.31446 \cdot (353.35)^2}{18,980} = 54.69\text{ K (theoretical ideal)} \]

With slight real-solution activity coefficient departure (Margules excess energy), the calibrated empirical slope is $S = 58.5000^\circ\text{C}$.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the Gibbs Phase Rule and what degrees of freedom exist at a binary eutectic point?

The phase rule is F = C - P + 1 (at constant atmospheric pressure); at the eutectic point of a binary system (C = 2), three phases coexist in equilibrium (liquid melt, solid A, and solid B, so P = 3), resulting in F = 2 - 3 + 1 = 0 degrees of freedom, meaning the eutectic temperature and composition are invariant.

How does supercooling affect cooling curves and how can it be distinguished from the true freezing point?

Supercooling causes liquid temperature to dip below the equilibrium freezing point before crystal nucleation begins; once nucleation occurs, latent heat rapidly recalesces the melt back up to the true liquidus plateau, which represents the equilibrium freezing point.

Why is the simple eutectic model valid for the naphthalene-biphenyl system?

Naphthalene (fused bicyclic aromatic ring) and biphenyl (two linked phenyl rings) have different molecular shapes and crystal lattices that prevent substitution into each other's solid crystals, resulting in negligible solid solubility while forming a nearly ideal liquid solution.

What is the physical interpretation of the cryoscopic constant K_f?

The cryoscopic constant K_f = (R · T_m^2 · M_solvent) / (1000 · ΔH_fus) represents the freezing point depression produced by an ideal solute at 1.0 molal concentration; it is an intrinsic thermodynamic property of the solvent matrix alone.

How can the eutectic composition and temperature be calculated theoretically?

By solving the two simultaneous Schröder–van Laar equations for component A and component B: ln(x_A) = -[ΔH_fus,A / R] [1/T_e - 1/T_mA] and ln(1 - x_A) = -[ΔH_fus,B / R] [1/T_e - 1/T_mB]; their simultaneous intersection gives T_e and x_A,eutectic.

What happens to a liquid mixture at the exact eutectic composition upon cooling?

It cools as a single homogeneous liquid until reaching the eutectic temperature, whereupon it solidifies isothermally at constant temperature into a fine, intergrown microstructural lamellar solid mixture of pure A and pure B without any prior liquidus slope break.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Maintain continuous mechanical stirring during cooling to prevent severe supercooling and ensure uniform temperature throughout the melt.
  • Operate inside a ventilated fume hood to avoid inhaling volatile aromatic naphthalene and biphenyl vapors at 95 °C.
  • Do not allow cooling water or oil bath fluids to enter the sample tube, as trace moisture forms a ternary system that distorts freezing points.
  • Verify complete remelting and homogeneous dissolution of all solid crystals before commencing each cooling curve run.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (0.04 to 0.24 mol/mol).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
