4. Theory and graph choice

Excess enthalpy of mixing (H^E, also referred to as heat of mixing or excess enthalpy of solution) is a critical thermodynamic property in chemical engineering process design, heat exchanger sizing, reactor thermal management, and flash separation calculations. For an ideal solution, the enthalpy of mixing is strictly zero (ΔH_mix^ideal = 0), meaning mixing pure components at constant temperature and pressure releases or absorbs no heat. In real mixtures, however, breaking like-molecule intermolecular bonds (A-A and B-B) and creating unlike-molecule bonds (A-B) results in an energy imbalance. In the binary ethanol-water system, mixing is exothermic at ambient temperatures (H^E < 0) due to strong cross-association hydrogen bonding between water molecules and ethanol hydroxyl groups, releasing significant heat. The molar excess enthalpy is modeled using the Redlich–Kister polynomial expansion: H^E = x_1 · x_2 · [A_0 + A_1 · (x_1 - x_2) + A_2 · (x_1 - x_2)^2 + ...], where x_1 and x_2 = 1 - x_1 are mole fractions and A_k are temperature-dependent empirical interaction coefficients. In a symmetric first-approximation formulation, excess enthalpy scales directly with the product of the mole fractions: H^E = A_0 · [x_1 · (1 - x_1)], where A_0 is the leading Redlich–Kister parameter. Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC) provides the highest precision method for measuring heats of mixing. Operating under active power-compensation feedback, the ITC sample cell (volume 1.40 mL) is maintained at strictly constant temperature (25.000 ± 0.0001 °C) while automated precision syringe injections (10.0 μL) deliver titrant into the stirred sample cell. Thermal compensation power pulses are integrated to quantify the enthalpy change of each injection with sub-microjoule resolution. In this laboratory experiment, an automated ITC unit measures the heat of mixing across composition products x_1·(1 - x_1) from 0.05 to 0.25 at 25.000 °C to determine the Redlich–Kister parameter A_0 = -3450.0000 J/mol.

H^E = A_0 · [x_1 · (1 - x_1)] = -3450.0000 · [x_1 · (1 - x_1)]

The governing phenomenological transport or equilibrium relationship was linearized into the coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Composition factor x_1·(1 - x_1), and the dependent coordinate y represents Molar excess enthalpy H^E (J/mol). The gradient m directly determines Redlich–Kister Excess Enthalpy Parameter A_0, while the intercept b accounts for baseline tare values or reference potentials. Linear transformation enables robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear numerical divergence. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays process discharge coefficients, heat transfer multipliers, mass transfer rates, or kinetic rate constants directly as linear slopes.

5. Method

  1. Degas HPLC water and absolute ethanol under vacuum for 15 minutes; fill reference cell with degassed water and establish 25.000 °C baseline.
  2. Load sample cell with 1.40 mL degassed pure water; fill automated injection syringe with 250 μL pure absolute ethanol, purging air bubbles.
  3. Program ITC sequence for 20 successive injections of 10.0 μL with 150 s spacing at 750 RPM stirring speed.
  4. Discard initial 0.4 μL pre-injection; initiate automated titration run, logging power compensation response curves (μcal/s) versus time.
  5. Integrate power compensation peaks for each injection to determine differential heats of mixing dQ; calculate cumulative molar excess enthalpy H^E.
  6. Plot molar excess enthalpy H^E versus composition factor x_1·(1 - x_1); fit linear regression through origin to extract A_0.

1. Objective & Practical Significance

This laboratory investigation determines the molar excess enthalpy of mixing ($H^E$) for the binary ethanol (1) + water (2) system at $25.000^\circ\text{C}$ using power-compensation Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC). Across composition factors $x_1 \cdot (1 - x_1)$ from $0.05$ to $0.25$, we evaluate exothermic mixing heat pulses, verify the Redlich–Kister linear relationship $H^E = A_0 \cdot [x_1 (1 - x_1)]$, and determine the leading parameter $A_0 = -3450.0000\text{ J/mol}$, establishing fundamental enthalpy data for chemical process design.

2. Governing Transport Equations & Phenomenological Relations

The molar excess enthalpy of mixing is modeled by the symmetric Redlich–Kister equation:

\[ H^E = A_0 \cdot [x_1 \cdot (1 - x_1)] = -3450.0000 \cdot [x_1 \cdot (1 - x_1)] \]

where $A_0 = -3450.0000\text{ J/mol}$. The partial molar excess enthalpy of ethanol at infinite dilution is $\bar{H}_1^{E,\infty} = A_0 = -3450.0000\text{ J/mol}$.

Testing was conducted on a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC power-compensation calorimeter equipped with a $1.40\text{ mL}$ coin-shaped Hastelloy cell, a $250\text{ μL}$ precision automated syringe with an integrated paddle stirrer ($750\text{ RPM}$), and active dual Peltier thermoelectric coolers maintaining cell temperature at $25.0000 \pm 0.0001^\circ\text{C}$.

9. Engineering Discussion & Phenomenological Interpretation

The exothermic excess enthalpy ($H^E < 0$) arises from strong hydrogen bond formation between water protons and ethanol oxygen lone pairs, which releases more energy than is required to disrupt bulk ethanol-ethanol and water-water interactions. Maximum heat release of $-862.5\text{ J/mol}$ occurs near $x_{\text{EtOH}} \approx 0.5$, reflecting optimal stoichiometry for cross-species hydrogen-bonded networks.

11. Self-Assessment Conceptual Questions & Model Answers

Q1: Why does the excess enthalpy of mixing influence the temperature dependence of VLE?
A1: By the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation $[\partial(\ln \gamma_i) / \partial T]_P = -\bar{H}_i^E / (R T^2)$; non-zero excess enthalpy dictates how activity coefficients shift with boiling temperature, determining the shape of VLE envelope curves.

Q2: Why is the sample cell coin-shaped in modern ITC designs?
A2: A coin-shaped cell maximizes the surface-area-to-volume ratio and reduces path length to the thermoelectric Peltier heat sink, enabling rapid thermal response time ($< 10\text{ s}$) and sensitive power feedback.
