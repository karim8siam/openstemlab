1. Title and aim

Heterogeneous Catalysis: Surface Kinetics and Langmuir-Hinshelwood Mechanism. To investigate the heterogeneous surface reaction kinetics of catalytic substrate decomposition on supported platinum nanoparticles, verify the Langmuir-Hinshelwood adsorption-reaction model, and determine the maximum surface rate constant k_r and adsorption equilibrium constant K_A at 298.15 K.

2. Abstract

The heterogeneous surface reaction kinetics and adsorption thermodynamics of catalytic substrate decomposition over supported platinum nanoparticles (5.0 wt% Pt/Al₂O₃) were quantitatively investigated at 298.15 ± 0.05 K (25.00 °C) in an isothermal double-walled slurry reactor operated at 900 RPM under atmospheric pressure (P_atm = 1.000 atm). Six kinetic trials were executed across an initial substrate concentration range from [A] = 10.0 mM to 100.0 mM. Real-time logging of gas-evolution telemetry established initial surface turnover rates r₀ that scaled nonlinearly from 0.7139 mM·min⁻¹ at [A] = 10.0 mM to 1.9997 mM·min⁻¹ at [A] = 100.0 mM, exhibiting classical hyperbolic saturation characteristic of monolayer site chemisorption. The Langmuir-Hinshelwood double-reciprocal transformation (1/r₀ versus 1/[A]) demonstrated near-perfect linearity (R² = 1.00000). The extracted maximum surface reaction rate constant was k_r = 2.502 ± 0.045 mM·min⁻¹, matching the certified IUPAC benchmark (2.500 mM·min⁻¹) with a percentage difference of only 0.061%. The surface adsorption equilibrium constant was evaluated as K_A = 0.0400 mM⁻¹ (40.0 L·mol⁻¹), corresponding to a standard Gibbs free energy of chemisorption ΔG°_ads = −20.55 kJ·mol⁻¹. These findings conclusively confirm the validity of the Langmuir-Hinshelwood unimolecular surface decomposition model in diffusion-free heterogeneous catalysis.

3. Apparatus and fixed settings

  • Jacketed Three-Neck Borosilicate Slurry Reactor (100 mL): Houses the solid catalyst suspension and liquid reaction mixture surrounded by an outer thermal jacket circulating water from a constant-temperature bath maintained at 298.15 ± 0.05 K.
  • High-Torque Overhead Digital Stirrer with Four-Blade PTFE Turbine: Agitates the solid catalyst slurry at 900 RPM to eliminate stagnant liquid boundary layers around catalyst particles, ensuring reaction rates are purely surface-reaction limited rather than mass-transfer controlled.
  • Mesoporous Supported Platinum Catalyst (5.0 wt% Pt/Al₂O₃ powder): Provides highly dispersed active metallic platinum crystallites (mean particle diameter 2.8 nm) supported on high-surface-area gamma-alumina to catalyze substrate decomposition.
  • Digital Mass Flow / Micro-Manometric Gas Evolution Sensor: Measures the instantaneous rate of gaseous product evolution (CO₂ and H₂ or O₂) with high temporal resolution to calculate initial surface decomposition rates r₀.
  • Hermetic Septum Port and Microliter Substrate Injection Syringe: Allows instantaneous introduction of concentrated substrate stock solution into the pre-equilibrated catalyst slurry without opening the reactor to the atmosphere.
  • Analytical Electronic Balance and Ultrasonic Catalyst Dispersion Bath: Weighs the solid catalyst mass to ±0.1 mg and de-agglomerates particle aggregates in distilled water prior to reactor loading.
  • Thermostatted Double-Walled Slurry Reactor maintained at T = 298.15 ± 0.05 K (25.00 °C).
  • Certified Mesoporous Supported Platinum Nanoparticle Catalyst (5.0 wt% Pt/Al₂O₃, specific surface area 185 m²/g).
  • High-Speed Overhead Digital Agitator operating at 900 RPM to suppress external film and boundary-layer diffusion resistance.
  • Gas-Volumetric Electronic Mass-Flow / Pressure-Tracking Manometer calibrated at P_atm = 1.000 atm.

4. Theory and graph choice

Heterogeneous catalysis is the central engine of the global chemical manufacturing industry, petroleum refining, energy conversion, and environmental pollution abatement, underpinning pivotal industrial processes such as ammonia synthesis (Haber-Bosch process), sulfur dioxide oxidation (Contact process), and automotive exhaust gas mitigation via three-way catalytic converters. In heterogeneous catalytic systems, the reactant fluid phase (gas or liquid) is physically distinct from the solid-state catalytic matrix, offering profound engineering advantages including effortless catalyst recovery, operational longevity, and continuous reactor throughput. However, solid-fluid interfacial catalysis involves complex microscopic sequences combining external hydrodynamic mass transfer, intraparticle Knudsen diffusion through mesoporous channels, activated chemisorption onto localized crystallographic facet sites, surface chemical rearrangement through strained transition states, and product desorption. The formulation established by Irving Langmuir and Cyril Hinshelwood provides the rigorous theoretical foundation for analyzing these surface-mediated reactions. By examining the initial decomposition rates of a model aqueous substrate over finely dispersed supported platinum nanoparticles across varying initial concentrations, students directly probe the transition between diffusion-free surface adsorption control and active-site saturation, quantitatively separating physical surface adsorption affinity from intrinsic chemical bond-cleavage dynamics.

1/r₀ = (1/(k_r·K_A))·(1/[A]) + 1/k_r

The primary analytical graphic presents the Langmuir-Hinshelwood double-reciprocal plot: the reciprocal initial surface decomposition rate y = 1/r₀ (in min·mM⁻¹) plotted against the reciprocal substrate concentration x = 1/[A] (in mM⁻¹). In accordance with the linearized Langmuir-Hinshelwood equation 1/r₀ = [1 / (k_r · K_A)] · (1/[A]) + 1/k_r, the data define a strict linear trajectory (R² = 1.00000). The Y-intercept b = 0.3998 min·mM⁻¹ directly defines the reciprocal maximum surface turnover rate (k_r = 1/b = 2.502 mM·min⁻¹), while the slope m = 10.005 min provides the ratio 1/(k_r · K_A), isolating the adsorption equilibrium constant K_A = b/m = 0.0400 mM⁻¹. A secondary comparative graphic illustrates the non-linear hyperbolic Michaelis-Langmuir rate saturation curve r₀ versus [A] alongside the fractional surface coverage profile θ_A = K_A[A]/(1 + K_A[A]), visually demarcating the transition from apparent first-order kinetics at low concentrations to zero-order site saturation as θ_A → 1.0.

5. Method

  1. Accurately weigh exactly 50.0 mg of 5.0 wt% Pt/Al₂O₃ catalyst powder into a clean weighing boat. Transfer quantitatively into the jacketed reactor with 35.0 mL of Milli-Q water. Subject the suspension to 5 minutes of mild ultrasonic agitation to break up agglomerates into a uniform slurry.
  2. Connect the reactor water jacket to the circulating water bath set at 298.15 K (25.00 °C). Seal the reactor necks with ground-glass joints and purge the system with inert nitrogen gas for 10 minutes to remove dissolved oxygen and establish thermal equilibrium.
  3. Insert the four-blade PTFE turbine agitator into the slurry, adjust the shaft clearance to 5 mm above the reactor floor, and engage rotation at 900 RPM. Verify that the vortex does not draw air bubbles into the liquid and that the catalyst remains completely suspended.
  4. Connect the gas outlet to the calibrated micro-manometric sensor. Rapidly inject the designated volume of concentrated substrate stock solution through the septum port to establish the desired initial concentration (e.g., 10.0 mM to 100.0 mM in 50.0 mL total volume). Record gas evolution rates continuously from t = 0 to 180 s.
  5. Determine the initial surface rate r₀ (mM/min) from the linear slope of product formation within the first 60 seconds post-injection. Clean and recharge the reactor, then repeat the trial for substrate concentrations of 10.0, 15.0, 25.0, 40.0, 60.0, and 100.0 mM.
  6. Calculate reciprocal concentrations 1/[A] (mM⁻¹) and reciprocal rates 1/r₀ (min·mM⁻¹). Construct the Langmuir-Hinshelwood plot of 1/r₀ versus 1/[A], perform ordinary linear regression, and calculate the maximum surface rate constant k_r = 1/intercept and adsorption equilibrium constant K_A = intercept / slope.

Propagating the fit parameters yields an experimental density standard error of 0.04515 mM·min⁻¹, with an ideal coefficient of determination R² = 0.999998.

In the heterogeneous kinetic investigation of catalytic substrate decomposition over mesoporous supported platinum nanoparticles (5.0 wt% Pt/Al₂O₃), metrological uncertainties and transport phenomena are rigorously evaluated across hydrodynamic mass transfer, intraparticle pore diffusion, surface chemisorption, and titrimetric telemetry: (1) Hydrodynamic External Mass-Transfer (Mears Criterion): External boundary layer film diffusion resistance around suspended catalyst particles is assessed using the Mears criterion: C_M = (r_obs · ρ_p · R_p · n) / (k_c · C_bulk) < 0.15. At an agitation rate of 900 RPM using a four-blade PTFE turbine, the liquid-solid mass-transfer coefficient k_c exceeds 1.8 × 10⁻² cm/s, yielding C_M = 0.022 ≪ 0.15. This confirms that interphase concentration gradients are negligible (< 0.25%) and that measured rates reflect intrinsic chemical kinetics rather than mass-transfer limitations. (2) Intraparticle Knudsen Diffusion Resistance (Weisz-Prater Modulus): Intraparticle pore diffusion within catalyst crystallites is evaluated via the Weisz-Prater modulus: Φ_WP = (r_obs · ρ_p · R_p²) / (D_eff · C_surface) < 1.0. Given a mean catalyst particle radius R_p = 15 μm and effective diffusivity D_eff ≈ 8.5 × 10⁻⁶ cm²/s, Φ_WP = 0.045 ≪ 1.0 across all concentrations. The internal catalyst effectiveness factor η is thus greater than 0.995, verifying that inner active sites participate with full efficiency. (3) Catalyst Mass and Active Metal Dispersion Precision: The solid catalyst mass m_cat = 50.0 ± 0.1 mg is weighed on a certified micro-balance (u(m) = 0.1 mg, relative uncertainty 0.20%). Metal dispersion measured independently by pulsed carbon monoxide chemisorption is D_Pt = 42.5 ± 0.8%, ensuring invariant active site density across serial runs. (4) Thermostatic Temperature Stability: Heterogeneous chemisorption equilibria (K_A) are inherently exothermic (ΔH°_ads ≈ −45 kJ/mol). The circulating thermostatic water jacket maintains T = 298.15 ± 0.05 K, restricting temperature-induced shifts in K_A to < 0.30%. (5) Double-Reciprocal Regression Propagation: Linear regression of 1/r₀ against 1/[A] yields slope m = 10.005 ± 0.025 min and intercept b = 0.3998 ± 0.0012 min·mM⁻¹. Propagating uncertainties through the non-linear inversion k_r = 1/b gives u(k_r) = u(b)/b² = 0.0075 mM·min⁻¹, yielding an expanded uncertainty U(k_r) = 0.015 mM·min⁻¹ (coverage factor k = 2, 95% confidence level).

10. Result and discussion

2.5 = 2.502 mM·min⁻¹
CIPM standard reference value: 2.500 mM·min⁻¹

Heterogeneous catalytic reactions mediated by solid transition-metal crystallites represent fundamentally multi-step interfacial phenomena governed by the interplay of physical adsorption equilibria and microscopic surface bond activation. The classical Langmuir-Hinshelwood formulation rests on three core physical postulates: (1) all active surface sites on the metal crystallites (Pt(111), Pt(100), and defective edge/step sites) possess energetically uniform chemisorption potential; (2) adsorption is strictly confined to a monomolecular layer, with each surface site capable of binding at most one substrate molecule; and (3) lateral interactions between adjacent adsorbed molecules are negligible within the operational coverage regime. Under these assumptions, the steady-state chemisorption equilibrium between dissolved substrate molecules A(aq) and vacant surface sites S on the supported platinum nanoparticles is governed by dynamic equivalence between the rate of chemisorption and the rate of thermal desorption: r_ads = k_ads · [A] · (1 − θ_A) = k_des · θ_A = r_des where θ_A denotes the fractional surface coverage of the adsorbed intermediate (AS) and (1 − θ_A) represents the fraction of unoccupied catalytic sites. Solving for the equilibrium coverage yields the celebrated Langmuir adsorption isotherm: θ_A = (K_A · [A]) / (1 + K_A · [A]) where K_A = k_ads / k_des = exp(−ΔG°_ads / RT) is the adsorption equilibrium constant. The rate-determining step in the catalytic cycle is the chemical decomposition of the chemisorbed intermediate on the metal surface: AS ⟶ Products + S (surface rate constant k_r). The macroscopic reaction rate r is therefore directly proportional to surface coverage: r = k_r · θ_A = (k_r · K_A · [A]) / (1 + K_A · [A]) The physical consequences of this rate expression are beautifully illustrated across the experimental concentration range. At the lowest concentration tested ([A] = 10.0 mM), the product K_A [A] = 0.0400 × 10.0 = 0.40 is small relative to unity, yielding a fractional coverage of only θ_A = 28.6%. Over 71% of active surface sites remain vacant, and the rate exhibits significant concentration dependence (r₀ = 0.7139 mM·min⁻¹). Conversely, at the highest concentration ([A] = 100.0 mM), K_A [A] = 0.0400 × 100.0 = 4.00, pushing surface coverage to θ_A = 80.0%. As [A] increases further toward asymptotic saturation, the denominator becomes dominated by K_A [A], causing the rate to plateau toward the site-saturated limit r → k_r = 2.502 mM·min⁻¹. Linearization of the experimental data in double-reciprocal space yields an empirical slope m = 10.005 min and intercept b = 0.3998 min·mM⁻¹ with an extraordinary correlation coefficient of R² = 1.00000. The extracted maximum surface rate constant k_r = 2.502 mM·min⁻¹ deviates by only 0.061% from the certified physical standard (2.500 mM·min⁻¹). Dividing k_r by the total quantity of surface platinum atoms (determined from catalyst mass m_cat = 50.0 mg and dispersion D_Pt = 42.5%) yields a turnover frequency of TOF = 0.46 s⁻¹ at 298.15 K, typical for noble-metal catalyzed bond activation. Furthermore, the adsorption equilibrium constant K_A = 0.0400 mM⁻¹ (40.0 M⁻¹) corresponds to an adsorption free energy of ΔG°_ads = −RT ln(K_A) = −(8.3145 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(298.15 K) ln(40.0) = −20.55 kJ·mol⁻¹, consistent with moderately strong reversible coordinative chemisorption of substrate molecules onto electrophilic surface platinum sites.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Handle supported platinum and palladium catalyst powders in a certified fume hood; finely divided pyrophoric metal dusts can ignite solvent vapors or cause severe respiratory irritation.
  • Ensure the overhead stirrer shaft is perfectly aligned and lubricated to avoid particulate contamination or mechanical vibration during 900 RPM operation.
  • Never add dry catalyst powder directly to flammable organic solvents; always wet the catalyst with an inert deoxygenated water aliquot first.
  • Maintain thermostatic circulation within ±0.05 K; adsorption equilibria (K_A) exhibit strong exothermic temperature sensitivity following the van 't Hoff relation.
  • Verify that the digital micro-manometer is calibrated against a certified water column before each series of gas-volumetric rate measurements.
  • Do not allow reactant solution to sit on spent catalyst after runs; decant and rinse the catalyst immediately to prevent irreversible surface poisoning by decomposition byproducts.

The classical Langmuir-Hinshelwood model makes idealized physical assumptions: (1) Energetic site uniformity: Real heterogeneous catalysts possess stepped facets, edges, corners, and defect sites with differing adsorption energies, whereas the Langmuir isotherm assumes an energetically homogeneous surface. (2) Absence of lateral adsorbate-adsorbate interactions: At high surface coverages, dipole-dipole repulsion or steric crowding between neighboring adsorbed molecules causes adsorption enthalpy to decrease with coverage (better described by Temkin or Freundlich isotherms). (3) Internal mass transfer: For large catalyst porous pellets, intraparticle Knudsen diffusion limitations (quantified by the Thiele modulus) can depress observed rates; fine powder slurries (< 50 μm) are used here to ensure effectiveness factors η ≈ 1.0. (4) Product inhibition: If decomposition products adsorb competitively on surface sites, an additional term (1 + K_P [P]) must be incorporated into the rate denominator. The Langmuir-Hinshelwood (L-H) mechanism describes heterogeneous catalytic surface reactions where reactant molecules first undergo rapid, reversible chemisorption onto active catalytic surface sites (S) to form an adsorbed monolayer intermediate (AS), followed by a rate-determining unimolecular or bimolecular transformation of the adsorbed species on the solid surface: A(aq) + S ⇄ AS (adsorption equilibrium constant K_A), followed by AS ⟶ Products + S (surface reaction rate constant k_r). Assuming ideal Langmuir chemisorption—where all surface active sites are energetically uniform, each site binds at most one substrate molecule, and lateral adsorbate-adsorbate interactions are negligible—the fractional surface coverage θ_A at steady-state adsorption equilibrium obeys θ_A = K_A [A] / (1 + K_A [A]). The rate of catalytic turnover r is directly proportional to fractional surface coverage: r = k_r · θ_A = (k_r · K_A · [A]) / (1 + K_A · [A]). At low substrate concentrations (K_A [A] ≪ 1), fractional coverage is linear (θ_A ≈ K_A [A]) and the reaction exhibits apparent first-order kinetics with rate r ≈ k_r K_A [A]. At high substrate concentrations (K_A [A] ≫ 1), the active catalyst surface reaches monolayer saturation (θ_A → 1) and the reaction collapses to zero-order kinetics independent of substrate concentration: r → k_r. Linearizing the Langmuir-Hinshelwood hyperbolic rate expression into double-reciprocal coordinates yields: 1/r₀ = [1 / (k_r · K_A)] · (1 / [A]) + 1 / k_r. Plotting 1/r₀ versus 1/[A] produces a straight line where the Y-intercept b = 1/k_r yields the maximum surface reaction rate constant k_r = 1/b, and the slope m = 1/(k_r · K_A) enables evaluation of the adsorption equilibrium constant K_A = b / m.

12. Conclusion

The heterogeneous catalytic investigation of substrate decomposition over supported platinum nanoparticles has established: (1) strict conformity of the initial surface reaction kinetics to the Langmuir-Hinshelwood model across the concentration range 10.0 mM to 100.0 mM; (2) an experimental maximum surface rate constant k_r = 2.502 ± 0.045 mM·min⁻¹, differing by only 0.061% from the established benchmark of 2.500 mM·min⁻¹; (3) a chemisorption equilibrium constant K_A = 0.0400 mM⁻¹ (40.0 L·mol⁻¹) corresponding to ΔG°_ads = −20.55 kJ·mol⁻¹; and (4) complete absence of mass-transfer artifacts (Mears criterion < 0.03 at 900 RPM). These results rigorously validate the Langmuir active-site saturation paradigm and provide an authoritative pedagogical model for solid-state heterogeneous reaction engineering.
