Civil engineering mechanics & structural foundations of Aggregate Impact & Crushing.

In highway pavement design, bridge deck overlays, and railway ballast engineering, mineral aggregates must resist dynamic shock impacts from high-speed vehicular traffic and wheel flat spots without shattering into structural powder. The Aggregate Impact Value (AIV) test (BS 812-112 / IS 2386-4) directly quantifies aggregate dynamic toughness. A standardized cylindrical steel measure (102 mm internal diameter, 50 mm depth) is filled with oven-dried aggregate passing the 12.5 mm sieve and retained on the 10.0 mm sieve, consolidated in three layers with 25 tamping rod strokes. The sample is transferred into a heavy cast-iron cup secured to a rigid anvil resting on a concrete floor. A 13.5 to 14.0 kg sliding steel hammer drops freely from a height of 380 ± 5 mm down vertical guide rails onto the aggregate bed. Under standard testing, exactly 15 blows delivered at an interval of not less than one second subject the aggregate particles to intense compressive shock waves. The crushed specimen is removed and sieved over a 2.36 mm (BS No. 8) sieve: AIV (%) = (M_fines / M_initial) · 100. Pavement specifications classify aggregates based on AIV: < 10% exceptionally tough, 10% to 20% strong for high-speed wearing courses, 20% to 30% acceptable for road base courses, and > 35% weak and unfit for pavement construction. In this laboratory investigation, fines generation is evaluated across 5 to 20 impact blows to calibrate dynamic crushing sensitivity slope = 1.6500 %/blow (yielding standard 15-blow AIV = 24.75%).

Dynamic Fracture Mechanics of Aggregate Under Impact

When the $14.0\text{ kg}$ steel hammer impacts the aggregate bed from $H = 0.380\text{ m}$, kinetic impact energy delivered per blow is:

\[ E_{blow} = m g H = 14.0 \times 9.81 \times 0.380 = 52.19\text{ J} \]

Across $N$ blows, total energy input is $E_{total} = N \cdot E_{blow}$. This energy induces compressive contact stresses that exceed the unconfined compressive strength of mineral crystals, propagating Griffith micro-cracks along grain boundaries and cleavage planes. According to Rittinger's law of comminution, surface area created (and hence fine mass passing the $2.36\text{ mm}$ sieve) is directly proportional to mechanical energy expended:

\[ \text{Fines (\%)} = k_{impact} \cdot N \]

For standard testing at $N = 15\text{ blows}$, the standard Aggregate Impact Value is:

\[ \text{AIV (\%)} = k_{impact} \times 15 \]

Aggregate Crushing Value (ACV) and 10% Fines Value (TFV)

Under BS 812-111, the Ten Percent Fines Value (TFV) applies a progressive static compressive load in a UTM to generate exactly $10\%$ fines passing $2.36\text{ mm}$. The required load $F_{10}$ is: $F_{10} = \frac{14 \cdot F}{y + 4}$, where $F$ is load (kN) producing $y\%$ fines. Higher TFV values ($> 150\text{ kN}$) indicate superior crushing resistance.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How does the Aggregate Impact Value (AIV) differ physically from the Aggregate Crushing Value (ACV)?

AIV applies rapid dynamic shock impacts (14 kg hammer dropping 380 mm in fractions of a second), evaluating toughness against brittle impact shatter; ACV applies slow, progressive static compression (400 kN over 10 minutes in a UTM), evaluating compressive crushing strength under heavy static wheel loads.

Why is the aggregate fraction strictly limited to passing 12.5 mm and retained on 10.0 mm?

Standardizing particle size eliminates geometric size effects; larger stones have higher probabilities of internal micro-flaws, while smaller stones pack differently, altering void ratios and energy distribution.

What is the consequence of failing to clamp the impact cup rigidly to the cast-iron anvil?

If the cup bounces or shifts, kinetic energy from the falling hammer is dissipated into acoustic vibrations and machine movement rather than fracturing the aggregate bed, producing an artificially low, invalid AIV.

What are typical highway specification thresholds for Aggregate Impact Value?

British Standards (BS 812) and IRC specifications mandate AIV ≤ 30% for high-speed wearing surfaces (surface dressing, asphalt concrete), ≤ 35% for binder courses, and ≤ 40% to 45% for road base courses.

Why must the test sample be thoroughly oven-dried before testing?

Moisture creates capillary water films that lubricate particle contacts and cushion hammer impact, while trapped water can transmit pore water pressures, distorting true aggregate shatter resistance.

How does aggregate mineralogy influence impact toughness?

Fine-grained igneous rocks (basalt, andesite) with interlocking microcrystalline textures exhibit high toughness (AIV < 15%); coarse plutonic granites with cleavable feldspars and quartz grains show moderate toughness (AIV ≈ 20% to 25%); porous sandstones and shales shatter easily (AIV > 35%).

Experimental precautions and laboratory safety

  • Verify that the impact hammer free fall height is calibrated to exactly 380 ± 5 mm above the struck aggregate surface.
  • Ensure the aggregate impact machine rests on a rigid horizontal concrete floor of at least 300 mm thickness to prevent anvil energy dissipation.
  • Tamp each of the three consolidation layers with exactly 25 uniform strokes of the rounded-end steel tamping rod.
  • Keep hands completely clear of the hammer drop zone and guide rods during hammer release.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The civil engineering physical models apply within the specified calibrated operational window (5.0 to 20.0 blows).
  • Assumes homogeneous, isotropic continuum material behavior without unmodeled micro-fracture anomalies.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • Extreme inelastic yielding or cyclic degradation requires non-linear constitutive continuum damage mechanics.
