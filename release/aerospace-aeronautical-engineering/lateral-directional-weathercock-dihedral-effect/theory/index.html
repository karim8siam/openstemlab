Aerodynamic and physical foundations of Directional & Lateral Stability.

Lateral-directional static stability governs how an aircraft responds to lateral aerodynamic disturbances and asymmetric sideslip angles beta. Directional (weathercock) stability ensures that when an aircraft experiences an uncommanded sideslip (e.g. from a lateral gust), the vertical stabilizer generates a restoring yawing moment to align the aircraft nose back into the relative wind. The mathematical requirement for directional static stability is C_n_beta = dC_n / dbeta > 0: positive sideslip (wind from the right, beta > 0) must produce a positive (nose-right) restoring yawing moment. Concurrently, lateral stability requires that a sideslip disturbance generates a restoring roll away from the slip—termed the dihedral effect. The requirement for lateral static stability is C_l_beta = dC_l / dbeta < 0: positive sideslip (wind from the right) must produce a negative (roll-left) moment. Wing geometric dihedral (upward angle of the wings Gamma), wing sweepback, and high-wing fuselage placement all provide powerful stabilizing contributions to C_l_beta. In this laboratory experiment, a complete 6-component wind tunnel model is tested in a yaw turntable across sideslip angles from beta = 1.0 to 10.0 degrees. Measuring the yawing moment coefficient C_n validates the linear weathercock stability derivative C_n_beta.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is weathercock stability and why must the derivative C_n_beta be positive?

Weathercock stability is the natural tendency of an aircraft to yaw into the oncoming relative wind. When an aircraft encounters a positive sideslip angle beta (wind from the right), the vertical tail experiences an angle of attack and generates a side force to the left. Acting behind the center of gravity, this creates a positive (nose-right) yawing moment, rotating the nose into the wind. Hence C_n_beta > 0 is strictly required.

What is the dihedral effect and why must C_l_beta be negative for lateral stability?

The dihedral effect causes an aircraft to roll away from an incoming sideslip. When slipping to the right (beta > 0), the right wing encounters the airflow at a higher effective angle of attack than the left wing due to upward geometric wing dihedral Gamma. The right wing generates more lift, producing a negative (roll-left) rolling moment: C_l_beta < 0.

How does wing sweepback contribute to lateral dihedral stability?

When a swept-wing aircraft sideslips, the windward wing becomes less swept relative to oncoming flow, increasing its effective span and normal velocity V * cos(Lambda - beta). The leeward wing becomes more swept, reducing its normal velocity. The windward wing produces significantly more lift, creating a strong stabilizing roll away from the slip.

Why does a high-wing aircraft configuration possess natural dihedral stability?

On a high-wing aircraft, the fuselage acts as an obstacle to lateral cross-flow during sideslip. Airflow diverted beneath the fuselage causes an upward flow component (upwash) on the windward wing and downwash on the leeward wing. This fuselage-wing aerodynamic interference provides an effective dihedral of 2 to 3 degrees without physical geometric dihedral.

What is vertical fin stall and why is it dangerous?

If an aircraft encounters an extreme sideslip angle (beta > 15-20 deg), the vertical fin can exceed its stall angle. The vertical tail stalls, causing an abrupt collapse of restoring yawing moment and a sudden loss of directional stability, which can trigger an unrecoverable spin.

How do directional stability C_n_beta and dihedral effect C_l_beta interact in dynamic Dutch roll oscillations?

The ratio of dihedral effect to directional stability (C_l_beta / C_n_beta) dictates the character of the lateral-directional Dutch roll mode. Excessive dihedral effect relative to weathercock stability causes strong roll oscillations coupled with yaw, leading to poor Dutch roll damping and pilot-induced oscillation (PIO).

Experimental precautions and wind tunnel safety

  • Ensure the yaw turntable rotates smoothly without introducing structural bending moments into the sting mount.
  • Verify that model fuselage boundary layers do not detach prematurely along sharp chine lines at high sideslip angles.
  • Account for wind tunnel sidewall proximity when testing wide-span models at large sideslip angles beta > 8 degrees.
  • Check that rolling moment and yawing moment channels are cross-calibrated to eliminate Wheatstone bridge cross-talk.

Theoretical assumptions & flow limits

  • Linear derivative C_n_beta is valid for attached flow on the vertical fin (beta < 12 deg); high sideslip causes vertical fin stall.
  • Fuselage body vortex shedding at high sideslip induces non-linear cross-flow forces.
  • Assumes zero rudder deflection (rudder fixed at delta_r = 0); directional trim requires rudder deflection.
  • Unpowered model does not account for propeller slipstream side forces (P-factor and slipstream swirl).
