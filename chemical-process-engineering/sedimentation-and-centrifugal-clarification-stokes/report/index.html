4. Theory and graph choice

Sedimentation and gravity clarification are foundational mechanical separation unit operations utilized extensively in chemical manufacturing, mineral beneficiation, hydrometallurgy, water clarification, and municipal wastewater treatment. In dilute suspensions (solids volume fraction φ < 0.005), individual spherical particles settle independently without hydrodynamic particle-particle interaction according to Stokes' law: v_0 = g · d_p^2 · (ρ_p - ρ_f) / (18 · μ). However, in concentrated industrial slurries (φ > 0.05), particles settle as a collective cloud or blanket with a sharp, visible interface between the descending clarified supernatant and the concentrated suspension—a phenomenon known as hindered settling or zone settling. According to the classical sedimentation theory formulated by George J. Kynch in 1952, the local settling velocity v_s of particles in a suspension depends solely on the local solids volume concentration: v_s = f(C_s). As solids concentration increases, the upward displacement velocity of displaced fluid through the narrowing interstitial pores increases, and viscous drag is amplified by surrounding particles, causing settling velocity to decrease sharply. In the moderate concentration regime (C_s = 10.0 to 60.0 g/L), the Kynch hindered settling velocity decreases linearly with solids concentration: v_s = v_0 - S · C_s, where v_0 = 120.0000 cm/min is the unhindered terminal velocity intercept and S = 1.8500 (cm/min)/(g/L) is the Kynch hindered settling slope. Alternatively, the non-linear relationship across wide concentration ranges is described by the Richardson–Zaki correlation: v_s = v_0 · ε^n = v_0 · (1 - φ)^n, where ε is suspension voidage and n is the Richardson–Zaki exponent (~4.65 in laminar creeping flow). In continuous industrial gravity thickeners, the solid flux method (Coe & Clevenger / Yoshioka analysis) utilizes these batch settling velocity curves to identify the critical solids handling capacity (minimum flux G_min) and determine the required cross-sectional clarifier area A = F_s / G_min. In this laboratory experiment, a 1.0-meter precision glass settling column equipped with automated optical array interface detection tracks suspension interface subsidence across CaCO_3 concentrations from 10.0 to 60.0 g/L to evaluate v_s, determine S = 1.8500 (cm/min)/(g/L), and calculate continuous thickener dimensions.

v_s = -S · C_s + v_0 = -1.8500 · C_s + 120.0000

The governing phenomenological transport or equilibrium relationship was linearized into the coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Slurry solids concentration C_s (g/L), and the dependent coordinate y represents Hindered settling velocity v_s (cm/min). The gradient m directly determines Kynch Hindered Settling Velocity Slope S, while the intercept b accounts for baseline tare values or reference potentials. Linear transformation enables robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear numerical divergence. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays process discharge coefficients, heat transfer multipliers, mass transfer rates, or kinetic rate constants directly as linear slopes.

5. Method

  1. Weigh pure precipitated CaCO_3 (d_p = 25.0 μm, ρ_p = 2710 kg/m^3); disperse in DI water in homogenization tank at 1200 RPM for 15 minutes.
  2. Charge the 1.0 m settling column with 10.0 g/L slurry; invert column 5 times to establish uniform initial suspension throughout height z_0 = 100.0 cm.
  3. Place column in vertical stand; initiate optical interface logging; record interface height z(t) versus elapsed time t at 5 s intervals.
  4. Extract constant settling velocity v_s from the initial linear slope of the subsidence curve: v_s = -dz/dt.
  5. Repeat homogenization, charging, and subsidence logging across C_s = 20.0, 30.0, 40.0, 50.0, and 60.0 g/L, recording v_s for each batch.
  6. Plot v_s versus solids concentration C_s; fit linear regression to evaluate slope S and v_0; calculate Coe–Clevenger thickener area.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Ensure the settling column is aligned strictly vertical with a precision spirit level, as a tilt of only 1° causes significant wall-effect channeling (Boycott effect).
  • Maintain slurry water temperature strictly at 20.0 ± 0.2 °C, as water viscosity changes by ~2.5% per °C, directly distorting Stokes settling rates.
  • Mix the slurry thoroughly before each run to ensure 100% uniform concentration throughout the entire column height before timing begins.
  • Never introduce air bubbles during column filling, as rising bubbles disrupt the downward zone settling interface.

In-Depth Technical Supplement & Historical Unit Operations Derivation

1. Objective & Practical Significance

This laboratory investigation determines batch hindered sedimentation kinetics, mudline interface subsidence rates, and continuous thickener sizing parameters for concentrated calcium carbonate ($CaCO_3$, $d_p = 25.0\ \mu\text{m}$, $\rho_p = 2710\text{ kg/m}^3$) slurries across initial solids concentrations $C_s = 10.0$ to $60.0\text{ g/L}$ in a $1.0\text{-meter}$ graduated glass settling column. By evaluating linear settling subsidence rates, we verify the Kynch hindered settling model $v_s = -S \cdot C_s + v_0$, determine slope $S = 1.8500\text{ (cm/min)/(g/L)}$ and free settling velocity $v_0 = 120.0000\text{ cm/min}$, construct the solids flux curve, and size an industrial gravity thickener.

2. Governing Transport Equations & Phenomenological Relations

Stokes' terminal settling velocity is: $v_0 = \frac{g d_p^2 (\rho_p - \rho_f)}{18 \mu}$. The Kynch linear hindered settling velocity model is:

\[ v_s = -S \cdot C_s + v_0 = -1.8500 \cdot C_s + 120.0000 \]

where $S = 1.8500\text{ (cm/min)/(g/L)}$ and $v_0 = 120.0000\text{ cm/min}$. The solids handling flux is $G_s = C_s \cdot v_s$, and thickener area is $A = F_s / G_{\text{min}}$.

3. Experimental Apparatus & Instrumentation Specification

The system comprises a $100.0\text{ cm}$ tall, $6.0\text{ cm}$ ID jacketed glass settling column ($V = 2.827\text{ L}$), an automated optical photodiode array scanning interface height $z(t)$ at $2\text{ Hz}$ ($\pm 0.5\text{ mm}$), a slow-speed wire rake ($1.0\text{ RPM}$), a $10\text{ L}$ high-shear homogenization tank ($1200\text{ RPM}$), a thermostatic water jacket at $20.00 \pm 0.05^\circ\text{C}$, and an analytical balance ($\pm 0.0001\text{ g}$).

4. Standard Operating Procedure & Measurement Protocols

Standardized precipitated $CaCO_3$ slurries were prepared gravimetrically for $C_s = 10.0, 20.0, 30.0, 40.0, 50.0,$ and $60.0\text{ g/L}$ in deionized water. The column was filled to initial height $z_0 = 100.0\text{ cm}$ and inverted repeatedly to achieve uniform suspension. The optical array logged interface subsidence height $z(t)$ versus time. Initial constant settling velocities $v_s = -dz/dt$ were calculated from the linear descent period for each concentration.

11. Self-Assessment Conceptual Questions & Model Answers

Q1: Why does a tilted settling column settle faster than a perfectly vertical column (Boycott effect)?
A1: Tilting the column creates an inclined upper wall where clarified liquid quickly disengages and flows upward in a thin, low-resistance boundary layer, while solids slide down the opposite lower wall, dramatically reducing upward liquid drag through the bulk suspension.

Q2: How does the critical settling point (compression point) differ from the hindered settling velocity?
A2: The hindered settling period occurs while particles settle through fluid at constant velocity; the compression point marks the moment when settling particles physically contact each other, transitioning from fluid-drag settling to mechanical compaction where solids support weight.
