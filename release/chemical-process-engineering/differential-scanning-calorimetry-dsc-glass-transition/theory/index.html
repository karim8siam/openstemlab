Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of DSC Polymer Glass Transition.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) is the primary thermal analysis technique employed in chemical, materials, and process engineering to characterize thermodynamic phase transitions, crystallization kinetics, chemical crosslinking, and polymeric glass transitions. When an amorphous polymer is heated, it undergoes a reversible second-order-like kinetic transition from a rigid, glassy state to a compliant, rubbery state known as the glass transition (T_g). At the microscopic level, this transition corresponds to the onset of cooperative segmental mobility of 20 to 50 repeating polymer backbone units. Unlike first-order phase transitions (such as melting or vaporization) which exhibit latent heat and discrete discontinuities in enthalpy and volume, the glass transition exhibits an endothermic step-change in heat capacity: ΔC_p = C_p(rubbery) - C_p(glassy). Because the glass transition is an inherently kinetic phenomenon governed by structural relaxation times, the observed transition temperature T_g shifts systematically toward higher temperatures as the instrumental heating rate β (dT/dt) increases. According to Moynihan, Richardson, and Flynn–Wall–Ozawa kinetic relaxation theories, the scan-rate dependence of the glass transition temperature obeys the logarithmic relation: T_g = T_g0 + S · ln(β), where the thermal shift slope S = R · T_g^2 / ΔH* is inversely proportional to the apparent activation energy for segmental relaxation ΔH*. In this laboratory exercise, high-purity amorphous poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) specimens (10.0 mg) sealed in hermetic aluminum pans are subjected to controlled heating scans from 5.0 to 30.0 °C/min under an inert nitrogen purge to determine the step change in heat capacity ΔC_p, extract the baseline glass transition temperature T_g0 = 100.00 °C, and evaluate the kinetic shift slope S = 2.1500 °C.

T_g = S · ln(β) + T_g0 = 2.1500 · ln(β) + 100.0000

Polymer Viscoelasticity & Molecular Origin of the Glass Transition

In amorphous and semi-crystalline polymers, the glass transition temperature ($T_g$) marks the fundamental kinetic boundary between brittle glassy behavior and ductile rubbery behavior. In the glassy state ($T < T_g$), polymer macromolecules are kinetically trapped in non-equilibrium disordered conformations. Chain segments cannot translate; thermal energy is stored exclusively in localized bond stretching, valence angle bending, and restricted side-group librations. As temperature rises to $T_g$, the thermal energy $k_B T$ becomes comparable to the rotational energy barriers governing main-chain torsional angles. Cooperative coordinated rotations of 20 to 50 skeletal backbone bonds (crankshaft motions) become activated, creating macroscopic flexibility, structural relaxation, and a sudden step increase in heat capacity $\Delta C_p$:

\[ \Delta C_p = C_{p,\text{rubbery}} - C_{p,\text{glassy}} = \left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial T} \right)_{P,\text{rubbery}} - \left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial T} \right)_{P,\text{glassy}} \]

Differential Scanning Calorimetry Heat Flow Principle

In a heat-flux DSC cell, the specimen (mass $m_s$) and an empty reference crucible are mounted on identical thermoelectric sensor platforms surrounded by a heated furnace. The differential heat flow rate $d Q / d t$ is measured via the differential thermoelectric voltage:

\[ \frac{d Q}{d t} = -\frac{\Delta T_{\text{sensor}}}{R_{\text{sensor}}} = m_s \, c_p \, \frac{d T}{d t} + f(T, t) \]

where $\beta = d T / d t$ is the programmed constant heating scan rate. Under steady heating, baseline heat flow is directly proportional to sample heat capacity: $q = m_s \, c_p \, \beta$. At the glass transition, heat flow shifts downward by $\Delta q = m_s \, \Delta c_p \, \beta$.

Kinetic Shift & Moynihan Activation Formalism

Because structural relaxation is governed by a characteristic relaxation time $\tau(T)$ that follows the Vogel–Fulcher–Tammann (VFT) or Williams–Landel–Ferry (WLF) equation, shifting the instrumental heating rate $\beta$ alters the observational Deborah number ($De = \tau / t_{\text{obs}}$). Faster scan rates force the transition to occur at shorter observation timescales, requiring higher thermal energy and elevating $T_g$. According to Moynihan and Richardson thermal activation theory:

\[ \frac{d \ln \beta}{d (1 / T_g)} = -\frac{\Delta H^*}{R} \]

For small temperature intervals $\Delta T_g \ll T_g$, expanding in a first-order Taylor series around baseline heating rate produces the linear logarithmic scaling relation:

\[ T_g = T_{g0} + S \cdot \ln(\beta) \]

where $S = \frac{R \, T_g^2}{\Delta H^*} = 2.1500^\circ\text{C}$ is the glass transition thermal shift slope and $T_{g0} = 100.00^\circ\text{C}$ is the extrapolated datum at $\beta = 1.0^\circ\text{C/min}$.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

Why is the glass transition classified as a pseudo-second-order thermodynamic transition?

In Ehrenfest thermodynamics, first-order transitions exhibit discontinuities in first derivatives of Gibbs energy (entropy and volume), while second-order transitions exhibit discontinuities in second derivatives (heat capacity, thermal expansion, compressibility); because T_g exhibits a finite step in C_p without latent heat, it resembles a second-order transition, but its kinetic dependence on heating rate prevents it from being a true equilibrium transition.

How does free volume theory explain the glass transition phenomenon?

Free volume theory (Fox–Flory) posits that total volume consists of occupied molecular volume and unoccupied free volume; as a polymer cools, free volume contracts until reaching a critical fraction (approximately 2.5% of total volume at T_g), below which large-scale segmental backbone motion is frozen out.

Why must the thermal history of a polymer sample be erased before measuring T_g?

Physical aging below T_g allows polymer chains to relax slowly into lower-enthalpy conformations, producing an endothermic enthalpy recovery peak superimposed on the glass transition step; heating above T_g followed by controlled cooling erases this thermal history, restoring an unaged baseline.

How does polymer molecular weight affect glass transition temperature?

The Fox–Flory equation T_g = T_g(inf) - K / M_n shows that lower molecular weights possess higher concentrations of chain ends, which have greater free volume and mobility, thereby depressing T_g; above a threshold molecular weight (typically ~50,000 g/mol for PMMA), T_g plateaus at T_g(inf).

What is the difference between midpoint T_g, onset T_g, and inflection T_g?

Onset T_g is the intersection of the pre-transition baseline with the tangent at maximum slope; inflection T_g is the peak of the derivative heat flow curve; midpoint T_g is the temperature at which the heat flow reaches half the total heat capacity step ΔC_p, providing the most reproducible metric.

How does plasticizer addition alter polymer glass transition temperature?

Plasticizers are low-molecular-weight compatible diluents that embed between polymer chains, shielding intermolecular polar forces and introducing substantial free volume, which dramatically lowers T_g (described by the Gordon–Taylor equation) to improve flexibility.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Ensure the aluminum sample pan is crimped hermetically without pinholes to prevent volatilization of residual monomer or moisture during heating.
  • Verify steady nitrogen purge gas flow at 50 mL/min to prevent atmospheric oxygen oxidation of PMMA at elevated temperatures.
  • Always include an identical empty aluminum pan in the reference cell position to balance instrument baseline heat capacity.
  • Perform a thermal history erasure cycle above 150 °C prior to data collection to eliminate physical aging enthalpy relaxation peaks.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (5.0 to 30.0 °C/min).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
