Probabilistic foundations of Metropolis-Hastings MCMC.

The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm is the bedrock of modern Bayesian computation and computational statistical physics. By constructing an ergodic Markov chain whose stationary equilibrium matches the unnormalized posterior density, it circumvents the curse of dimensionality and the intractability of high-dimensional normalizing integrals. In this laboratory, we implement random walk Metropolis sampling, analyze acceptance probability dynamics, track burn-in trajectories, compute autocorrelation times, and confirm the universal Monte Carlo error attenuation rate α = -0.500. In mathematical statistics, empirical data science, and modern computational engineering, understanding the asymptotic sampling properties and theoretical behavior of Metropolis-Hastings Algorithm, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), and Acceptance Dynamics provides crucial guarantees. Rigorous experimental validation connects foundational probability laws with observable empirical phenomena, ensuring that statistical inference remains valid under finite sample sizes and real-world data collection constraints.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), and why did it revolutionize Bayesian statistics?

Prior to MCMC, Bayesian statistics was largely restricted to simple models with analytical conjugate priors. In realistic multi-parameter models, computing posterior expectations E[g(θ) | y] = ∫ g(θ) p(θ | y) dθ requires integrating over high-dimensional spaces where the normalizing constant p(y) = ∫ L(θ) p(θ) dθ is intractable. MCMC constructs an ergodic Markov chain whose unique stationary distribution is exactly the target posterior distribution p(θ | y). By simulating the chain forward in time, sample states asymptotically represent draws from the posterior, bypassing the need to compute normalizing constants.

How is the general Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability formulated?

Introduced by Nicholas Metropolis et al. in 1953 and generalized by W. K. Hastings in 1970, the algorithm generates states as follows: at current state x, a candidate y is proposed from density q(y | x). The candidate is accepted with probability: α(x, y) = min(1, [p(y) q(x | y)] / [p(x) q(y | x)]). If accepted, X_{t+1} = y; if rejected, the chain remains at its current state X_{t+1} = x. Crucially, the target density p(x) appears only as a ratio p(y)/p(x), meaning any unknown normalizing constant cancels out completely.

How does the Metropolis-Hastings rule satisfy Detailed Balance?

Detailed balance requires p(x) P(x, y) = p(y) P(y, x) for all x ≠ y. The transition probability for moving from x to y is P(x, y) = q(y | x) α(x, y). Without loss of generality, assume p(y) q(x | y) ≤ p(x) q(y | x). Then α(x, y) = [p(y) q(x | y)] / [p(x) q(y | x)] and α(y, x) = 1. Substituting into the flux equations: p(x) P(x, y) = p(x) q(y | x) [p(y) q(x | y)] / [p(x) q(y | x)] = p(y) q(x | y) = p(y) q(x | y) · 1 = p(y) P(y, x). Because detailed balance holds, p(x) is guaranteed to be a stationary distribution of the chain.

What is the optimal acceptance rate for random walk Metropolis algorithms?

Gelman, Roberts, and Gilks (1997) proved celebrated asymptotic optimality results for random walk Metropolis on target densities in d dimensions: (1) In 1 dimension (d = 1), the optimal acceptance rate that minimizes autocorrelation is approximately 44% (0.44); (2) As dimension d → ∞, the optimal acceptance rate asymptotically converges to 23.8% (0.234). If acceptance is too high (>70%), the proposal step size is too small, resulting in slow diffusive exploration; if acceptance is too low (<10%), proposals are constantly rejected, causing the chain to freeze.

What is Effective Sample Size (ESS) and Integrated Autocorrelation Time?

Because MCMC states are correlated across successive iterations, N dependent MCMC samples contain less statistical information than N independent draws. The variance of the sample mean is Var(θ̄) = (σ^2 / N) · [1 + 2 ∑_{k=1}^∞ ρ_k] = (σ^2 / N) · τ_int, where ρ_k is the lag-k autocorrelation and τ_int = 1 + 2 ∑ ρ_k is the integrated autocorrelation time. The Effective Sample Size is defined as ESS = N / τ_int. An MCMC chain of 10,000 steps with τ_int = 10 provides only ESS = 1,000 independent-equivalent draws.

What is the burn-in period, and how do Gelman-Rubin diagnostics assess convergence?

The initial trajectory of an MCMC chain depends heavily on the starting state X_0. The burn-in period is the initial sequence of iterations discarded to allow the chain to reach its stationary distribution. Andrew Gelman and Donald Rubin (1992) introduced the potential scale reduction factor R̂ (Gelman-Rubin diagnostic) using multiple chains initialized from dispersed starting values. R̂ compares the between-chain variance B to the within-chain variance W: R̂ = √[(W (n - 1)/n + B/n) / W]. An R̂ < 1.05 across all parameters indicates that chains have mixed and converged to stationarity.

How does Gibbs Sampling specialize the Metropolis-Hastings framework?

Introduced by Stuart and Donald Geman in 1984, Gibbs Sampling is a special case of Metropolis-Hastings where parameters are updated one at a time by sampling directly from full conditional posterior distributions: θ_j^(t+1) ~ p(θ_j | θ_{-j}^(t), y). Because proposing from the exact full conditional yields an acceptance ratio α = min(1, 1) = 1, Gibbs sampling has an acceptance probability of identically 100%, avoiding tuning proposal step sizes.

Experimental precautions and statistical checks

  • Always discard an initial burn-in period of iterations to eliminate transient dependency on starting coordinates.
  • Tune proposal standard deviation σ_prop to achieve an acceptance rate near the optimal 23-44% target range.
  • Run multiple independent chains from over-dispersed starting points and verify that Gelman-Rubin R̂ < 1.05.
  • Compute Effective Sample Size (ESS) using autocorrelation functions before quoting posterior standard deviations.

Theoretical assumptions & limitations

  • Random walk Metropolis can become trapped in local modes when sampling from multi-modal distributions with deep valleys.
  • High autocorrelation between successive states dramatically inflates the number of iterations required to achieve reliable precision.
