Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Radial Thermal Conductivity.

In chemical process plants, cryogenic storage terminals, and steam distribution systems, thermal insulation materials—such as calcium silicate, mineral wool, expanded perlite, and cellular glass—are mandatory to minimize parasitic thermal losses, prevent personnel burn injuries, avoid pipeline freezing, and suppress condensation. Determining the thermal conductivity k of tubular process insulation is vital for accurate thermal design. In cylindrical geometries, heat flows radially outward from an internal coaxial heating element through an annular insulation sleeve of inner radius r_1, outer radius r_2, and length L. Applying Fourier's fundamental law of heat conduction in one-dimensional steady cylindrical coordinates yields: q_r = -k A(r) · (dT/dr) = -k (2π r L) · (dT/dr). Separating variables and integrating across the annular wall establishes the classic logarithmic temperature profile: T(r) = T_1 - (T_1 - T_2) · [ln(r/r_1) / ln(r_2/r_1)], and gives the steady radial heat flow rate: q_r = [2π k L / ln(r_2/r_1)] · (T_1 - T_2). In this laboratory experiment, an annular specimen of refractory process insulation is heated with an internal coaxial electrical element across power levels from 20 to 120 W with guarded end heaters to eliminate axial heat leakage. Measuring the radial temperature difference ΔT = T_1 - T_2 directly evaluates the thermal resistance slope S and determines the material thermal conductivity k.

ΔT = S · Q_e = 0.3850 · Q_e

1. Fourier's Law in Cylindrical Coordinates

Thermal insulation in chemical plants protects piping, reactors, and distillation columns from energy loss. Analysis of thermal conduction through cylindrical pipe walls requires Fourier's law expressed in cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$. Assuming steady state, one-dimensional radial heat flow, and constant thermal conductivity $k$:

\[ q_r = -k A(r) \frac{dT}{dr} = -k (2\pi r L) \frac{dT}{dr} \]

where $A(r) = 2\pi r L$ is the isothermal cylindrical surface area at radius $r$.

2. Integration of Radial Conduction Equation

Separating variables and integrating between inner boundary ($r = r_1, T = T_1$) and outer boundary ($r = r_2, T = T_2$):

\[ \frac{q_r}{2\pi k L} \int_{r_1}^{r_2} \frac{dr}{r} = -\int_{T_1}^{T_2} dT \implies \frac{q_r}{2\pi k L} \ln\left( \frac{r_2}{r_1} \right) = T_1 - T_2 = \Delta T \]

Solving for temperature difference $\Delta T$ in terms of radial heat flow rate $q_r$:

\[ \Delta T = \left[ \frac{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}{2\pi k L} \right] q_r = R_{cyl} \cdot q_r \]

where $R_{cyl} = \frac{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}{2\pi k L}$ is the conductive thermal resistance of the cylindrical sleeve in $\text{K/W}$.

3. Measurement Model & Thermal Conductivity Evaluation

In an electrically heated apparatus with active guard heaters, axial losses are suppressed, meaning electrical power input $Q_e = V I$ equals radial conduction heat rate ($q_r = Q_e$). The linear model relating temperature difference $\Delta T$ to power $Q_e$ is:

\[ \Delta T = S \cdot Q_e \]

where $S$ is the experimental thermal resistance slope. From the known sleeve geometry ($r_1, r_2, L$), the thermal conductivity $k$ is directly evaluated as:

\[ k = \frac{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}{2\pi L \cdot S} \]

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

Why does the temperature profile in a cylinder vary logarithmically rather than linearly as in a flat slab?

In cylindrical coordinates, the heat conduction area A(r) = 2π r L expands continuously with increasing radius; to maintain constant total heat flux q_r, the temperature gradient dT/dr must decrease inversely with radius (dT/dr ∝ 1/r), integrating to a logarithmic curve.

What is the critical radius of insulation r_cr and why is it vital for pipe insulation design?

The critical radius r_cr = k / h is the outer insulation radius at which total thermal resistance is minimized; adding insulation to a pipe with radius r_o < r_cr actually increases heat loss because surface area expansion outweighs conductive insulation resistance.

How do active guard heaters prevent experimental measurement error in radial conduction apparatus?

Guard heaters at cylinder ends are maintained at identical temperatures to the central core, driving axial temperature gradients to zero (dT/dz = 0) and ensuring 100% of electrical power dissipates purely radially.

How does moisture ingress degrade the performance of industrial thermal insulation?

Water has a thermal conductivity (k ≈ 0.6 W/(m·K)) more than an order of magnitude higher than dry air (k ≈ 0.026 W/(m·K)) trapped in insulation pores; moisture filling pores drastically increases effective conductivity and accelerates corrosion under insulation (CUI).

What mechanisms of heat transfer operate inside porous insulation materials?

Apparent thermal conductivity combines solid matrix conduction, pore gas conduction, natural convection within micro-pores (negligible for pore diameter < 1 mm), and radiative radiation between pore walls.

What is the economic thickness of insulation (ETI) in process engineering?

ETI is the insulation thickness that minimizes total annualized cost, balancing the annualized capital cost of insulation against the monetary value of saved thermal energy over the facility lifetime.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Allow sufficient equilibration time (30 to 45 minutes) between power changes, as insulating materials have very low thermal diffusivity.
  • Verify that active guard heaters match the core temperature to within 0.1 °C to eliminate axial heat loss.
  • Ensure outer water jacket temperature remains stable to establish a constant outer boundary condition.
  • Do not exceed maximum allowable core temperature rating of the insulation material (typically 300 °C).

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (20.0 to 120.0 W).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
