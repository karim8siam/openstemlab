Agricultural and physical foundations of Moldboard Plow Specific Draft.

Primary tillage via moldboard plowing represents one of the most energy-intensive operations in agricultural crop production. The moldboard plow functions by cutting a rectangular prism of soil (furrow slice) with the horizontal share and vertical coulter, lifting it along the curved shin and breast, and inverting it across the moldboard wing to bury crop residues and aerate the root zone. The total horizontal draft resistance D is governed by soil mechanical properties (cohesion, internal friction, soil-metal adhesion), cutting geometry, and operating speed. In classical tillage mechanics (ASABE D497 and Gorjatschkin's rational equation), draft is partitioned into three terms: static cutting and friction resistance proportional to furrow cross-sectional area (A = w·d), soil acceleration and throwing resistance proportional to velocity squared, and parasitic landside friction. At steady field speed, specific soil resistance k_s = D / A (expressed in kPa or N/cm²) provides an objective benchmark of soil workability and structural state.

D = k_s · A = 48.5000 · A

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

State Gorjatschkin's rational formula for tillage tool draft.

Gorjatschkin's equation is D = f·G + k_s·a·b + ε·a·b·v², where f·G is parasitic sliding resistance of the plow body weight, k_s·a·b is static soil cutting and deforming resistance, and ε·a·b·v² represents the dynamic inertial force required to accelerate the furrow slice sideways and upward.

What is specific soil resistance and what factors determine its magnitude?

Specific soil resistance k_s = D / (w·d) is the draft force required per unit cross-sectional area of tilled soil. It depends on soil texture (clay vs sand), moisture content, bulk density, root proliferation, and moldboard surface scouring quality.

How does soil moisture content affect moldboard plow draft?

At very low moisture (dry soil), high cohesion and shear strength produce hard clods and extreme draft. As moisture increases to the friable range, draft drops to a minimum. Above the plastic limit, draft increases again due to sticky soil-metal adhesion and friction.

Why is proper plow horizontal and vertical suction critical?

Vertical suction (downward pitch of share point) pulls the plow into the ground to maintain working depth without ballast, while horizontal suction (inward angle) holds the landside against the furrow wall to maintain consistent cutting width.

What role does the rolling coulter play in reducing overall draft?

The circular coulter cuts clean vertical edges through surface trash and roots ahead of the shin, reducing tearing friction and preventing residue accumulation on the plow frog.

How does increasing plowing speed alter specific soil draft?

Increasing speed increases draft quadratically due to the kinetic energy imparted to throw and invert the soil slice (the ε·v² term in Gorjatschkin's equation), increasing fuel consumption per hectare.

Experimental precautions and agricultural field safety

  • Scour all moldboard surfaces thoroughly with abrasive pads before testing to eliminate rust and establish steady soil-metal friction.
  • Ensure tractor right wheels run inside the previous furrow wall at constant offset to prevent variation in first-bottom width.
  • Check that draft load cells are properly temperature-compensated to prevent zero drift under field solar radiation.
  • Disengage the differential lock only after entering the straight test run to maintain true axial alignment.

Theoretical assumptions & biological limits

  • Linear proportionality D = k_s·A holds strictly at constant operating speed; speed variations introduce quadratic dynamic errors.
  • Does not separate landside lateral friction from moldboard normal reaction forces.
  • Assumes uniform furrow depth across all three bottoms; slight linkage flexure can introduce small inter-bottom depth disparities.
  • Field stones or root obstacles produce transient draft spikes that must be filtered using median windowing.
