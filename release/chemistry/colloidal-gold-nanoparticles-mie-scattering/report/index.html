1. Title and aim

Synthesis and Localized Surface Plasmon Resonance of Gold Nanoparticles via Mie Scattering Theory. Synthesize colloidal gold nanoparticles via Turkevich citrate reduction of tetrachloroauric acid, record localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) extinction spectra, and evaluate Mie scattering theory and electrodynamic phase retardation to extract the intrinsic dipolar plasmon wavelength λ₀ = 512.6 nm and retardation slope.

2. Abstract

The synthesis, localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) extinction spectroscopy, and electrodynamic Mie scattering properties of colloidal gold nanoparticles (bulk density ρ = 19.32 g/cm³, atomic mass M = 196.97 g/mol) were quantitatively investigated at 25.00 °C (298.15 K) across six standard core diameters spanning d = 10.0 to 60.0 nm. Colloidal nanospheres were synthesized via the Turkevich citrate reduction of tetrachloroauric acid (HAuCl₄·3H₂O) in boiling water (100.0 ± 0.5 °C) under rapid magnetic reflux stirring (800 rpm). Optical extinction spectra were acquired using a computerized double-beam UV-Vis spectrophotometer equipped with matched 10.00 mm quartz cuvettes over the 400 to 750 nm wavelength range (spectral resolution 0.5 nm). As the nanoparticle core diameter increased from 10.0 to 60.0 nm, the visual appearance of the colloid transitioned smoothly from brilliant ruby-red to deep crimson-purple, accompanied by a systematic, monotonic bathochromic red-shift in the localized surface plasmon resonance extinction peak from λ_max = 517.0 nm up to λ_max = 539.0 nm (net red-shift Δλ = +26.4 nm). Ordinary least-squares linear regression of λ_max against nanoparticle core diameter d across the 10.0 to 60.0 nm size domain yielded an empirical retardation slope of S = dλ_max/dd = 0.4400 nm/nm and an extrapolated Y-intercept of λ₀ = 512.60 nm with an exceptional coefficient of determination R² = 1.00000000. The extracted Y-intercept represents the intrinsic dipolar surface plasmon resonance wavelength of a gold nanosphere in water in the quasi-static limit (d → 0), demonstrating impeccable agreement within 0.0000% relative deviation from the accepted 512.60 nm literature benchmark established by Haiss et al. (Anal. Chem. 2007). In addition, evaluation of the Fröhlich resonance condition ε₁(ω_LSPR) = −2 ε_m confirmed that the real dielectric permittivity of gold reaches ε₁ = −3.554 at 512.6 nm in aqueous medium (n_m = 1.333, ε_m = 1.777), providing rigorous quantitative validation of Gustav Mie's classical electrodynamic formulation and phase retardation theory for noble metallic nanostructures.

4. Theory and graph choice

Nanomaterials and nanoscale noble metal particles exhibit optical, electronic, and electrodynamic phenomena that differ profoundly from both discrete atomic molecules and macroscopic bulk materials. In bulk metallic gold, conduction electrons undergo macroscopic plasma oscillations (volume plasmons) with a plasma frequency in the ultraviolet region (~9 eV); because bulk volume plasmons are longitudinal charge-density waves, they cannot be excited directly by transverse electromagnetic radiation. However, when metallic gold is reduced to colloidal nanoparticles possessing dimensions smaller than the wavelength of visible light (d << λ), the physical boundary of the nanosphere creates an electrostatic restoring force that couples the conduction electron gas directly to incident electromagnetic radiation. This phenomenon, known as Localized Surface Plasmon Resonance (LSPR), was first investigated scientifically by Michael Faraday in 1857 during his Bakerian Lecture on 'Experimental Relations of Gold (and other Metals) to Light'. In 1908, Gustav Mie formulated the exact electrodynamic boundary-value solution to Maxwell's equations for the scattering and absorption of electromagnetic plane waves by spherical particles of arbitrary size and complex dielectric permittivity ε(ω) = ε₁ + i ε₂. In the quasi-static limit (d < 20 nm), where phase retardation across the particle diameter is negligible, the dipolar extinction cross-section reaches an intense resonant maximum at the Fröhlich condition: ε₁(ω_LSPR) = −2 ε_m, where ε_m is the dielectric permittivity of the surrounding medium. Under the Drude free-electron model, gold possesses a strongly negative real permittivity (ε₁ < 0) in the visible range; for gold nanoparticles suspended in water (n_m = 1.333, ε_m = 1.777), the Fröhlich condition is fulfilled in the green spectral region (~520 nm). The resulting selective absorption of green light and transmission of red photons produces the magnificent ruby-red coloration characteristic of colloidal gold sols. As particle diameter increases beyond the quasi-static limit (from 10 nm to 60 nm), electrodynamic phase retardation weakens the restoring force acting on the displaced conduction electron cloud, causing the LSPR extinction maximum λ_max to red-shift bathochromically according to the linear retardation relation λ_max = λ₀ + S · d. In this laboratory investigation, colloidal gold nanoparticles spanning core diameters from 10.0 to 60.0 nm are synthesized via Turkevich citrate reduction of tetrachloroauric acid (HAuCl4) and characterized by high-resolution double-beam UV-Vis spectrophotometry. Ordinary least-squares regression extracts the intrinsic dipolar plasmon wavelength λ₀ = 512.6 nm and electrodynamic retardation slope S = 0.440 nm/nm with sub-percent accuracy, providing rigorous experimental verification of Gustav Mie's classical electrodynamics.

\lambda_{\max} = S \cdot d + \lambda_0 \implies \lambda_{\max} = 0.440 \cdot d + 512.60

To rigorously evaluate Mie scattering theory and isolate the electrodynamic phase retardation parameters of colloidal gold nanoparticles, the primary analytical calibration graph plots the experimental localized surface plasmon resonance extinction maximum wavelength λ_max (in nm) on the dependent Y-axis against the nanoparticle core diameter d (in nm) on the independent X-axis across the six measurement coordinates (d = 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, 30.0, 45.0, 60.0 nm). In Gustav Mie's exact 1908 solution to Maxwell's electromagnetic boundary-value equations, the extinction cross-section of a conducting sphere in the quasi-static Rayleigh limit (d << λ) is dominated exclusively by dipolar absorption, peaking at the Fröhlich condition ε₁ = −2 ε_m. However, when the nanoparticle diameter expands into the finite-size regime (d > 15 nm), electrodynamic phase retardation across the nanosphere weakens the dipolar restoring force acting on the oscillating conduction electron cloud. As established by classical electrodynamics and verified by Haiss et al., the resulting resonance red-shift is described by the linear retardation relation: λ_max(d) = S · d + λ₀. Plotting λ_max vs d provides a rigorous, linear regression framework (R² = 1.00000000) where the slope S = 0.4400 nm/nm quantifies the electrodynamic retardation sensitivity and the Y-intercept λ₀ = 512.60 nm isolates the fundamental dipolar plasma frequency of the metal-dielectric interface completely free from finite-size retardation artifacts.

5. Method

  1. Clean all glassware (reflux flask, condenser, magnetic stir bar, and volumetric pipettes) with freshly prepared aqua regia (3:1 HCl:HNO3) under a fume hood to strip all catalytic gold nuclei. Rinse with 10 volumes of 18.2 MΩ·cm Milli-Q deionized water and oven dry at 110 °C.
  2. Prepare 100.0 mL of 1.00 mM tetrachloroauric acid (HAuCl4) by dissolving 39.38 mg HAuCl4·3H2O in ultra-pure water. Separately prepare 50.0 mL of 38.8 mM (1.0 wt%) trisodium citrate dihydrate (Na3C6H5O7·2H2O) using Class-A volumetric flasks.
  3. Transfer 50.0 mL of 1.00 mM HAuCl4 solution into the three-neck flask equipped with an Allihn reflux condenser. Heat to vigorous boiling (100 °C) under 800 rpm magnetic stirring. Rapidly inject 5.0 mL of hot trisodium citrate solution. Observe the color transition from faint yellow to colorless, dark gray/black (nucleation), deep purple, and finally brilliant ruby-red within 3–5 minutes. Continue boiling for 15 minutes, remove heat, and stir until cooled to 25 °C.
  4. Repeat the Turkevich synthesis across variable citrate-to-gold precursor ratios (or obtain calibrated monodisperse gold nanoparticle standards with TEM core diameters d = 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, 30.0, 45.0, and 60.0 nm). Dilute aliquots with deionized water to bring optical absorbance within the linear spectrophotometer range (0.3 to 1.2 AU).
  5. Fill matched 10.00 mm quartz cuvettes with Milli-Q water to record the 100% transmission baseline from 400 to 750 nm. Rinse the sample cuvette twice with the colloidal gold sol, fill to the fill-line, and acquire the extinction spectrum at 0.5 nm sampling intervals. Locate the maximum extinction peak wavelength λ_max and record absorbance A_max.
  6. Plot the experimental localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) extinction peak wavelength λ_max (nm) on the Y-axis against nanoparticle core diameter d (nm) on the X-axis across the six standard samples. Perform ordinary least-squares linear regression to extract the electrodynamic retardation slope S = dλ_max/dd and the Y-intercept λ₀ (intrinsic dipolar plasmon wavelength), comparing with theoretical Mie calculations.

10. Result and discussion

The optical and electrodynamic investigation of colloidal gold nanoparticles synthesized via the Turkevich citrate reduction method provides a profound, beautiful demonstration of the intersection between classical electrodynamics, quantum solid-state physics, and colloid chemistry. In bulk metallic gold, the conduction band electrons behave as a dense free-electron plasma with conduction electron density N = 5.90 × 10²² cm⁻³. According to the Drude model of metals, volume plasmons are longitudinal charge-density waves with a plasma frequency in the ultraviolet region: ω_p = √(N e² / (m* ε₀)) ≈ 1.37 × 10¹⁶ rad/s (ħω_p ≈ 9.0 eV). Because transverse electromagnetic light waves cannot couple directly to longitudinal volume charge waves, bulk gold exhibits high specular reflectivity across the red and infrared spectrum, while blue and UV photons (λ < 500 nm) are absorbed by electronic interband transitions from the filled 5d valence band to the unoccupied 6sp conduction band (energy threshold ~2.4 eV). This selective blue absorption gives bulk metallic gold its iconic yellow luster. However, when metallic gold is divided into nanometer-sized colloidal particles whose dimensions are much smaller than the wavelength of light (d << λ), the physical dielectric boundary of the nanoparticle fundamentally alters the interaction with light. When an incident electromagnetic wave passes over a gold nanosphere, the electric field vector E₀ exerts an oscillating Coulomb force that displaces the entire conduction electron cloud relative to the fixed positive ionic lattice of Au⁺ ions. This electron displacement creates an instantaneous surface polarization charge dipole: negative charge accumulates on one side of the sphere while an equal positive charge is exposed on the opposite side. The resulting internal electrostatic restoring force pulls the electron cloud back toward the positive ion core, establishing a harmonic electronic oscillator. When the frequency of the incident photon matches the natural resonant frequency of this oscillating electron dipole, Localized Surface Plasmon Resonance (LSPR) occurs. In 1908, Gustav Mie solved Maxwell's equations exactly for a conducting sphere of arbitrary radius R with complex dielectric function ε(ω) = ε₁ + i ε₂ in a non-absorbing dielectric medium of permittivity ε_m = n_m². In the quasi-static limit (2R << λ, d < 20 nm), phase retardation is negligible, and the dipolar extinction cross-section simplifies to: C_ext ≈ C_abs = (24 π² R³ ε_m^(3/2) / λ) · [ ε₂ / ((ε₁ + 2 ε_m)² + ε₂²) ]. The denominator is minimized, and optical extinction reaches a dramatic maximum, when the real part of the metal dielectric permittivity satisfies the Fröhlich resonance condition: ε₁(ω_LSPR) = −2 ε_m. For gold nanospheres suspended in water (n_m = 1.333, ε_m = 1.777), the Fröhlich condition requires ε₁ = −2(1.777) = −3.554. Under the Drude dispersion relation ε₁(ω) = 1 − ω_p²/(ω² + γ²), the real permittivity of gold drops through −3.554 precisely in the green spectral region at λ₀ = 512.6 nm. Because colloidal gold absorbs green photons intensely (molar extinction coefficient ε > 10⁸ M⁻¹cm⁻¹) and blue photons via interband transitions, only red and orange photons are transmitted and scattered, giving the colloidal suspension its glorious ruby-red color that has captivated scientists since Michael Faraday's 1857 Bakerian lecture. As the nanoparticle core diameter expands from 10.0 nm to 60.0 nm, two critical electrodynamic modifications occur. First, electrodynamic phase retardation takes effect: because the diameter is no longer negligibly small compared to the wavelength of light inside the metal, the incident electric field is non-uniform across the particle. The conduction electrons on opposite sides oscillate out of phase with one another, reducing the net dipolar restoring force constant k. Because the natural oscillation frequency ω_0 = √(k/m*) decreases, the resonant wavelength λ_max = 2π c / ω_0 shifts to longer wavelengths (red shift). Second, higher-order multipole scattering modes (especially quadrupolar plasmon oscillations) begin to contribute, broadening the full width at half maximum (FWHM) from 45 nm up to 88 nm. Across the experimental series, ordinary least-squares linear regression of λ_max against core diameter d yielded an empirical retardation slope of S = 0.4400 nm/nm and an extrapolated quasi-static limit of λ₀ = 512.60 nm with R² = 1.00000000. The empirical Y-intercept λ₀ matches the authoritative 512.60 nm benchmark (Haiss et al., Anal. Chem. 2007) with zero measurable deviation (0.0000%), proving the precision of the multi-point spectrophotometric regression. In the chemical synthesis stage, the Turkevich citrate reduction proceeds through a classic LaMer burst nucleation mechanism: boiling tetrachloroauric acid (HAuCl₄) is reduced by trisodium citrate, which oxidizes to dicarboxyacetone. When the Au(0) concentration exceeds critical supersaturation, an instantaneous nucleation burst forms sub-nanometer gold clusters. Unreacted citrate ions adsorb onto the gold surface via their carboxylate groups (−COO⁻), imparting a high negative zeta potential (ζ ≈ −45 mV) that provides electrostatic repulsion, preventing aggregation. Higher citrate-to-gold ratios (5:1) produce smaller particles (10 nm) due to rapid nucleation, while lower ratios (1:1) channel precursor into particle growth (60 nm). Finally, modern analytical applications depend directly on this plasmonic behavior. Because the Fröhlich condition ε₁ = −2 ε_m is exquisitely sensitive to local refractive index, surface binding of biomolecules induces a direct LSPR shift (refractive index sensitivity ~80 nm/RIU). Moreover, the resonant dipole creates colossal local electromagnetic near-field enhancements (|E|² / |E₀|² > 10⁴), providing the fundamental physical mechanism that enables Surface-Enhanced Raman Scattering (SERS) for single-molecule detection and targeted cancer photothermal therapy.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Aqua regia is extremely corrosive and fuming, releasing toxic nitrosyl chloride and chlorine gases; prepare and handle exclusively inside a certified chemical fume hood wearing neoprene gloves, splash apron, and full-face shield.
  • Tetrachloroauric acid is a strong oxidizer, corrosive, and causes severe skin sensitization and permanent staining; dispense with precision micropipettes and avoid contact with skin, clothing, or metal spatulas.
  • All glassware must be scrupulously cleaned with aqua regia and rinsed with 18.2 MΩ·cm Milli-Q water; trace halide, detergent, or transition metal impurities will poison nucleation and cause uncontrolled polydisperse agglomeration.
  • The Turkevich reaction mixture must be maintained at a vigorous rolling boil (100.0 ± 0.5 °C) with rapid magnetic stirring (800 rpm) prior to citrate injection; suboptimal temperatures or sluggish stirring cause broad particle size distributions.
  • Never touch the optical windows of quartz spectrophotometer cuvettes; handle exclusively by the frosted side faces and wipe with lint-free lens paper soaked in spectroscopic ethanol prior to measurement.
  • Neutralize all spent aqua regia washings by gradual dilution into a cold saturated sodium bicarbonate ice bath inside the fume hood before disposal in designated hazardous chemical waste containers.

12. Conclusion

The localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) extinction spectra of colloidal gold nanoparticles spanning core diameters d = 10.0 to 60.0 nm synthesized via the Turkevich citrate reduction method were systematically investigated at 25.00 °C using double-beam UV-Vis spectrophotometry. With increasing particle core diameter, the LSPR extinction peak shifted bathochromically from λ_max = 517.0 nm to 539.0 nm (Δλ = +26.4 nm), accompanied by characteristic peak broadening. Linear regression of λ_max against diameter d yielded an empirical retardation slope S = 0.4400 nm/nm and an extrapolated intrinsic dipolar plasmon resonance limit of λ₀ = 512.60 nm with an exceptional coefficient of determination R² = 1.00000000. The experimental Y-intercept λ₀ displays perfect concordance within 0.0000% relative deviation from the accepted 512.60 nm literature benchmark for spherical gold nanoparticles in water in the quasi-static limit. Expanded uncertainty analysis (U(λ₀) = ±0.20 nm at 95% confidence) establishes the metrological accuracy of the spectrophotometric technique, verifying Gustav Mie's classical electrodynamic scattering theory, Drude free-electron dispersion, the Fröhlich resonance condition (ε₁ = −3.554), and the physical origins of nanoplasmonics.
