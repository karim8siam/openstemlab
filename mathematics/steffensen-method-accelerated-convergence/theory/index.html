Mathematical foundations of Steffensen's Method.

Steffensen's method, introduced in 1933 by Danish mathematician Johan Frederik Steffensen, stands as one of the most brilliant triumphs of algorithmic acceleration in numerical analysis. For centuries, applied mathematicians were trapped between two competing paradigms: fixed-point Picard iteration was simple and required no derivatives, but suffered from sluggish linear convergence; Newton-Raphson was blazing fast with quadratic convergence, but demanded the exact analytical derivative f'(x), which is frequently intractable, computationally prohibitive, or unavailable in experimental simulations. In 1926, New Zealand mathematician Alexander Aitken discovered his celebrated Δ² acceleration process, which dramatically speeds up linearly converging sequences by estimating the asymptotic limit of a geometric progression. Steffensen's stroke of genius was to embed Aitken's Δ² formula directly into the recurrence loop of fixed-point iteration. Instead of waiting for a sequence to crawl along linearly before accelerating it, Steffensen generated two consecutive Picard steps—y = g(x) and z = g(y)—and immediately applied Aitken's extrapolation to generate the next iterate: x_{k+1} = x − (y − x)² / (z − 2y + x). When expressed directly for a root-finding problem f(x) = 0, Steffensen's formula assumes a breathtakingly simple derivative-free form: x_{k+1} = x_k − [f(x_k)]² / [f(x_k + f(x_k)) − f(x_k)]. A careful inspection reveals that the denominator divided by f(x_k) is precisely the forward-difference quotient [f(x_k + f(x_k)) − f(x_k)] / f(x_k), where the perturbation step size is chosen to be the function value f(x_k) itself! As the iterates approach the root x*, the function value f(x_k) naturally shrinks to zero at exactly the same rate as the error. Consequently, the truncation error of the finite-difference derivative quotient drops to zero in tandem with the root error, preserving the full quadratic convergence order α = 2.000 of Newton's method without requiring any analytic differentiation whatsoever. Furthermore, Steffensen's Ostrowski efficiency index is Eff = 2^(1/2) ≈ 1.414, exactly matching Newton's method while freeing the user from symbolic calculus. In this laboratory investigation, students evaluate Steffensen's accelerated method applied to the quadratic polynomial f(x) = x² − 2 = 0 (x* = √2 ≈ 1.414214), trace Aitken's Δ² acceleration, and extract the empirical quadratic convergence order α = 2.000 via logarithmic linear regression.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the mathematical connection between Aitken's Δ² process and Steffensen's method?

In 1926, New Zealand mathematician Alexander Aitken introduced the Δ² acceleration process to accelerate linearly converging sequences: x_{acc} = x_k − (Δx_k)² / (Δ²x_k), where Δx_k = x_{k+1} − x_k and Δ²x_k = x_{k+2} − 2x_{k+1} + x_k. In 1933, Danish mathematician Johan Frederik Steffensen applied Aitken's extrapolation directly into the iteration loop of a fixed-point map: instead of running Picard iteration for many steps and accelerating at the end, Steffensen took two Picard steps (y = g(x), z = g(y)) and immediately applied Aitken's acceleration to produce the next iterate x_{k+1}. This converted the sluggish linear convergence of fixed-point iteration into genuine quadratic convergence.

How does Steffensen's method achieve Newton-like quadratic convergence without computing derivatives?

Newton's method requires the analytic derivative f'(x_k) in the denominator: x_{k+1} = x_k − f(x_k)/f'(x_k). Steffensen replaces f'(x_k) with a forward-difference quotient using the function value itself as the perturbation step size: h = f(x_k). The difference quotient is: [f(x_k + f(x_k)) − f(x_k)] / f(x_k). Substituting this approximation into Newton's formula yields: x_{k+1} = x_k − [f(x_k)]² / [f(x_k + f(x_k)) − f(x_k)]. Because the perturbation h = f(x_k) automatically shrinks to zero at the exact same rate as the error itself (h = O(ε_k)), the finite-difference approximation error is O(ε_k), preserving the full quadratic convergence order α = 2.0 without ever evaluating an analytic derivative.

What is the Ostrowski computational efficiency index of Steffensen's method?

Steffensen's method requires 2 function evaluations per step: f(x_k) and f(x_k + f(x_k)). Its convergence order is α = 2. Therefore, its Ostrowski efficiency index is Eff = 2^(1/2) ≈ 1.414, identical to Newton's method. However, because Steffensen requires zero derivative calculations, it is vastly more practical for functions whose symbolic derivatives are cumbersome, expensive, or unavailable.

What happens if the denominator in Steffensen's formula approaches zero?

If f(x_k + f(x_k)) = f(x_k), the denominator vanishes. This can happen if f(x_k) is zero (the root is already found) or if the forward point lands on a local extremum. In software, if |f(x_k + f(x_k)) − f(x_k)| < ε_mach, the method falls back to a bisection step or a small numerical perturbation.

How does Steffensen's method perform on multiple roots?

Like Newton's method, if f(x) has a multiple root of multiplicity m > 1 (where f'(x*) = 0), Steffensen's method loses its quadratic convergence and degrades to linear convergence with asymptotic rate 1 − 1/m. Standard acceleration techniques or multiplicity modifications can be applied to restore higher-order convergence.

Can Steffensen's method be applied to solve systems of nonlinear equations?

Yes. The multivariate generalization of Steffensen's method replaces the Jacobian matrix J(x) with a matrix of divided differences evaluated along coordinate perturbations, creating a derivative-free quasi-Newton solver with quadratic convergence in ℝⁿ.

Under what conditions can Steffensen's method fail or diverge?

If the initial guess x₀ is far from the root, the perturbation h = f(x₀) can be very large, causing the forward evaluation f(x₀ + f(x₀)) to land in an unpredictable region of the function landscape, potentially leading to divergence. For robust implementation, a step-size safeguard is recommended.

Algorithm precautions and stability rules

  • Check for division by zero: ensure |f(x_k + f(x_k)) − f(x_k)| > ε_mach before evaluating the denominator.
  • Verify that the initial guess x₀ is sufficiently close to the root so that f(x₀) does not cause wild forward jumps.
  • If f(x_k) is large, scale the perturbation h = c·f(x_k) to prevent evaluations outside the function domain.
  • Monitor for multiple roots where convergence degrades from quadratic to linear.
  • Do not evaluate f(x_k + f(x_k)) if x_k + f(x_k) falls outside domain restrictions (e.g. negative inputs for logarithms).
  • Maintain 64-bit precision to capture the rapid doubling of significant digits.

Theoretical limitations

  • Sensitivity to initial guess: large f(x₀) can cause the forward point x₀ + f(x₀) to diverge violently.
  • Requires two function evaluations per iteration, double that of the secant method.
  • Degrades to linear convergence on multiple roots where f'(x*) = 0.
