Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Convective Tray Drying Kinetics.

Drying of moist solids is an energy-intensive simultaneous heat and mass transfer unit operation in chemical engineering, encompassing the manufacturing of pharmaceuticals, pigments, fertilizers, catalysts, polymers, and food products. In convective tray drying, hot dry air sweeps across exposed wet granular slabs placed in shallow trays. Heat conducts and convects from the air stream to the wet solid surface, providing the latent heat required to evaporate moisture, while evaporated water vapor diffuses across the hydrodynamic boundary layer into the bulk air. Plotting the drying rate flux R = - (L_s / A) · (dX / dt) against moisture content X (dry basis, kg water/kg dry solid) reveals characteristic drying periods. In the initial constant-rate drying period (X > X_c), the solid surface is completely saturated with free liquid water, and vaporization proceeds at a constant rate R_c governed purely by external boundary layer heat and mass transfer. Because convective mass transfer across a flat plate scales with air velocity as v_a^0.8 (Reynolds analogy), the constant drying rate follows: R_c = S · v_a^0.8. When moisture drops to the critical moisture content X_c, capillary action can no longer replenish surface water, dry spots appear, and drying enters the falling-rate period where internal moisture diffusion governs drying. In this laboratory investigation, a wet sand slab is dried in a benchtop tunnel tray dryer across air velocities from 1.0 to 6.0 m/s at 50.0 °C to determine the constant drying rate scaling slope S and identify critical moisture content X_c.

R_c = S · (v_a)^0.8 = 0.6500 · (v_a)^0.8

1. Heat and Mass Transfer Mechanisms in Convective Drying

Drying involves simultaneous thermal energy transfer from a heated drying gas to a wet solid and mass transfer of vaporized moisture from the solid back into the gas stream. In tray drying, warm air at temperature $T_a$ and humidity $Y_a$ flows over a horizontal wet porous slab.

2. The Constant-Rate Drying Period

When the solid contains abundant moisture ($X > X_c$), capillary forces pump liquid water from internal pores to the surface as rapidly as it evaporates. The surface behaves as a continuous pool of water. Equating convective heat supply to latent heat vaporization flux:

\[ q = h A (T_a - T_w) = R_c A \lambda_w \]

where $h$ is the convective heat transfer coefficient, $T_w$ is the wet-bulb surface temperature, $\lambda_w$ is latent heat of vaporization, and $R_c$ is drying flux ($\text{kg/(m}^2\cdot\text{h)}$). Expressing in terms of mass transfer:

\[ R_c = k_y (Y_w - Y_a) \]

where $k_y$ is the gas-phase mass transfer coefficient and $Y_w$ is saturation humidity at $T_w$.

3. Velocity Scaling & Boundary Layer Analogy

From the Chilton–Colburn $j_H$ and $j_D$ analogies for turbulent flow over flat plates, convective heat and mass transfer coefficients scale with Reynolds number as $Re^{0.8} \propto v_a^{0.8}$. Therefore, constant drying rate flux follows the power-law relation:

\[ R_c = S \cdot (v_a)^{0.8} \]

where $S$ is the convective drying velocity slope in $\text{kg/(m}^2\cdot\text{h)/(m/s)}^{0.8}$.

4. Critical Moisture Content & The Falling-Rate Period

At the critical moisture content $X_c$, internal capillary flow can no longer maintain surface saturation. Dry spots develop, and the drying rate declines. In the first falling-rate period, unsaturated surface drying occurs; in the second falling-rate period, the evaporation plane recedes into the interior, and internal vapor diffusion governs drying kinetics.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

Why does the drying rate remain strictly constant during the constant-rate period?

The solid surface remains completely covered by a continuous film of free water fed by capillary action from the porous interior; the rate is governed entirely by external convective heat and mass transfer across the air boundary layer, functioning like a wet-bulb thermometer.

What is the critical moisture content X_c and what parameters influence its value?

X_c is the average moisture content at which the constant-rate period ends and the falling-rate period begins; it depends on particle size, pore structure, slab thickness, and drying rate (higher drying rates cause earlier surface dryout, increasing X_c).

What mechanisms govern moisture transport during the falling-rate drying period?

Internal liquid movement occurs via liquid capillary flow in pores, vapor diffusion through unsaturated pores, and surface adsorption diffusion; as moisture drops toward equilibrium moisture X_e, internal diffusion becomes severely rate-limiting.

Why does the temperature of the wet solid equal the wet-bulb temperature during the constant-rate period?

All heat conducted and convected from the air to the solid is consumed as latent heat of vaporization (h (T_air - T_s) = R_c λ); dynamic thermal equilibrium naturally stabilizes the surface at the adiabatic saturation (wet-bulb) temperature.

How does relative humidity of the drying air affect drying rate?

The mass transfer driving force is the humidity difference (Y_sat - Y_air); higher ambient humidity reduces this gradient, decreasing the constant drying rate and elevating equilibrium moisture content X_e.

What is case hardening (surface crusting) in food and ceramic drying?

Excessively rapid early drying evaporates surface moisture faster than internal water can diffuse, causing colloidal shrinkage, pore collapse, and formation of an impermeable hard crust that traps internal moisture and causes cracking.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Insulate the bottom and sides of the tray to ensure heat transfer occurs strictly across the top exposed horizontal surface.
  • Maintain constant air dry-bulb and wet-bulb temperatures throughout the drying cycle.
  • Ensure the sand surface is packed smooth and flush with tray edges to avoid aerodynamic trip edges.
  • Calibrate the under-pan balance with test weights before each run.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (1.0 to 6.0 m/s).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
