Probabilistic foundations of Chi-Squared Independence Test.

Contingency table analysis and the Chi-Squared test of independence form the core methodology for evaluating relationships between categorical variables in social science, medicine, epidemiology, and market research. Developed by Karl Pearson and expanded by Harald Cramér, the framework compares observed cross-classifications against theoretical independence margins, providing robust significance testing and normalized effect sizes. Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How is the Chi-Squared test of independence formulated for an R × C contingency table?

Let X and Y be two categorical variables with R and C levels, respectively. In a sample of size N, observations are classified into an R × C contingency table where O_{ij} is the observed count in row i and column j. The null hypothesis H_0 asserts statistical independence: P(X = i, Y = j) = P(X = i) P(Y = j) for all i, j. The marginal totals are R_i = ∑_{j=1}^C O_{ij} and C_j = ∑_{i=1}^R O_{ij}. Under H_0, the expected cell frequency is E_{ij} = N (R_i / N) (C_j / N) = (R_i C_j) / N. The test statistic is χ² = ∑_{i=1}^R ∑_{j=1}^C (O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2 / E_{ij}.

Why are degrees of freedom for an R × C contingency table equal to (R - 1)(C - 1)?

An R × C table has R · C individual cell counts. However, the row marginal sums are fixed (R constraints), and the column marginal sums are fixed (C constraints). Because the sum of row sums equals the sum of column sums (∑ R_i = ∑ C_j = N), one marginal constraint is redundant. Thus, the number of independent constraints is R + C - 1. Subtracting these constraints from total cells yields: df = R C - (R + C - 1) = R C - R - C + 1 = (R - 1)(C - 1). In a 2 × 2 table, df = (2 - 1)(2 - 1) = 1.

What is Cramér's V, and how does it normalize Chi-Squared association strength?

The raw Chi-Squared statistic χ² scales linearly with sample size N, making it useless as a direct measure of association strength. Harald Cramér (1946) introduced Cramér's V (or Cramér's phi): V = √[χ² / (N · min(R - 1, C - 1))]. For a 2 × 2 table, min(R - 1, C - 1) = 1, so V simplifies to the phi coefficient φ = √(χ² / N). Cramér's V is strictly bounded in [0, 1], where V = 0 indicates complete independence and V = 1 indicates complete deterministic association, independent of sample size N.

What is Yates' continuity correction, and when should it be applied?

Frank Yates (1934) proposed a continuity correction for 2 × 2 contingency tables with 1 degree of freedom to account for approximating discrete hypergeometric probabilities with the continuous Chi-Squared distribution. The corrected statistic is χ²_Yates = ∑∑ (|O_{ij} - E_{ij}| - 0.5)^2 / E_{ij} = N (|O_{11} O_{22} - O_{12} O_{21}| - N/2)^2 / (R_1 R_2 C_1 C_2). The correction prevents Type I error inflation in small-to-moderate tables, though modern statisticians note it can be slightly conservative.

When should Fisher's Exact Test be used instead of the Chi-Squared test of independence?

When expected cell counts are small (violating Cochran's criterion E_{ij} ≥ 5), the continuous Chi-Squared approximation fails. Sir Ronald Fisher (1935) developed Fisher's Exact Test for 2 × 2 tables. Conditioning on fixed marginal totals, the probability of observing cell count O_{11} follows the Hypergeometric distribution: P(O_{11} = a) = [(R_1 choose a) (R_2 choose (C_1 - a))] / (N choose C_1). The exact p-value is computed by summing hypergeometric probabilities over all tables as or more extreme than the observed table.

What is the Odds Ratio (OR) in a 2 × 2 table, and how does it quantify association?

In a 2 × 2 table with cells [[a, b], [c, d]], the odds of success in row 1 is a/b, and in row 2 is c/d. The Odds Ratio is OR = (a / b) / (c / d) = (a d) / (b c), the cross-product ratio. An OR = 1 indicates equal odds (independence); OR > 1 indicates positive association; OR < 1 indicates negative association. Unlike risk ratios, the odds ratio is symmetric and invariant under case-control retrospective sampling, making it the primary metric in epidemiology.

How does Simpson's Paradox arise in three-way contingency tables?

Simpson's paradox occurs when an apparent association between two categorical variables in a 2 × 2 table is reversed or eliminated when the data is disaggregated across the levels of a third confounding variable Z. Mathematically, it arises because marginal probabilities are weighted averages of conditional probabilities: P(Y | X) = ∑ P(Y | X, Z = z) P(Z = z | X). If the confounder distribution P(Z | X) differs between groups, the aggregated association can point in the exact opposite direction of the true causal effect.

Experimental precautions and statistical checks

  • Ensure that each observation contributes to exactly one cell in the contingency table (mutually exclusive classification).
  • Verify that expected cell counts satisfy Cochran's rule (E_{ij} ≥ 5); use Fisher's exact test if counts are sparse.
  • Always report effect size metrics (Cramér's V or Odds Ratio) alongside p-values to communicate practical relationship strength.
  • Beware of Simpson's paradox: check for lurking confounding variables before drawing causal conclusions from aggregated tables.

Theoretical assumptions & limitations

  • The Chi-Squared test of independence measures statistical association, but cannot establish directional causation.
  • Cramér's V does not indicate the sign or direction of association in tables larger than 2 × 2.
