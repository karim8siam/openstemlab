Civil engineering mechanics & structural foundations of Double Shear Bolted Joint.

Bolted connections represent the primary joining technique in structural steel buildings, industrial truss bridges, and offshore platforms, enabling rapid on-site erection without specialized field welding. Structural bolts are subjected to shear in two primary connection configurations: single shear (lap joints) and double shear (butt joints with dual splice plates). Double shear connections are vastly superior because the symmetric dual splice plates eliminate eccentric bending moments and prying forces, doubling the effective shear resistance area. Under ASTM A325 / AISC 360, when a tensile load P is applied across a double-shear butt splice joined by a bolt of nominal diameter d, the load is resisted by two parallel shear failure planes intersecting the bolt shank. The average nominal shear stress on each shear plane is τ = P / (2 A_b) = P / [2 (π d^2 / 4)]. Prior to slip, friction between tightly clamped plate surfaces resists the load (slip-critical connection); once clamping friction is overcome, the bolt goes into direct mechanical bearing against hole walls, exhibiting an elastic joint shear deformation Δ proportional to applied load P. In this laboratory investigation, double-shear bolted joints are tested across load levels from 20 to 120 kN to evaluate joint shear compliance and determine shear performance.

Δ = 0.0285 · P

1. Mechanics of Double-Shear Bolted Joints

Consider a symmetric double-shear butt splice connection comprising a central main plate of thickness $t_m$ clamped between two symmetric outer splice plates of thickness $t_s = t_m / 2$ joined by a single high-strength bolt of diameter $d$. When a tensile force $P$ is applied to the main plate and equilibrated by forces $P/2$ in each splice plate, two distinct shear planes traverse the bolt at the interfaces between the main plate and splice plates.

By static equilibrium of the isolated central bolt segment, the total shear force $V_{total} = P$ is partitioned equally between the two shear planes:

\[ V_1 = V_2 = \frac{P}{2} \]

The nominal average double-shear stress $\tau$ acting across the gross cross-sectional area $A_b = \frac{\pi d^2}{4}$ of the bolt is:

\[ \tau = \frac{P}{2 A_b} = \frac{P}{2 \left( \pi d^2 / 4 \right)} = \frac{2 P}{\pi d^2} \]

2. Elastic Joint Compliance & Bolt-Plate Deformation Model

Total relative joint shear displacement $\Delta$ measured across the connection interfaces represents the combined sum of three elastic mechanisms: (1) Elastic shear deformation of the bolt shank $\Delta_{shear} = \frac{P t_m}{2 G A_b}$; (2) Localized elastic bearing indentation of the plate hole walls and bolt shank $\Delta_{bearing}$; and (3) Elastic flexural bending curvature of the bolt shank acting as a short beam $\Delta_{bend}$.

In the elastic bearing regime, the composite joint deformation $\Delta$ scales directly with applied tensile load $P$:

\[ \Delta = C_{joint} \cdot P \]

where $C_{joint} = 0.0285\text{ mm/kN}$ represents the experimental joint shear compliance parameter for a standard M16 connection assembly.

3. Ultimate Shear Capacity & AISC Design Limits

According to AISC 360 Section J3, nominal bolt shear strength $R_n$ is:

\[ R_n = F_{nv} \cdot A_b \cdot n_s \]

where $F_{nv}$ is nominal shear strength ($372\text{ MPa}$ for ASTM A325 with threads included, $469\text{ MPa}$ with threads excluded), $A_b$ is gross bolt area, and $n_s = 2$ is the number of shear planes. For an M16 Grade 8.8 bolt ($A_b = 201.06\text{ mm}^2$) with threads excluded, double-shear capacity is $R_n = 469 \times 201.06 \times 2 = 188.6\text{ kN}$.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

Why is a double-shear connection inherently more efficient than a single-shear lap joint?

A single-shear lap joint is geometrically asymmetric, generating severe eccentric bending moments that pry the plates apart and induce combined tension-shear in the bolt; double shear is perfectly symmetric, eliminating eccentric prying and doubling the shear capacity.

What is the difference between a bearing-type connection and a slip-critical connection?

In slip-critical connections, high bolt pretension clamps plates tightly, transferring load entirely through surface friction with zero slip; in bearing-type connections, load exceeds friction, allowing plates to slip into direct mechanical bearing against the bolt shank.

How does thread inclusion in the shear plane affect bolt shear capacity under AISC 360?

If bolt threads are excluded from the shear planes (AISC 'X' condition), full shank gross area A_b resists shear; if threads are included ('N' condition), the reduced root area decreases nominal shear capacity by approximately 20% to 25%.

What is bolt pretensioning, and how is it achieved in structural steel construction?

Pretensioning elongates the bolt to create clamping force; achieved via calibrated torque wrench, turn-of-nut method (e.g. 1/3 turn past snug tight), twist-off tension-control (TC) bolts, or direct tension indicators (DTI).

What are the three primary failure modes of a bolted structural connection?

Failure modes: (1) Bolt shear fracture across the shank; (2) Plate bearing failure (tear-out or ovalization of bolt holes); (3) Plate net-section tensile fracture.

Why are hardened steel washers (ASTM F436) mandatory beneath high-strength bolt heads and nuts?

Washers distribute intense clamping pressure, prevent galling and scoring of softer structural steel plates during torque tightening, and cover oversized holes to maintain proper bearing area.

Experimental precautions and laboratory safety

  • Verify connection plates are perfectly aligned along the load axis to eliminate parasitic in-plane bending moments.
  • Ensure LVDT gauge mounting brackets are attached securely to plates without loose play or rocking.
  • Tighten bolts with calibrated torque wrenches; never use uncalibrated impact guns for experimental joints.
  • Operate behind safety blast screens when loading high-strength bolted connections to protect against sudden shear snap.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The civil engineering physical models apply within the specified calibrated operational window (20.0 to 120.0 kN).
  • Assumes homogeneous, isotropic continuum material behavior without unmodeled micro-fracture anomalies.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • Extreme inelastic yielding or cyclic degradation requires non-linear constitutive continuum damage mechanics.
