Biomedical and physiological foundations of Electromagnetic Blood Flowmeter.

In cardiovascular surgery, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), and organ perfusion hemodynamics, real-time continuous measurement of volumetric blood flow rate is essential for evaluating cardiac output and graft patency. Invented by Alexander Kolin in 1936, the electromagnetic blood flowmeter operates on the fundamental physical principle of Faraday's law of induction: when an electrically conductive fluid (such as blood containing dissociated Na⁺, K⁺, and Cl⁻ electrolytes) moves with mean velocity v through a transverse magnetic field of flux density B, moving ions experience Lorentz forces (F = q · (v × B)) that deflect positive and negative ions toward opposite vessel walls. This creates a transverse electric potential V_i perpendicular to both the flow velocity vector and magnetic field vector: V_i = B · d · v, where d is the inner lumen vessel diameter. Substituting volumetric flow rate Q = v · (π·d² / 4) yields a strictly linear relationship: V_i = [4·B / (π·d)] · Q = k_q · Q. To eliminate electrochemical DC half-cell offset drift, modern medical flowmeter probes utilize low-frequency square-wave or sinusoidal AC electromagnet excitation (typically 200 Hz to 400 Hz). In this experiment, a calibrated cannulated electromagnetic flow probe is tested with physiological saline across controlled volumetric flow rates to evaluate linear induction sensitivity k_q.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How does Faraday's law of induction apply to blood flow measurement?

Blood is an electrical conductor containing dissociated electrolyte ions. When blood flows through an applied magnetic field B, the Lorentz force (F = q·[v × B]) pushes positive and negative ions to opposite sides of the vessel, generating an induced transverse voltage V = B·d·v directly proportional to flow velocity.

Why is AC magnetic field excitation used instead of a static DC magnetic field?

DC magnetic fields cause the platinum electrodes to suffer from huge, drifting electrochemical half-cell potentials and galvanic contact offsets that dwarf the microvolt flow signal. AC square-wave excitation alternates the signal polarity, allowing AC coupling and synchronous demodulation to completely reject DC drift.

What is the 'transformer artifact' in electromagnetic flowmeters?

Alternating current in the electromagnet generates time-varying magnetic flux (dΦ/dt) that induces an unwanted secondary transformer voltage in the loop formed by the pickup electrodes and lead wires. Sampling the signal only during the flat plateau of a square wave (where dB/dt = 0) eliminates this spike.

Does blood hematocrit affect the calibration of an electromagnetic flowmeter?

As long as blood electrical conductivity exceeds a minimum threshold (~0.1 S/m), the induced potential is strictly independent of fluid conductivity and hematocrit because the high amplifier input impedance draws virtually zero current.

How does non-axisymmetric velocity profile affect measurement accuracy?

According to Shercliff's weight function theory, if the magnetic field is uniform across a circular lumen, the induced potential reflects the true volume-averaged flow velocity regardless of whether the profile is laminar, parabolic, or turbulent.

What is the difference between a cannulated flow probe and a perivascular cuff probe?

A cannulated probe requires cutting the blood vessel and inserting the fluid through an internal rigid lumen (used in heart-lung bypass and ECMO), whereas a perivascular cuff snaps externally around the intact, pulsating blood vessel during surgical monitoring.

Experimental precautions and patient safety

  • Purge all microscopic air bubbles from the sensor lumen; air is an electrical insulator that open-circuits the electrode path.
  • Ensure grounding of the fluid reservoir to avoid stray electrostatic charge buildup in plastic tubing.
  • Verify that magnetic excitation frequency is locked to synchronous demodulator sampling gates.
  • Maintain constant fluid temperature (37.0 °C) to stabilize electrolyte conductivity.

Theoretical assumptions & biological limits

  • The linear induction model applies across non-turbulent and turbulent regimes up to 300 mL/min for the 6 mm probe.
  • Assumes full lumen contact; partial filling or vessel collapse causes severe calibration errors.
  • Requires minimum fluid electrical conductivity (> 0.05 S/m); cannot measure pure distilled water or non-conductive oils.
  • Perivascular in-vivo applications can be affected by blood vessel wall thickness and non-conductive fat deposits.
