Biomedical and physiological foundations of EEG Alpha Wave Spectral Power.

Electroencephalography (EEG) records continuous postsynaptic potential fluctuations generated by pyramidal neurons in the cerebral cortex. Discovered by Hans Berger in 1929, the dominant rhythm of the relaxed, awake human brain with eyes closed is the alpha rhythm (8 to 12 Hz, amplitude 20 to 80 μV), most prominent over occipital and parietal electrode sites (O1, O2, Pz). Alpha oscillations originate from synchronized reciprocal thalamocortical reverberating loops that reflect a sensory idling or gating state of visual cortices. When an individual opens their eyes or engages in focused visual-spatial problem solving, visual photic input via the optic nerve and lateral geniculate nucleus desynchronizes these thalamocortical networks—a phenomenon known as the 'Berger effect' or alpha desynchronization (alpha blocking). In neuroengineering, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), and cognitive ergonomics, the attenuation of alpha power serves as a quantifiable metric of cortical sensory processing and mental workload. Across increasing ambient visual illuminance E_v, occipital alpha spectral power density P_alpha declines in a predictable linear relationship: P_alpha = -k_alpha · E_v + P_0. In this experiment, occipital EEG signals recorded using the International 10-20 system are digitized to analyze alpha band spectral power attenuation across calibrated photopic illumination levels.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the Berger effect (alpha blocking) in neurophysiology?

The Berger effect is the immediate desynchronization and reduction in amplitude of the 8–12 Hz occipital alpha rhythm when a relaxed subject opens their eyes or directs active attention to visual stimuli, transitioning the cortex from synchronized idling to active desynchronized processing.

What neural structures generate the occipital alpha rhythm?

Alpha oscillations are generated by reciprocal resonant loops between thalamic relay neurons (lateral geniculate nucleus and pulvinar) and cortical pyramidal neurons in visual areas (Brodmann areas 17, 18, 19). Thalamic pacemakers drive the rhythmic synchrony.

What are the four primary frequency bands in clinical human EEG?

Delta (0.5 to 4 Hz, deep slow-wave sleep, pathology), Theta (4 to 8 Hz, drowsiness, memory encoding), Alpha (8 to 12 Hz, relaxed wakefulness with eyes closed), and Beta (13 to 30 Hz, active thinking, motor planning, alertness). Gamma (> 30 Hz) reflects cross-modal binding.

What is the International 10-20 System for electrode placement?

The 10-20 system is an internationally standardized method to place EEG electrodes based on anatomical landmarks (nasion, inion, and preauricular points). Electrode spacings are placed at intervals of 10% or 20% of the total skull contour distances, ensuring reproducible anatomical coverage.

Why are ocular electrooculogram (EOG) artifacts so prominent in frontal EEG leads?

The human eyeball behaves as a permanent electrical dipole (+6 mV cornea relative to retina). Blinking and saccadic eye movements rotate this dipole, inducing massive (100 to 400 μV) artifact potentials that propagate into frontal EEG channels (Fp1, Fp2).

How do Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs) utilize alpha rhythms?

BCIs use changes in alpha power (such as sensorimotor mu-rhythms over C3/C4 during motor imagery, or occipital alpha during visual attention) as voluntary control signals to operate computer cursors, communication spellers, or neuro-prosthetic robotic hands.

Experimental precautions and patient safety

  • Instruct the subject to refrain from blinking or clenching their jaw during the 30-second EEG data recording epochs.
  • Ensure all electrode contact impedances are balanced and strictly under 3.0 kΩ to prevent noise unbalance.
  • Use ripple-free DC power for lighting; standard AC fluorescent lamps induce 120 Hz optical flicker artifacts.
  • Enclose the recording station in a Faraday cage to eliminate 60 Hz mains interference.

Theoretical assumptions & biological limits

  • The linear attenuation model applies across photopic ambient light levels (50 to 300 lux).
  • Assumes calm, non-anxious subject; psychological anxiety or arousal can suppress alpha rhythm independently of light.
  • Approximately 5% to 10% of the healthy human population naturally produces little to no surface-recordable alpha rhythm.
  • Subject to drowsiness effects: onset of sleep transitions the alpha rhythm into slower theta frequencies.
