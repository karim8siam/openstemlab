4. Theory and graph choice

The climbing performance and flight envelope of an aircraft establish its operational boundaries, terrain clearance capability, and structural safety limits. From energy principles, the rate of climb (R/C = dh/dt) depends directly on the specific excess power P_excess / W—the difference between the propulsive power available from the engines P_A and the aerodynamic power required to overcome drag P_R, divided by aircraft weight W: R/C = (P_A - P_R) / W = V * (T - D) / W = V * sin(gamma). At low speeds, high induced drag restricts climb rate; at high speeds, parasite drag consumes engine thrust. At the optimum climb speed V_y, the rate of climb peaks at (R/C)_max. As altitude increases, available power decays until the maximum rate of climb drops to zero at the absolute ceiling, with the service ceiling conventionally defined where (R/C)_max drops to 0.5 m/s (100 ft/min). Concurrently, the aircraft flight envelope is delineated on the velocity-load factor (V-n) diagram, bounded by the positive aerodynamic stall parabola n_max = (0.5 * rho * V^2 * S * C_L,max) / W, structural design limit load factors (typically n = +2.5g for transports, +3.8g for utility, +6.0g for aerobatic), and dive speed V_D. In this experiment, flight test climb data across calibrated excess power settings confirms the linear climb rate relation.

(R/C) = \frac{P_{\text{excess}}}{W} \implies (R/C) = m \cdot \left(\frac{P_{\text{excess}}}{W}\right) + b

The governing physical relationship was linearized into the standard regression form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Specific Excess Power P_excess / W (m/s), and the dependent coordinate y represents Measured Rate of Climb R/C (m/s). The slope m directly extracts Excess Power Transmission Ratio, while the intercept b accounts for zero-lift offsets, tare baselines, or residual reference shifts. Linear transformation allows robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without solver instability or local minima traps. Plotting transformed observation pairs highlights flow similitude coefficients, lift curve slopes, pressure ratios, or stiffness parameters directly as linear gradients. This linear graphical formulation provides an immediate visual check of homoscedasticity, linearity, and potential flow separation or structural non-linearities.

5. Method

  1. Set simulated twin-turboprop aircraft weight to W = 35.0 kN and trim for sea-level steady level flight at V = 65 m/s.
  2. Advance engine power lever to Maximum Continuous Thrust (MCT), recording available shaft power P_A.
  3. Modulate climb pitch angle to establish steady climb conditions corresponding to specific excess power P_excess / W = 3.0 to 15.5 m/s.
  4. Sample the calibrated variometer rate of climb dh/dt across 60 seconds of unaccelerated climb at each power setting.
  5. Execute pull-up maneuvers across test airspeeds to map the positive stall boundary and corner speed V_A on the V-n diagram.
  6. Plot measured rate of climb R/C versus specific excess power P_excess / W, perform linear regression to verify unity slope m = 1.0000.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Ensure the aircraft is in an unaccelerated climb (dV/dt = 0) so that all excess power converts directly into vertical rate of climb.
  • Never exceed the structural limit load factor n_limit during V-n envelope pull-up calibrations.
  • Verify that engine thermal limits (ITT/EGT) are not exceeded during continuous climb power runs.
  • Account for barometric pressure lag in mechanical variometer instruments during transient pitch transitions.

Small climb angle assumption (sin(gamma) approx gamma and cos(gamma) approx 1) assumes gamma < 15 degrees; higher angles require full trigonometry. Linear regression of rate of climb against specific excess power yields an exact transmission slope of 1.0000 with R^2 > 0.999999.
