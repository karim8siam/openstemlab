Simulation assumptions and limits

The teaching model assumes pure longitudinal Hookean elasticity and a linear velocity distribution along the spring wire corresponding to Rayleigh's m_eff = (1/3) m_s approximation. In a physical laboratory, small pitch-angle helix corrections, air drag damping, and slight support stand compliance introduce minute second-order deviations. The loaded spring harmonic vibration experiment provides an elegant demonstration of distributed mass mechanics in continuous systems. By combining ordinary least-squares regression with energy principles, students verify both Hooke's elastic stiffness constant k and Rayleigh's one-third kinetic energy theorem m_eff = (1/3) m_s with sub-percent precision.

Know your apparatus.

Precision Close-Coiled Helical Spring

Provide a linear Hookean axial restoring force (k₀ = 20.00 N m⁻¹) under vertical elongation, with measured physical wire mass m_s = 60.0 g.

Rigid Support Stand & Suspension Clamp

Secure the top loop of the helical spring rigidly without slippage or mechanical compliance, preventing parasitic energy dissipation to the bench.

Calibrated Slotted Weight Hanger & Masses

Provide precision loading increments (100.0 g to 300.0 g, calibrated to ±0.05 g) to vary the oscillating system inertial mass.

Vertical Anti-Parallax Millimeter Scale

Verify vertical oscillation amplitude (≤ 2.0 cm) and maintain small-strain linear elastic behavior throughout all observation runs.

Precision Digital Electronic Chronometer

Measure the elapsed transit duration for 20 complete vertical cycles with 0.01 s resolution, minimizing fractional period timing error.

Fiducial Equilibrium Level Pointer

Provide a sharp horizontal reference line at static equilibrium to trigger chronometer timing precisely at the point of maximum vertical velocity.

FOLLOW THE METHOD

A reason behind every step.

Determine the elastic spring stiffness constant from the period of vertical simple harmonic oscillation under varying suspended loads, and evaluate the effective dynamic contribution of the spring's distributed self-mass. Suggested independent settings: 100.0, 150.0, 200.0, 250.0, 300.0 g.

1

Spring Mass Measurement and Support Mounting

Weigh the unextended helical spring on a precision laboratory balance to determine its physical wire mass m_s = 60.0 g. Clamp the upper suspension hook firmly into the rigid support stand boss-head, ensuring that the spring hangs freely without rubbing against the vertical mounting rod.

Why this step matters

Determining the physical mass of the spring wire is essential for comparison with the dynamic effective mass m_eff extracted from the regression y-intercept.

2

Suspended Load Attachment and Static Equilibrium Tare

Attach the weight hanger and load weights to establish the initial trial mass of m = 100.0 g. Allow the loaded spring to gently extend under gravity until it comes completely to rest at its static equilibrium position y_eq = m g / k.

Why this step matters

The system must oscillate symmetrically about its true gravitational equilibrium level, where the gravitational force is exactly balanced by initial elastic tension.

3

Fiducial Pointer Alignment and Amplitude Setting

Align the horizontal pointer on the weight hanger with the index mark on the vertical scale. Displace the load vertically downward by a small, controlled amplitude of approximately 1.5 cm to 2.0 cm strictly along the vertical axis, ensuring zero lateral wobble or conical swinging.

Why this step matters

Small vertical displacements guarantee that the spring remains strictly within its linear Hookean regime and prevents the excitation of parasitic pendulum or torsional modes.

4

Chronometer Triggering and 20-Cycle Timing

Release the load cleanly from rest without imparting any initial velocity. Allow two initial cycles to pass to ensure steady-state harmonic motion, then trigger the digital chronometer as the pointer passes upward through the static equilibrium fiducial mark. Count exactly 20 complete cycles and stop the timer, recording t₂₀ to 0.01 s.

Why this step matters

Timing 20 complete cycles reduces the fractional uncertainty of human reaction time by a factor of 20, and triggering at equilibrium maximizes timing sharpness where velocity is greatest.

5

Systematic Load Variation Across the Mass Range

Add slotted masses in increments of 50.0 g to test settings of 150.0 g, 200.0 g, 250.0 g, and 300.0 g. At each mass station, verify vertical alignment, maintain a constant initial displacement amplitude, and record the elapsed time for 20 complete oscillations.

Why this step matters

Collecting observations across a broad range of load masses provides the leverage required for high-precision slope and intercept determination in ordinary least-squares regression.

6

Period Calculation, Squaring, and Regression Analysis

Compute the single-cycle period T = t₂₀ / 20.0 and calculate T² for each load station. Plot T² (in s²) on the vertical axis against suspended load mass m (in kg) on the horizontal axis. Fit a linear regression line to determine the slope S and y-intercept b, then compute the spring stiffness k = 4π² / S and the effective self-mass m_eff = b / S.

Why this step matters

Plotting T² against m linearizes the harmonic oscillator governing equation, allowing simultaneous extraction of the elastic stiffness constant and the spring's effective self-mass.
