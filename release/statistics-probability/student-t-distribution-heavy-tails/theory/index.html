Probabilistic foundations of Student's t & Heavy Tails.

Student's t-distribution is the cornerstone of small-sample statistical inference and robust data modeling. Derived by William Sealy Gosset to account for the uncertainty of estimated sample variances, the t-distribution features heavier, polynomial tails that protect against false-positive inference in small datasets. In this laboratory, we synthesize Gosset ratios, explore leptokurtic tail behavior, investigate the Cauchy limit, track asymptotic Gaussian convergence, and verify the theoretical variance scaling relation m = 2.000. In mathematical statistics, empirical data science, and modern computational engineering, understanding the asymptotic sampling properties and theoretical behavior of Student's t-Distribution, Degrees of Freedom, and Asymptotic Normal Convergence provides crucial guarantees. Rigorous experimental validation connects foundational probability laws with observable empirical phenomena, ensuring that statistical inference remains valid under finite sample sizes and real-world data collection constraints.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

Who was 'Student', and why was the t-distribution developed?

William Sealy Gosset, a chemist and statistician working at the Guinness Brewery in Dublin, published under the pseudonym 'Student' in 1908. Brewery quality control involved small samples (often n = 3 to 5), where the population standard deviation σ was unknown and had to be estimated by sample standard deviation s. Gosset demonstrated that standardizing via (x̄ - μ) / (s / √n) does not yield a normal distribution, but rather a heavier-tailed distribution whose shape depends explicitly on sample size n.

How is Student's t-distribution mathematically constructed from Gaussian and Chi-Squared variables?

Let Z ~ N(0, 1) be a standard normal random variable and V ~ χ²(ν) be an independent Chi-Squared random variable with ν degrees of freedom. The random variable T = Z / √(V / ν) follows Student's t-distribution with ν degrees of freedom, denoted T ~ t(ν). Its probability density function is f(t; ν) = (Γ((ν + 1)/2) / (√(πν) Γ(ν/2))) (1 + t^2 / ν)^(- (ν + 1)/2) for t ∈ (-∞, ∞).

What are the mean, variance, and higher moments of Student's t-distribution?

Due to its power-law tail decay (1 + t^2/ν)^(-(ν+1)/2) ∝ t^(-(ν+1)), moments of order k exist if and only if k < ν: (1) Mean: E[T] = 0 for ν > 1 (undefined for ν ≤ 1). (2) Variance: Var(T) = ν / (ν - 2) for ν > 2 (infinite for 1 < ν ≤ 2, undefined for ν ≤ 1). (3) Skewness: γ_1 = 0 for ν > 3 by symmetry. (4) Excess Kurtosis: γ_2 = 6 / (ν - 4) for ν > 4 (infinite for 2 < ν ≤ 4).

What is the Cauchy distribution, and why does it represent a pathological case of Student's t?

When ν = 1, Student's t-distribution simplifies to the standard Cauchy distribution, with probability density f(t) = 1 / (π (1 + t^2)). The tails decay as 1/t^2, which is so heavy that the integral ∫_{-∞}^∞ |t| f(t) dt diverges. Consequently, the Cauchy distribution has no mathematical expectation, no variance, and violates the classical Law of Large Numbers (the sample mean of n Cauchy variates has the exact same Cauchy distribution as a single draw).

How does Student's t-distribution converge to the standard Normal distribution as ν → ∞?

By the fundamental limit definition of the exponential function, lim_{ν → ∞} (1 + t^2 / ν)^(- (ν + 1)/2) = exp(-t^2 / 2). Furthermore, applying Stirling's approximation to the Gamma function ratio yields lim_{ν → ∞} Γ((ν + 1)/2) / (√(πν) Γ(ν/2)) = 1 / √(2π). Therefore, lim_{ν → ∞} f(t; ν) = (1 / √(2π)) e^(-t^2 / 2), establishing pointwise convergence to standard normal N(0, 1). In practice, t(ν) is nearly indistinguishable from N(0, 1) when ν ≥ 30.

Why are t-based confidence intervals wider than z-based confidence intervals for the same confidence level?

For a 100(1 - α)% confidence interval, the critical value t_{α/2, ν} is strictly greater than the standard normal critical value z_{α/2} for any finite degrees of freedom ν (e.g., for 95% confidence, z_{0.025} = 1.960, while t_{0.025, 4} = 2.776). The wider t interval accounts for the additional sampling uncertainty introduced by estimating the unknown population variance σ^2 using sample variance s^2.

What is the non-central t-distribution, and what is its role in statistical power analysis?

If X ~ N(δ, 1) has non-zero mean δ and V ~ χ²(ν) is independent, the ratio T' = X / √(V / ν) follows a non-central t-distribution with ν degrees of freedom and non-centrality parameter δ. The non-central t-distribution is skewed and provides the exact mathematical framework for computing statistical power and sample size requirements in one-sample and two-sample t-tests.

Experimental precautions and statistical checks

  • Do not compute sample variance or kurtosis for degrees of freedom ν ≤ 2, where theoretical variance is infinite.
  • Verify that Z and V are drawn from completely independent random number generator streams in the Gosset quotient.
  • Use logarithmic gamma functions (lgamma) when evaluating t-distribution densities to prevent arithmetic overflow.
  • Ensure sample sizes N ≥ 50,000 when benchmarking high-order tail quantiles (e.g., 99.5th percentile) of heavy-tailed distributions.

Theoretical assumptions & limitations

  • The t-distribution assumes underlying parent populations are normally distributed; severe skewness distorts small-sample t properties.
  • Extreme outliers in Cauchy-like data can dominate sample estimators and cause empirical variance metrics to fluctuate erratically.
