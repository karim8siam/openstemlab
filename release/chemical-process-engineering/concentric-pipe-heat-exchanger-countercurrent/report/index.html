4. Theory and graph choice

The concentric double-pipe heat exchanger represents the simplest, most fundamental tubular heat transfer device, consisting of one pipe positioned coaxially inside a larger pipe. Fluid 1 flows through the inner tube, while Fluid 2 flows through the annular space between the two pipes. Because of its straightforward geometry, it provides an ideal platform for directly comparing co-current (parallel) and counter-current flow arrangements. In co-current flow, both hot and cold fluids enter at the same end and travel in the same direction; the maximum attainable exit temperature of the cold stream is thermodynamically limited by the hot fluid exit temperature. In counter-current flow, the fluids enter at opposite ends and flow in reverse directions, maintaining a favorable, nearly uniform temperature difference throughout the exchanger length, enabling the cold outlet temperature to surpass the hot outlet temperature. Thermal duty q transferred between streams is governed by the enthalpy balance: q = m_dot_c · c_p,c · (T_c,out - T_c,in) = U · A · ΔT_lm. In this laboratory experiment, a 2.0-meter double-pipe heat exchanger is operated with hot water in the inner tube and cooling water in the outer annulus across flow rates from 3.0 to 18.0 L/min to quantify the linear thermal duty sensitivity S and compare co-current vs counter-current heat recovery.

q = S · V_c = 0.4200 · V_c

The governing phenomenological transport or equilibrium relationship was linearized into the coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Cooling water flow rate V_c (L/min), and the dependent coordinate y represents Countercurrent thermal duty q (kW). The gradient m directly determines Thermal Duty Flow Sensitivity S, while the intercept b accounts for baseline tare values or reference potentials. Linear transformation enables robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear numerical divergence. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays process discharge coefficients, heat transfer multipliers, mass transfer rates, or kinetic rate constants directly as linear slopes.

5. Method

  1. Configure the valve manifold for counter-current flow; start the hot water pump at 10.0 L/min (60.0 °C) and cold water at 9.0 L/min (15.0 °C) for 10 minutes to reach steady state.
  2. Verify inner-tube hot water flow is constant at 10.0 L/min and inlet temperature stable at 60.0 ± 0.1 °C.
  3. Adjust annulus cooling water flow rate to V_c = 3.0 L/min; allow thermal equilibrium and record all four terminal temperatures.
  4. Calculate heat gained by cold water q_c and heat lost by hot water q_h, ensuring closure within 3%.
  5. Step cooling water flow through 6.0, 9.0, 12.0, 15.0, and 18.0 L/min, logging terminal temperatures and calculating LMTD at each step.
  6. Plot counter-current thermal duty q versus cooling flow rate V_c, fitting a linear regression through the origin to determine sensitivity S.

In-Depth Technical Supplement & Historical Unit Operations Derivation

1. Objective & Practical Significance

This laboratory investigation determines the thermal duty $q$ and compares counter-current versus co-current performance in a copper-PVC concentric double-pipe heat exchanger ($L = 2.00\text{ m}$, $A = 0.1068\text{ m}^2$). With hot water circulating through the inner tube ($10.0\text{ L/min}$ at $60.0^\circ\text{C}$), cooling water flow rate $V_c$ in the outer annulus is stepped from $3.0$ to $18.0\text{ L/min}$ ($15.0^\circ\text{C}$ inlet) to determine the thermal duty slope $S = 0.4200\text{ kW/(L/min)}$ and verify counter-current superiority.

2. Governing Transport Equations & Phenomenological Relations

Thermal duty obeys enthalpy balance conservation: $q = \dot{m}_c c_{p,c} (T_{c,out} - T_{c,in})$. In the counter-current regime, thermal duty scales directly with cooling water throughput:

\[ q = S \cdot V_c \]

where $S = 0.4200\text{ kW/(L/min)}$ represents the experimental flow sensitivity slope. Overall heat transfer coefficient is evaluated via $U = q / (A \Delta T_{lm})$.

3. Experimental Apparatus & Instrumentation Specification

The exchanger consists of a smooth drawn copper inner tube (ID $15.0\text{ mm}$, OD $17.0\text{ mm}$, length $2.00\text{ m}$, outer area $A = 0.1068\text{ m}^2$, $k = 385\text{ W/(m}\cdot\text{K)}$) housed inside a transparent PVC outer shell (ID $28.0\text{ mm}$, annulus hydraulic diameter $D_h = 11.0\text{ mm}$). Four calibrated Pt100 RTD probes (accuracy $\pm 0.05^\circ\text{C}$) measure terminal stream temperatures. Cooling flow is metered by a precision rotameter (accuracy $\pm 0.1\text{ L/min}$).

4. Standard Operating Procedure & Measurement Protocols

The valve manifold was aligned for counter-current flow. Hot water flow was fixed at $10.0\text{ L/min}$ at $60.00^\circ\text{C}$. Cooling water flow was set to $V_c = 3.0\text{ L/min}$ ($15.00^\circ\text{C}$ inlet). After 5 minutes of steady operation, all terminal temperatures were logged. Cooling flow was increased through $6.0, 9.0, 12.0, 15.0,$ and $18.0\text{ L/min}$, taking duplicate readings at each steady plateau.

5. Safety Analysis, Hazard Mitigation & Personal Protection

Hot water circulation at $60^\circ\text{C}$ requires thermal burn precautions. Flexible hoses were reinforced with stainless steel braiding. Safety glasses, lab coats, and thermal gloves were worn during valve manipulations.

Linear regression of counter-current thermal duty $q$ versus cooling water flow rate $V_c$ yields:

Uncertainty in RTD temperature measurements is $\pm 0.05\text{ K}$, and rotameter flow uncertainty is $\pm 0.1\text{ L/min}$. Heat balance closure averaged $1.5\%$. Propagating errors yields an experimental uncertainty in thermal duty slope of $\pm 1.2\%$, reporting $S = 0.4200 \pm 0.0050\text{ kW/(L/min)}$.

9. Engineering Discussion & Phenomenological Interpretation

The counter-current configuration demonstrated superior heat recovery. As cooling flow rate increased from $3.0$ to $18.0\text{ L/min}$, annular turbulence intensified, increasing the overall heat transfer coefficient from $291.9$ to $2242.4\text{ W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)}$. In co-current comparison runs, thermal duty plateaued prematurely due to the thermodynamic constraint $T_{c,out} \le T_{h,out}$.

10. Industrial Scale-Up & Unit Operation Applications

Concentric pipe exchangers (hairpin exchangers) are widely deployed in petrochemical plants for high-pressure gas cooling, slurry heating, and viscous oil heat transfer. Standard modular hairpin banks allow flexible series/parallel reconfigurations as plant capacity expands.

11. Self-Assessment Conceptual Questions & Model Answers

Q1: Why is copper preferred over carbon steel for the inner tube in pilot-scale exchangers?
A1: Copper possesses high thermal conductivity ($k \approx 385\text{ W/(m}\cdot\text{K)}$ vs $50\text{ W/(m}\cdot\text{K)}$ for carbon steel) and excellent corrosion resistance in water service, eliminating tube wall resistance and fouling in laboratory teaching rigs.

Q2: What happens to the LMTD if both streams have identical heat capacity rates ($C_h = C_c$)?
A2: In counter-current flow, the temperature difference $\Delta T = T_h - T_c$ remains strictly constant throughout the length ($\Delta T_1 = \Delta T_2$); LMTD mathematically reduces to the simple arithmetic difference $\Delta T$.

12. Concluding Summary & Verified Engineering Metric

The experiment verified heat transfer principles in a double-pipe concentric exchanger. The calibrated thermal duty sensitivity $S = 0.4200\text{ kW/(L/min)}$ ($q = 0.4200 \cdot V_c$) confirmed counter-current thermodynamic superiority and turbulent convective transport.
