1. Title and aim

Micro-Irrigation Drip Emitter Flow vs Pressure & Manufacturing Variation (CV). Determine the discharge-pressure relationship, emitter discharge exponent x, and manufacturing coefficient of variation (CV) for agricultural drip irrigation emitters.

4. Theory and graph choice

Drip (trickle) irrigation represents the most water-efficient irrigation method in modern agriculture, applying small, frequent quantities of water directly to the crop root zone through labyrinthine emitters at near-ambient pressure. Emitter hydraulic behavior follows the power-law flow equation q = k·P^x (linearized as ln q = x·ln P + ln k), where q is emitter discharge (L/h), P is operating pressure (kPa), k is the emitter discharge coefficient, and x is the emitter discharge exponent. The exponent x characterizes pressure sensitivity: for fully turbulent non-compensating orifice emitters, x ≈ 0.50; for laminar long-path micro-tubes, x ≈ 1.00; while for pressure-compensating (PC) elastomeric diaphragm emitters, x approaches 0.0 (0.0 to 0.1), delivering constant discharge despite lateral elevation drops and friction losses. In accordance with ASABE EP405 and ISO 9261, emitter manufacturing variability is quantified by the coefficient of manufacturing variation CV = s_q / q_bar across 25 replicate emitters, which directly governs field Emission Uniformity EU = 100·(1 - 1.27·CV)·(q_min / q_bar). In this lab, an automated drip test manifold tests 25 inline emitters across pressures from 50 to 250 kPa.

ln(q) = x · ln(P) + ln(k) = 0.5000 · ln(P) - 0.7500

The governing physical relationship was linearized into the standard regression coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent axis x represents ln(Pressure P in kPa), and the dependent axis y represents ln(Discharge q in L/h). The gradient m directly determines Emitter Discharge Exponent x, while the intercept b accounts for zero-load offsets, baseline tares, or reference thresholds. Linear transformation allows robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear solver instability or convergence failure. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays soil resistance gradients, flow exponents, drying kinetics, or power conversion efficiencies directly as linear slopes. This linear graphical formulation provides an immediate visual check of homoscedasticity, linearity, and potential soil shear failure, flow cavitation, or saturation limits.

5. Method

  1. Flush the drip manifold with clean water through the end flush valve for 2.0 minutes, verifying that the 120-mesh disc filter is clean.
  2. Set the electronic regulator to P = 50 kPa (ln P = 3.9120), allowing all 25 emitters to discharge into waste troughs until steady flow is established.
  3. Position the 25 graduated catch cups under the emitters simultaneously for exactly 10.0 minutes, then remove them synchronously.
  4. Weigh each individual catch cup to calculate the discharge q_i (L/h) of each emitter, computing mean discharge q_bar and standard deviation s_q to find CV.
  5. Increment operating pressure across five additional stages: 75, 100, 140, 190, and 250 kPa (ln P up to 5.5215), repeating catch audits at each pressure.
  6. Plot ln(q_bar) versus ln(P), perform linear regression to determine the emitter flow exponent x and coefficient k, and compute Emission Uniformity EU.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Always operate with pre-filtered water; microscopic particles permanently lodge inside emitter labyrinths.
  • Ensure manifold lateral sits level so that static elevation heads across emitters remain identical.
  • Empty catch cups thoroughly between runs to prevent residual tare weight errors.
  • Verify that water temperature remains constant within ±1.5°C during pressure stepping.

Evaluates clean tap water; field irrigation water containing biological slimes, iron precipitates, or carbonates requires chemical chlorination and acid injection. Evaluated using a manifold of 25 non-compensating turbulent vortex inline emitters (nominal 4.0 L/h at 100 kPa) across pressures from 50 to 250 kPa.
