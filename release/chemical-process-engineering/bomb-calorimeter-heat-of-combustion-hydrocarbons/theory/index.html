Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Oxygen Bomb Calorimetry.

Oxygen bomb calorimetry is the gold standard thermochemical method in chemical, petroleum, and energy engineering for determining standard gross heats of combustion (higher heating values, HHV), internal energies of reaction (ΔU_c^°), and standard enthalpies of formation (ΔH_f^°) of fuels, polymers, foodstocks, and chemical feedstocks. In an isoperibol oxygen bomb calorimeter, a known mass of combustible sample is ignited electrically inside a thick-walled, high-strength stainless steel pressure vessel (bomb) charged with excess pure oxygen gas at 3.0 MPa (30 bar). Complete combustion transforms organic carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen exclusively into gaseous CO_2 and condensed liquid H_2O: C_a H_b O_c (s) + (a + b/4 - c/2) O_2 (g) -> a CO_2 (g) + (b/2) H_2O (l). The bomb is submerged in a precisely weighed bucket of deionized water (2000.0 g) enclosed within a thermostatically jacketed isoperibol air space. Heat released by combustion transfers through the bomb walls into the surrounding water and calorimeter hardware, inducing a temperature rise of 1 to 4 °C. Because the calorimeter is not perfectly adiabatic, heat exchange with the constant-temperature jacket occurs during the rating periods. The Regnault–Pfaundler radiation correction method accounts for this thermal leakage, yielding the corrected temperature rise ΔT_c. Applying the first law of thermodynamics to the closed constant-volume bomb system yields: -ΔU_c = W_cal · ΔT_c - e_fuse - e_acid, where W_cal is the energy equivalent (effective heat capacity) of the calorimeter system, e_fuse is the combustion energy of the nickel-chromium ignition wire, and e_acid is the thermochemical correction for nitric acid formed from residual nitrogen. Standardized benzoic acid pellets (certified gross heat of combustion -Δu_c = 26.434 kJ/g) are combusted across masses from 0.40 to 1.40 g to determine the specific combustion thermometric slope S = (-Δu_c) / W_cal = 2.6500 °C/g, validating the calorimeter energy equivalent W_cal = 9.975 kJ/°C (9975 J/K).

ΔT_c = S · m_s = 2.6500 · m_s

First Law of Thermodynamics & Constant-Volume Bomb Calorimetry

Bomb calorimetry measures the internal energy of combustion ($\Delta U_c$) of chemical substances under constant-volume conditions. Applying the First Law of Thermodynamics to the closed, rigid calorimeter bomb system ($d V = 0$):

\[ d U = \delta Q - \delta W = \delta Q - P \, d V = \delta Q_V \]

Because the bomb boundaries are rigid, expansion work is zero ($W = 0$). The total heat released by the combustion reaction ($Q_{\text{comb}}$) equals the internal energy change of the reacting system:

\[ -\Delta U_c = Q_{\text{comb}} = W_{\text{cal}} \, \Delta T_c - e_{\text{fuse}} - e_{\text{acid}} \]

where $W_{\text{cal}}$ is the effective energy equivalent (heat capacity) of the calorimeter system (bomb, bucket, water, stirrer, and thermometer), $\Delta T_c$ is the corrected temperature rise, $e_{\text{fuse}}$ is the combustion energy of the nickel-chromium ignition wire ($e_{\text{fuse}} = 9.6\text{ J/cm} \times L_{\text{burned}}$), and $e_{\text{acid}}$ is the thermochemical correction for nitric and sulfuric acid formation.

Regnault–Pfaundler Radiation Correction Method

In an isoperibol calorimeter, the jacket is maintained at constant temperature $T_j$. During the combustion process, heat exchanges with the jacket according to Newton's law of cooling:

\[ \frac{d T}{d t} = -k \, (T - T_j) + w_{\text{stir}} \]

where $k$ is the calorimeter cooling constant and $w_{\text{stir}}$ is the thermal dissipation from mechanical stirring. The Regnault–Pfaundler method evaluates the radiation leakage correction $\Delta T_{\text{rad}}$ across the reaction period from $t_a$ (ignition) to $t_b$ (start of steady post-period cooling):

\[ \Delta T_{\text{rad}} = k \, \int_{t_a}^{t_b} (T(t) - T_{\infty}) \, d t \] \[ \Delta T_c = T_b - T_a + \Delta T_{\text{rad}} \]

Linear Thermometric Scaling & Standard Heat of Combustion

For a standardized pure substance (such as benzoic acid, certified specific heat of combustion $-\Delta u_c = 26.434\text{ kJ/g}$), the corrected temperature rise scales directly with sample mass $m_s$:

\[ \Delta T_c = S \cdot m_s = \left( \frac{-\Delta u_c}{W_{\text{cal}}} \right) \cdot m_s \]

Linear regression of $\Delta T_c$ versus $m_s$ extracts the thermometric slope $S = 2.6500^\circ\text{C/g}$, from which the calorimeter energy equivalent is determined:

\[ W_{\text{cal}} = \frac{-\Delta u_c}{S} = \frac{26.434\text{ kJ/g}}{2.6500^\circ\text{C/g}} = 9.9751\text{ kJ/}^\circ\text{C} = 9975.1\text{ J/K} \]

The standard enthalpy of combustion $\Delta H_c^\circ$ at $298.15\text{ K}$ is related to $\Delta U_c^\circ$ through the gas stoichiometric mole change $\Delta n_g$:

\[ \Delta H_c^\circ = \Delta U_c^\circ + \Delta n_g \, R T \]

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

Why is 1.0 mL of deionized water added to the bomb cylinder before sealing?

The water saturates the interior vapor space, ensuring all water produced by combustion condenses completely into the liquid phase (releasing latent heat of vaporization) and provides an aqueous medium to dissolve and neutralize any nitric acid formed from trace nitrogen.

What is the difference between gross heat of combustion (HHV) and net heat of combustion (LHV)?

Gross heat of combustion (higher heating value, HHV) includes the latent heat of condensation of water formed during combustion because product water ends as liquid; net heat of combustion (lower heating value, LHV) assumes all product water remains in the vapor phase, subtracting the enthalpy of vaporization (2.442 MJ/kg H2O).

How does the Regnault–Pfaundler correction account for non-adiabatic calorimeter heat leakage?

The method integrates Newton's law of cooling across the reaction period using the measured cooling constants from the initial steady pre-period and final post-period, adding a calculated radiation correction ΔT_rad to the raw temperature rise ΔT_raw to yield the true adiabatic ΔT_c.

Why must the sample be compacted into a cohesive pellet rather than burned as a loose powder?

Loose fine powders tend to be blown out of the combustion crucible by the rapid rush of pressurized oxygen during charging or by the sudden expansion wave upon ignition, leading to incomplete combustion and unburned carbon soot.

How is the constant-volume internal energy of combustion ΔU_c converted to constant-pressure enthalpy of combustion ΔH_c?

Using the thermodynamic identity ΔH_c = ΔU_c + Δn_g · R T, where Δn_g = n_g(products) - n_g(reactants) is the net change in moles of gaseous species (moles of CO2 produced minus moles of O2 consumed).

Why must pure oxygen be charged to a high pressure of 3.0 MPa (30 bar)?

High oxygen pressure ensures a vast stoichiometric excess of O2 (typically 10 to 20 times the stoichiometric requirement), guaranteeing 100% complete combustion to CO2 and H2O without carbon monoxide (CO) or soot formation.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Never exceed 3.5 MPa oxygen charging pressure, and inspect bomb head threads and sealing O-rings before every pressurization.
  • Ensure the bomb is completely submerged in water to test for escaping gas bubbles before placing inside the calorimeter jacket.
  • Do not lean over the calorimeter during ignition; maintain electrical firing switch outside the protective blast zone.
  • Verify that ignition fuse wire does not touch the stainless steel capsule walls to prevent electrical short-circuiting.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (0.4 to 1.4 g).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
