Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of CSTR Cascade in Series.

In industrial chemical reaction engineering, connecting multiple continuous stirred-tank reactors in series (a CSTR cascade or tank battery) is one of the most effective strategies for overcoming the low conversion efficiency of a single backmix vessel while preserving excellent temperature control and slurry handling capabilities. In a single CSTR, feed is immediately diluted to the final product concentration, forcing the entire vessel to operate at the slowest reaction rate. In contrast, in a cascade of N equal-volume tanks in series, reactant concentration drops in discrete stages from tank to tank. The first tank operates at a high intermediate concentration and high reaction rate, the second at a moderate rate, and only the final tank operates at the exit concentration. Mathematically, as the number of stages N approaches infinity while total volume V_tot is held constant, the discrete staircase concentration profile approaches the smooth continuous exponential decay of an ideal plug-flow reactor (PFR). For an equimolar first-order or pseudo-first-order reaction (-r_A = k · C_A) conducted across N identical CSTRs each having individual space time τ_i = τ_tot / N, mass balances across each tank yield: C_Ai = C_A,i-1 / (1 + k · τ_i). Cascading across N stages gives: C_A0 / C_AN = (1 + k · τ_tot / N)^N. For a three-stage cascade (N = 3): (C_A0 / C_A3)^(1/3) = 1 + (k / 3) · τ_tot = S · τ_tot + 1.0. In this laboratory investigation, an instrumented three-tank CSTR cascade is operated across total space times from 6.0 to 36.0 min to measure inter-stage concentrations, verify the N-stage power law, and evaluate kinetic slope S = k / 3 = 0.0450 min^-1 (k = 0.1350 min^-1).

(C_A0 / C_A3)^(1/3) = S · τ_tot + 1.0000 = 0.0450 · τ_tot + 1.0000

1. Theory of Continuous Stirred-Tank Reactors in Series

Connecting multiple CSTRs in series combines the operational advantages of agitated vessels (uniform mixing, facile temperature control, solids suspension) with the high kinetic conversion efficiency of plug flow. Liquid flows sequentially from Tank 1 through Tank $N$ via gravity overflow.

2. Mathematical Derivation for a First-Order Cascade

Consider $N$ equal-volume CSTRs in series, each having volume $V_i = V_{tot} / N$ and space time $\tau_i = \tau_{tot} / N$. For a first-order irreversible reaction $-r_A = k C_A$, the steady-state material balance on tank $i$ is:

\[ v_0 C_{A,i-1} - v_0 C_{Ai} - k C_{Ai} V_i = 0 \implies C_{A,i-1} = C_{Ai} (1 + k \tau_i) \]

Solving for the concentration leaving stage $i$:

\[ C_{Ai} = \frac{C_{A,i-1}}{1 + k \tau_i} \]

Applying this recurrence relation across all $N$ stages from feed concentration $C_{A0}$ to exit concentration $C_{AN}$:

\[ \frac{C_{A0}}{C_{AN}} = (1 + k \tau_i)^N = \left( 1 + k \frac{\tau_{tot}}{N} \right)^N \]

3. Three-Stage Cascade Linear Regression Model

For a three-stage cascade ($N = 3$), taking the cube root of both sides:

\[ \left( \frac{C_{A0}}{C_{A3}} \right)^{1/3} = 1 + \frac{k}{3} \tau_{tot} = S \cdot \tau_{tot} + 1.0000 \]

where $S = k / 3$ is the cascade kinetic slope in $\text{min}^{-1}$. Plotting $(C_{A0} / C_{A3})^{1/3}$ versus total space time $\tau_{tot}$ yields a straight line with intercept $1.0000$ and slope $S$, from which the reaction rate constant $k$ is directly evaluated:

\[ k = 3 \cdot S \]

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

Why does a cascade of N CSTRs in series approach plug-flow behavior as N increases?

In a cascade, concentration drops in N smaller steps rather than one single drop; as N increases, the staircase concentration profile approaches a smooth continuous exponential decay curve, with the residence time distribution narrowing until it becomes a Dirac delta function as N -> ∞.

How does the total volume required for a 3-CSTR cascade compare to a single CSTR for 90% conversion?

For 90% conversion of a first-order reaction, a single CSTR requires k τ = 9.0; a 3-CSTR cascade requires k τ_tot = 3 [(10)^(1/3) - 1] ≈ 3.46, reducing total required reactor volume by more than 60%.

What is the tanks-in-series model for non-ideal flow in chemical reactors?

The tanks-in-series model characterizes non-ideal reactors by finding the equivalent number of ideal CSTRs in series N = t_mean^2 / σ^2 that reproduces the experimental residence time distribution variance σ^2.

Why are equal-volume CSTRs optimal for first-order reactions in series?

Mathematical differentiation of the overall conversion expression with respect to individual tank volumes demonstrates that equal-sized vessels minimize the total volume required for any specified conversion.

How can inter-stage cooling in an exothermic CSTR cascade optimize equilibrium conversion?

By cooling the process stream between reactors, temperature can be adjusted to follow the optimal temperature progression curve: high temperature in early tanks for fast kinetics, and low temperature in later tanks to shift exothermic equilibrium toward products.

What happens if one impeller in a 3-CSTR cascade fails during operation?

The failed stage loses macro-mixing, developing stagnant dead zones and severe short-circuiting; the effective active volume of that tank drops, reducing overall cascade conversion significantly.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Allow at least 3 to 4 cascade residence times (3τ_tot to 4τ_tot) after changing flow rate before recording data, as multi-tank dynamics have long settling times.
  • Verify that gravity overflow lines are clear and unobstructed to prevent liquid buildup and volume expansion in upstream tanks.
  • Ensure all three impellers operate at identical speeds (350 RPM) with stable vortex suppression.
  • Maintain uniform jacket temperatures across all three vessels.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (6.0 to 36.0 min).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
