FOLLOW THE METHOD

A reason behind every step.

Measure the steady-state radial temperature distribution across a cylindrical specimen of process insulation material under controlled electrical heating, verify Fourier's law in cylindrical coordinates, and determine thermal conductivity k. Standardized parameter settings: 20.0, 40.0, 60.0, 80.0, 100.0, 120.0 W.

01

Specimen Alignment & Guard Tuning

Install the cylindrical insulation sleeve over the central core; energize guard heaters and adjust differential thermocouples to read 0.0 °C to eliminate axial losses.

02

Water Jacket Cooling Initialization

Circulate cooling water through the outer jacket at 5.0 L/min to stabilize outer wall temperature at T_2 = 25.0 °C.

03

Low Power Steady-State Establishment

Set central heater power to Q_e = 20.0 W; allow 45 minutes for the low-conductivity material to reach thermal steady state (drift < 0.05 °C over 10 min).

04

Temperature Profile Logging

Record inner temperature T_1, outer temperature T_2, and intermediate radial temperatures to confirm logarithmic distribution.

05

Power Stepping Protocol

Step heater power through 40.0, 60.0, 80.0, 100.0, and 120.0 W, allowing full steady-state equilibration and recording ΔT at each level.

06

Conductivity Regression Analysis

Plot radial temperature difference ΔT versus heater power Q_e, extract the thermal resistance slope S, and compute material thermal conductivity k.

THEORY AND UNIT OPERATIONS CONTEXT

Process engineering background & physical principles.

In chemical process plants, cryogenic storage terminals, and steam distribution systems, thermal insulation materials—such as calcium silicate, mineral wool, expanded perlite, and cellular glass—are mandatory to minimize parasitic thermal losses, prevent personnel burn injuries, avoid pipeline freezing, and suppress condensation. Determining the thermal conductivity k of tubular process insulation is vital for accurate thermal design. In cylindrical geometries, heat flows radially outward from an internal coaxial heating element through an annular insulation sleeve of inner radius r_1, outer radius r_2, and length L. Applying Fourier's fundamental law of heat conduction in one-dimensional steady cylindrical coordinates yields: q_r = -k A(r) · (dT/dr) = -k (2π r L) · (dT/dr). Separating variables and integrating across the annular wall establishes the classic logarithmic temperature profile: T(r) = T_1 - (T_1 - T_2) · [ln(r/r_1) / ln(r_2/r_1)], and gives the steady radial heat flow rate: q_r = [2π k L / ln(r_2/r_1)] · (T_1 - T_2). In this laboratory experiment, an annular specimen of refractory process insulation is heated with an internal coaxial electrical element across power levels from 20 to 120 W with guarded end heaters to eliminate axial heat leakage. Measuring the radial temperature difference ΔT = T_1 - T_2 directly evaluates the thermal resistance slope S and determines the material thermal conductivity k.

ΔT = S · Q_e = 0.3850 · Q_e
