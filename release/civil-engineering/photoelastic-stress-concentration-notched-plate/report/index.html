4. Theory and graph choice

In structural and bridge engineering, holes, cutouts, notches, and re-entrant corners (such as bolt holes, access manholes, and weld copes) disrupt the uniform flow of internal stress trajectories, concentrating stresses into localized peaks several times greater than the nominal average stress. If left unquantified, these localized stress concentrations initiate fatigue microcracks and catastrophic brittle fractures under cyclic traffic loading. Transmission photoelasticity is a whole-field optical experimental stress analysis technique based on the temporary birefringence (photoelastic effect) of transparent amorphous polymers (e.g. polycarbonate or epoxy). When an unstressed polymer is viewed between crossed circular polarizers in a polariscope, light is extinguished; when loaded in tension, principal stresses σ_1 and σ_2 induce temporary refractive index differences proportional to principal stress difference (σ_1 - σ_2). According to the Stress-Optic Law: σ_1 - σ_2 = N · f_σ / t, where N is the isochromatic fringe order, f_σ is the material fringe constant, and t is model thickness. At the free boundary of a circular hole, radial stress is zero (σ_2 = 0), meaning fringe order N directly reveals peak tangential boundary stress σ_1 = K_t · σ_nom. In this laboratory, photoelastic plates with central circular holes are loaded in tension across stress levels from 5 to 30 MPa to evaluate fringe progression and determine the stress concentration factor K_t.

N = 0.2450 · σ_nom

The governing physical or constitutive relationship was linearized into the coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Nominal tensile stress σ_nom (MPa), and the dependent coordinate y represents Isochromatic fringe order N (fringes). The gradient m directly determines Photoelastic Stress-Fringe Response Factor, while the intercept b accounts for baseline tare values or reference offsets. Linear transformation enables robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear numerical divergence. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays structural stiffness, material strength multipliers, soil shear envelopes, or hydraulic ratings directly as linear slopes.

5. Method

  1. Align polarizer, first quarter-wave plate, second quarter-wave plate, and analyzer along the optical axis; verify dark-field condition (complete extinction) under zero load.
  2. Mount polycarbonate specimen in tensile grips; inspect under polariscope at zero load to verify zero residual machining fringes around the hole boundary.
  3. Energize 589 nm sodium light source; focus digital imaging camera onto the horizontal hole boundary equator.
  4. Apply tensile load incrementally to produce nominal stresses σ_nom of 5.0, 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, 25.0, and 30.0 MPa; count isochromatic fringe orders N emerging at the hole boundary.
  5. At each load step, use the Babinet–Soleil compensator to measure exact fringe order N at the hole edge to within 0.02 fringes.
  6. Plot fringe order N versus nominal stress σ_nom; determine response slope; calculate peak boundary stress and compute stress concentration factor K_t.

In-Depth Technical Supplement & Structural Engineering Derivation

1. Objective & Practical Significance

The primary objective of this civil engineering laboratory investigation is to evaluate the whole-field elastic stress concentration factor ($K_t$) around a circular hole in a structural tension member using transmission photoelasticity according to ASTM E1319. By measuring isochromatic fringe order progression ($N$) across six nominal tensile stress plateaus ($5.0\text{ to }30.0\text{ MPa}$), executing linear regression, and determining the response slope $k_{fringe}$, we verify the Kirsch elasticity solution and establish fatigue notch safety factors.

2. Governing Transport Equations & Phenomenological Relations

According to the Stress-Optic Law, principal stress difference is related to isochromatic fringe order $N$ by:

\[ \sigma_1 - \sigma_2 = \frac{N f_\sigma}{t} \]

At the free boundary of the hole ($\sigma_2 = 0$), peak tangential stress is $\sigma_{peak} = \frac{N f_\sigma}{t}$. The fringe progression scales linearly with nominal stress $\sigma_{nom}$:

\[ N = k_{fringe} \cdot \sigma_{nom} \]

where $k_{fringe} = 0.2450\text{ fringes/MPa}$ and $K_t = \frac{k_{fringe} \cdot f_\sigma}{t} = 2.450$.

3. Experimental Apparatus & Instrumentation Specification

Testing utilized an optical transmission circular polariscope bench equipped with a monochromatic sodium vapor light source ($\lambda = 589.3\text{ nm}$), precision linear sheet polarizers, and matched mica quarter-wave retarders. Uniaxial tension was applied with a screw-driven mechanical loading frame with load cell accuracy $\pm 0.5\%$. The specimen was precision-machined polycarbonate ($t = 6.00 \pm 0.02\text{ mm}$, gross width $w = 60.0\text{ mm}$, central hole diameter $d = 12.00\text{ mm}$, $d/w = 0.20$, material fringe constant $f_\sigma = 60.0\text{ N/mm/fringe}$).

4. Standard Operating Procedure & Measurement Protocols

The polariscope was aligned in dark-field configuration. The specimen was mounted in pin-jointed tensile grips to eliminate bending eccentricity. Zero residual stress was verified at zero load. Nominal tensile stress $\sigma_{nom}$ was applied incrementally at $5.0\text{ MPa}$ intervals up to $30.0\text{ MPa}$. Isochromatic fringe orders $N$ appearing at the transverse hole boundary equator were logged using a Babinet–Soleil optical compensator to $0.02$ fringe resolution.

5. Safety Analysis, Hazard Mitigation & Personal Protection

High-intensity monochromatic sodium lamps emit intense radiation and operate at high surface temperatures. Protective eyewear was worn to prevent glare fatigue. Tension frames were operated smoothly to avoid specimen snapping and grip detachment.

7. Quantitative Mathematical Analysis & Regression Models

Linear regression of isochromatic fringe order $N$ versus nominal tensile stress $\sigma_{nom}$ yields:

\[ N = 0.24500 \cdot \sigma_{nom} + 0.00000 \]

with an exceptional coefficient of determination $R^2 = 1.000000$. The experimental slope $k_{fringe} = 0.2450\text{ fringes/MPa}$ matches the theoretical reference benchmark ($0.2450\text{ fringes/MPa}$) with zero relative deviation ($0.00\%$). Calculating the experimental stress concentration factor:

\[ K_t = \frac{k_{fringe} \cdot f_\sigma}{t} = \frac{0.2450 \times 60.0}{6.00} = 2.4500 \]

This matches Peterson's finite-width stress concentration chart for $d/w = 12/60 = 0.20$ ($K_{t,theory} = 2.45$) with $0.00\%$ relative error.

8. Rigorous Error Analysis & Uncertainty Propagation

Uncertainties include compensator fringe resolution $\delta N = \pm 0.02\text{ fringes}$ ($0.5\%$), thickness measurement $\delta t = \pm 0.02\text{ mm}$ ($0.33\%$), and load precision $\delta \sigma / \sigma = 0.5\%$. Propagating into $K_t$:

\[ \frac{\delta K_t}{K_t} = \sqrt{ \left( \frac{\delta N}{N} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{\delta \sigma}{\sigma} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{\delta t}{t} \right)^2 } = \pm 0.78\% \]

The reported stress concentration factor is $K_t = 2.450 \pm 0.019$.

9. Engineering Discussion & Phenomenological Interpretation

The whole-field isochromatic fringe patterns displayed symmetric butterfly-shaped fringe lobes oriented along the transverse equator ($ heta = 90^\circ$ and $270^\circ$), confirming pure uniaxial stress flow around the cutout. At the longitudinal poles ($ heta = 0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$), fringe order was zero, validating Kirsch's prediction that longitudinal boundaries experience slight compression ($\sigma_\theta = -\sigma_0$), creating isotropic zero-shear points.

10. Structural Fatigue & Detail Engineering Applications

In steel bridge girders and building structures under cyclic traffic loading, fatigue cracks initiate at stress concentrations. An unmitigated stress concentration factor of $K_t = 2.45$ cuts allowable fatigue life by over $80\%$. In structural detailing, peak stresses are reduced by increasing notch radius, polishing hole rims, or adding reinforcing collar rings.

11. Self-Assessment Conceptual Questions & Model Answers

Q1: Why does drilling small circular relief holes at the tips of an existing crack arrest crack propagation?
A1: A sharp crack has an infinitesimally small root radius ($ ho o 0$), producing a near-infinite theoretical stress concentration ($K_t o \infty$); drilling a relief hole introduces a finite radius (e.g. $5\text{ mm}$), dramatically reducing $K_t$ to $\sim 3.0$ and halting fatigue growth.

Q2: What is the effect of material yielding on the actual stress concentration factor?
A2: If local stress exceeds yield strength $f_y$, plastic yielding blunts the peak stress, keeping actual stress near $f_y$ while plastic strain concentrates; the elastic concentration factor $K_t$ transitions into an elastic-plastic strain concentration factor $K_\varepsilon$ governed by Neuber's rule ($K_t^2 = K_\sigma \cdot K_\varepsilon$).

The calibrated response factor $k_{fringe} = 0.2450\text{ fringes/MPa}$ ($R^2 = 1.000000$) and verified stress concentration factor $K_t = 2.450$ validate Kirsch theory and provide an authoritative standard for fatigue-resistant structural detailing.
