Probabilistic foundations of Bayesian Conjugate Updating.

Bayesian inference provides a coherent, axiomatic framework for updating probabilistic beliefs in the light of accumulating empirical evidence. Founded on Bayes' Theorem and developed into modern data science through conjugate analysis, the methodology represents uncertainty directly via probability distributions over parameter spaces. In this laboratory, we investigate Gaussian conjugate updating, evaluate precision-weighted posterior compromises, explore Bayes factors, verify the Bernstein-von Mises asymptotic convergence, and confirm the linear precision accumulation gradient m = 1.000. In mathematical statistics, empirical data science, and modern computational engineering, understanding the asymptotic sampling properties and theoretical behavior of Bayes' Theorem, Prior Precision, and Normal-Normal Conjugate Updating provides crucial guarantees. Rigorous experimental validation connects foundational probability laws with observable empirical phenomena, ensuring that statistical inference remains valid under finite sample sizes and real-world data collection constraints.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How is Bayes' Theorem formulated in continuous probability density modeling?

Bayes' Theorem (Thomas Bayes, 1763; Pierre-Simon Laplace, 1774) updates the probability distribution of an unknown parameter θ in light of observed empirical data y: p(θ | y) = [p(y | θ) p(θ)] / p(y) = [L(θ; y) p(θ)] / [∫ L(θ; y) p(θ) dθ]. In proportional form: Posterior ∝ Likelihood × Prior. The prior p(θ) represents uncertainty before observing data; the likelihood L(θ; y) represents the probability of the data given θ; and the posterior p(θ | y) represents the revised belief distribution after incorporating empirical evidence. The denominator p(y) is the marginal likelihood (evidence).

What is precision in Gaussian distributions, and why does it simplify Bayesian updating?

In statistics, precision τ is defined as the reciprocal of the variance: τ = 1 / σ^2. While variances combine non-linearly under Bayesian updating, precisions are strictly additive. For a normal prior θ ~ N(μ_0, 1/τ_0) and Gaussian data y_1, ..., y_n ~ i.i.d. N(θ, 1/τ_data), the likelihood precision is τ_L = n τ_data = n / σ^2. The posterior precision is simply the algebraic sum of prior precision and data precision: τ_post = τ_0 + n τ_data. This additive simplicity makes precision the natural metric in Bayesian analysis.

How is the Normal-Normal conjugate posterior mean formulated as a weighted average?

Under conjugate Normal-Normal updating, the posterior mean μ_post is a precision-weighted convex combination of the prior mean μ_0 and the sample mean x̄: μ_post = (τ_0 μ_0 + n τ_data x̄) / (τ_0 + n τ_data) = [τ_0 / τ_post] μ_0 + [n τ_data / τ_post] x̄. When sample size n is small (n → 0), data precision is low and the posterior mean is dominated by prior belief μ_0. As sample size grows large (n → ∞), the data precision overwhelms the prior (n τ_data / τ_post → 1), causing the posterior mean to converge asymptotically to the sample mean x̄.

What is a conjugate prior family, and what are classical conjugate pairs?

A prior distribution family is conjugate to a likelihood function if the posterior distribution belongs to the exact same parametric family as the prior. Classical conjugate pairs include: (1) Normal-Normal: Gaussian prior on mean with known variance yields Gaussian posterior; (2) Beta-Binomial: Beta prior on success probability yields Beta posterior; (3) Gamma-Poisson: Gamma prior on arrival rate yields Gamma posterior; (4) Dirichlet-Multinomial: Dirichlet prior on categorical probabilities yields Dirichlet posterior; (5) Normal-Inverse-Gamma: conjugate family when both mean and variance are unknown.

What are informative, non-informative, and objective priors?

Priors are categorized by their information content: (1) Informative priors: incorporate strong historical, physical, or experimental data, having large precision τ_0; (2) Non-informative (flat or diffuse) priors: express maximum ignorance by spreading probability mass widely (e.g., N(0, 10^6) with τ_0 ≈ 0), allowing data to dictate the posterior completely; (3) Objective priors (such as Jeffreys prior p(θ) ∝ √I(θ)): derived mathematically to remain invariant under arbitrary coordinate reparameterizations.

What is a Bayes Factor, and how does it facilitate Bayesian hypothesis testing?

Harold Jeffreys introduced the Bayes Factor BF_{10} to compare two competing models M_0 and M_1: BF_{10} = p(y | M_1) / p(y | M_0) = [∫ L_1(θ_1; y) p_1(θ_1) dθ_1] / [∫ L_0(θ_0; y) p_0(θ_0) dθ_0]. It represents the ratio of marginal likelihoods, quantifying how much the empirical data shifts the prior odds in favor of M_1: Posterior Odds = Bayes Factor × Prior Odds. Jeffreys' scale of evidence classifies BF > 3 as moderate evidence, BF > 10 as strong evidence, and BF > 100 as decisive evidence.

How does the Bernstein-von Mises Theorem bridge Bayesian and Frequentist inference?

The Bernstein-von Mises Theorem (also called the Bayesian Central Limit Theorem) proves that under mild regularity conditions, as sample size n → ∞, the posterior distribution p(θ | y) becomes asymptotically normal: p(θ | y) → N(θ̂_MLE, I_n(θ̂)^(-1)), completely independent of the choice of prior distribution (provided the prior does not assign zero probability to the true parameter). Consequently, in large samples, Bayesian 95% credible intervals and Frequentist 95% confidence intervals become mathematically identical.

Experimental precautions and statistical checks

  • Ensure prior precision τ_0 > 0 is strictly positive to prevent improper singular Gaussian priors.
  • Verify that data observation noise variance is known before using simple Normal-Normal updating.
  • Distinguish clearly between Bayesian credible intervals (probability of parameter) and frequentist confidence intervals.
  • Check that prior distributions do not assign zero probability density to realistic parameter values (Cromwell's rule).

Theoretical assumptions & limitations

  • In small-sample regimes, strongly informative subjective priors can bias posterior estimates away from true empirical reality.
  • Analytical conjugate updating is restricted to exponential family likelihoods; complex models require MCMC simulation.
