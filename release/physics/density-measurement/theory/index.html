Density of solids theory

Understand density of solids: the equations, assumptions, supporting concepts and measurement method.

01Definition and physical significance of density

Density ρ represents the spatial concentration of mass within a substance, defined as mass divided by volume (ρ = M/V). In the SI system, density is expressed in kilograms per cubic meter (kg m⁻³), though grams per cubic centimeter (g cm⁻³) is widely used in laboratory practice (1 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³). Density is an intensive thermodynamic property that characterizes the purity, composition, and crystal lattice packing of materials.

ρ = rac{M}{V}
Why is density considered an intensive property rather than an extensive property?

An intensive property does not depend on the quantity or spatial extent of the sample. When a homogeneous block of copper is halved, both its mass and its volume are halved, leaving their quotient ρ = M/V strictly invariant.

02Linear regression model for multiple specimens

When measuring a family of specimens of identical chemical composition but differing sizes, mass M scales proportionally with volume V according to M = ρV. Fitting an empirical straight line M = mV + b by ordinary least squares (OLS) allows the slope m to represent the best estimate of density ρ. The empirical intercept b serves as an experimental diagnostic: in an ideal measurement free from balance tare errors or systematic buoyancy offsets, b equals zero.

What physical meaning does a non-zero intercept b indicate in an experimental density plot?

A non-zero intercept indicates a systematic tare error in the balance, a persistent zero offset on the volumetric scale, or an uncorrected tare mass such as the suspension thread.

03Archimedes’ principle and displacement methods

When a solid body is wholly or partially immersed in a fluid at rest, it experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the body: F_b = ρ_f V g. In liquid displacement volumetry, the volume of liquid displaced equals the volume of the immersed solid provided no air bubbles cling to the surface and the solid is non-porous and insoluble in the fluid.

How does air bubble entrapment on the submerged solid affect the measured density?

Entrapped air bubbles increase the apparent displaced volume without adding significant mass, leading to an overestimation of volume and a systematic underestimation of the calculated solid density.

04Buoyancy correction in air weighings

Weighing an object in air yields an apparent mass M_app that is slightly less than its true vacuum mass M_vac due to the buoyant lift exerted by the surrounding atmospheric air: M_vac = M_app (1 - ρ_air / ρ_weights) / (1 - ρ_air / ρ_solid). For copper (ρ ≈ 8960 kg m⁻³) weighed with brass weights in air (ρ_air ≈ 1.2 kg m⁻³), this correction is on the order of 0.01%, which is negligible for routine teaching measurements but essential in high-precision metrology.

Under what condition would an object weigh exactly the same in air as in a vacuum?

An object would have identical apparent and vacuum weights only if the density of the object equaled the density of the reference balance weights, making the two buoyant forces cancel perfectly.

05Geometric volume calculation vs fluid displacement

For regular solids such as cylinders, volume can be computed geometrically from linear dimensions: V = π d² h / 4. Caliper measurement avoids surface tension meniscus errors and water absorption, but requires accurate cylindrical geometry without taper or surface flaws. Discrepancies between geometric volume and displacement volume often reveal internal voids, porosity, or surface irregularities.

V_{ ext{cylinder}} = rac{\pi d^2 h}{4}
Which method is preferred for determining the volume of an irregularly shaped mineral sample?

Fluid displacement (Archimedes method) is preferred for irregular shapes because geometric formulae cannot accurately accommodate arbitrary surfaces and contours.

06Temperature dependence of density and thermal expansion

As the temperature of a solid increases, thermal expansion causes its volume to increase according to V(T) = V_0 (1 + 3α ΔT), where α is the linear thermal expansion coefficient. Because mass is conserved, density decreases inversely: ρ(T) ≈ ρ_0 (1 - 3α ΔT). For copper with α ≈ 1.65 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹, a temperature change of 10 °C alters density by only about 0.05%, confirming that room-temperature thermal expansion introduces negligible error in standard student experiments.

Why should density values in standard reference tables always be accompanied by the measurement temperature?

Because thermal expansion changes volume while mass remains constant, density varies with temperature; specifying temperature ensures reproducibility and fair comparison against standard physical tables.

07Uncertainty propagation in density determinations

For a single specimen measured directly by ρ = M/V, the relative uncertainty satisfies (u_ρ / ρ)² = (u_M / M)² + (u_V / V)². In practice, volumetric measurement with a graduated cylinder typically exhibits a relative uncertainty of 1% to 2%, whereas mass measurement on a balance has an uncertainty below 0.05%. Consequently, the uncertainty in volume dominates the overall error budget of the density determination.

Why does multi-point linear regression provide a more reliable density value than averaging single-specimen quotients?

Multi-point regression pools evidence over a broad dynamic range, reduces random reading noise through least-squares filtering, and isolates any zero-point systematic tare error into the intercept parameter b.
