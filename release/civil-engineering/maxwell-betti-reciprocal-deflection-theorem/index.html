FOLLOW THE METHOD

A reason behind every step.

Verify the Maxwell–Betti reciprocal deflection theorem on an elastic simply supported beam by comparing deflection at station A due to load at station B (δ_AB) with deflection at station B due to load at station A (δ_BA). Standardized parameter settings: 5.0, 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, 25.0 kN.

01

Beam Station Layout & LVDT Mounting

Set beam clear span L = 1000.0 mm; mark Station A at x_A = 300.0 mm and Station B at x_B = 600.0 mm; position digital LVDTs at stations A and B; zero both gauges.

02

Case 1 Loading: Load at Station B, Measure at Station A

Position loading jack at Station B (x_B = 600 mm); apply loads P_B incrementally at 5.0, 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, and 25.0 kN.

03

Deflection Logging for Case 1

At each load step, record deflection at Station A (δ_AB) and direct deflection at Station B (δ_BB); release load completely.

04

Transducer Verification & Re-Zeroing

Verify both transducers return to zero (residual deflection < 0.005 mm) confirming purely elastic response.

05

Case 2 Loading: Load at Station A, Measure at Station B

Move loading jack to Station A (x_A = 300 mm); apply identical loads P_A at 5.0, 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, and 25.0 kN.

06

Deflection Logging & Reciprocal Comparison

Record deflection at Station B (δ_BA) and direct deflection at Station A (δ_AA); compare δ_AB with δ_BA at every load step to verify reciprocity.

THEORY AND STRUCTURAL CONTEXT

Civil engineering background & physical principles.

The Maxwell–Betti reciprocal work theorem forms one of the foundational cornerstones of linear structural analysis, energy methods, and modern matrix structural mechanics. Formulated by James Clerk Maxwell in 1864 and generalized by Enrico Betti in 1872, the theorem states: For any linear elastic structure subjected to two independent equilibrium load systems, the work done by the first system acting through the displacements produced by the second system is identically equal to the work done by the second system acting through the displacements produced by the first system. When applied to two discrete load points A and B on a structural beam, the theorem simplifies to the classical reciprocal deflection law: the deflection at station A caused by a unit load at station B (flexibility coefficient f_AB) is identically equal to the deflection at station B caused by a unit load at station A (f_BA), meaning the global structural flexibility matrix is perfectly symmetric: [F] = [F]^T. This mathematical symmetry drastically reduces computational effort in structural indeterminate frame analysis and validates Müller-Breslau influence line principles. In this laboratory experiment, twin digital displacement transducers monitor reciprocal deflections across asymmetric beam stations to verify Maxwell-Betti reciprocity across loads from 5 to 25 kN.
