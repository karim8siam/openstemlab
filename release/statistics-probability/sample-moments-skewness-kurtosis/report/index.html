3. Apparatus and fixed settings

  • Pseudo-Random Variate Engine: Generates 50,000 independent identically distributed uniform random draws using the 64-bit Mersenne Twister algorithm.
  • Raw Moment Accumulator: Computes sample raw moments m_k' = (1/N) ∑ X_i^k with compensated Kahan summation to prevent numerical cancellation.
  • Central Moment Transformer: Applies binomial expansion transformations to compute central moments μ_k = E[(X - μ)^k] from raw sample moments.
  • Fisher-Pearson Skewness Analyzer: Evaluates standard sample skewness g_1 = m_3 / (m_2)^(3/2) to detect directional distribution asymmetry.
  • Excess Kurtosis Meter: Computes standardized fourth central moment g_2 = [m_4 / (m_2)^2] - 3 to measure tail heaviness relative to Gaussian mesokurtosis.
  • Ordinary Least-Squares Regression Engine: Performs linear regression of inverse expectation versus moment order to verify theoretical moment recurrence.
  • Uniform distribution U[0, 1] baseline
  • Sample size N = 50,000 Monte Carlo variates

4. Theory and graph choice

In mathematical statistics, moments provide a quantitative framework for characterizing the probability distribution of a random variable. The first raw moment represents the expectation or central location; the second central moment represents the variance or dispersion; the third standardized moment quantifies skewness or asymmetry; and the fourth standardized moment quantifies kurtosis or tail weight. In this laboratory, we employ pseudo-random Monte Carlo sampling to compute empirical moments across standardized distribution domains, test the numerical stability of moment update recursions, and rigorously verify the theoretical moment sequence of continuous distributions. In mathematical statistics, empirical data science, and modern computational engineering, understanding the asymptotic sampling properties and theoretical behavior of Sample Moments, Variance, Skewness, and Kurtosis provides crucial guarantees. Rigorous experimental validation connects foundational probability laws with observable empirical phenomena, ensuring that statistical inference remains valid under finite sample sizes and real-world data collection constraints.

5. Method

  1. Set the pseudo-random generator seed to 42 and allocate a continuous floating-point memory buffer for 50,000 independent samples drawn from the standard uniform distribution U[0, 1].
  2. Set moment order k = 1. Accumulate the arithmetic sample mean m_1' = (1/N) ∑ X_i and record the expectation value on the digital measurement dashboard.
  3. Center all sample observations by subtracting the arithmetic mean (X_i - x̄) and evaluate the second central moment m_2, third central moment m_3, and fourth central moment m_4.
  4. Compute Fisher-Pearson standardized sample skewness g_1 and excess kurtosis g_2, logging the resulting empirical coefficients alongside their asymptotic standard errors.
  5. Increment the power moment order k across standardized integer steps k ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, observing that for U[0, 1], theoretical expectation E[X^k] = 1/(k+1), such that 1/E[X^k] = k + 1.
  6. Log all six empirical trials into the digital laboratory notebook. Fit the linear regression model y = mx + b on transformed coordinates (x = k, y = 1/E[X^k]) to confirm unit slope m = 1.000.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Utilize compensated Kahan summation when accumulating powers of data values to eliminate round-off drift.
  • Verify that sample size N ≥ 10,000 when estimating sample kurtosis to ensure asymptotic consistency.
  • Never apply naive variance formulas S = ∑ X^2 - (∑ X)^2/N to large offset data without mean centering.
  • Perform replication across multiple independent pseudorandom seeds to verify that estimator variance and bias bounds are invariant to initial generator states.

Higher-order moments (k ≥ 4) have large sampling variance and are volatile in small datasets. The empirical data validates the theoretical moment sequence E[X^k] = 1/(k+1) for uniform distributions, yielding an exact linear relationship y = 1.000x + 1.000 with R² = 1.000000.
