Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of CSTR Saponification Kinetics.

In chemical reaction engineering, continuous stirred-tank reactors (CSTRs or backmix reactors) are widely utilized for liquid-phase synthesis, polymerization, neutralization, and bioprocess fermentation. Operating under steady state with intense mechanical agitation, an ideal CSTR exhibits perfect spatial macro-mixing, meaning temperature, composition, and reaction rates throughout the reactor volume are uniform and identical to the exiting effluent stream. The benchmark model reaction in chemical engineering kinetics is the liquid-phase saponification of ethyl acetate with sodium hydroxide: CH3COOC2H5 + NaOH -> CH3COONa + C2H5OH. At equimolar feed concentrations (C_A0 = C_B0 = 0.050 mol/L), the reaction exhibits second-order irreversible kinetics: -r_A = k · C_A · C_B = k · C_A0^2 · (1 - X_A)^2. A steady-state species conservation balance over reactor volume V with total volumetric feed flow rate v_0 yields: V · (-r_A) = v_0 · C_A0 · X_A. Introducing the reactor space time τ = V / v_0 (the time required to process one reactor volume of feed) yields: k · C_A0 · τ = X_A / (1 - X_A)^2. Reaction progress is tracked in real-time by measuring solution electrical conductivity, which decreases as high-mobility hydroxide ions (OH-) are replaced by lower-mobility acetate ions (CH3COO-). In this laboratory investigation, an instrumented 2.0-liter glass CSTR is operated across space times from 2.0 to 12.0 min to measure conversion X_A and determine the kinetic slope S = k · C_A0 = 0.2800 min^-1 (k = 5.600 L/(mol·min)).

X_A / (1 - X_A)^2 = S · τ = 0.2800 · τ

1. Chemical Reaction Engineering & The Ideal CSTR Model

Continuous stirred-tank reactors (CSTRs) are central to industrial chemical synthesis. Assuming steady state, constant fluid density, and ideal macro-mixing (perfect micro-homogeneity), the fundamental species conservation equation for reactant $A$ over reactor volume $V$ is:

\[ F_{A,in} - F_{A,out} + r_A V = \frac{dN_A}{dt} = 0 \]

Expressing molar flow rates in terms of volumetric flow $v_0$ and fractional conversion $X_A$ ($F_{A,in} = v_0 C_{A0}$, $F_{A,out} = v_0 C_{A0} (1 - X_A)$):

\[ v_0 C_{A0} - v_0 C_{A0} (1 - X_A) + r_A V = 0 \implies V (-r_A) = v_0 C_{A0} X_A \]

Defining reactor space time $\tau = V / v_0$ (the hydrodynamic holding time):

\[ \tau = \frac{C_{A0} X_A}{-r_A} \]

2. Second-Order Saponification Kinetics

The alkaline hydrolysis of ethyl acetate with sodium hydroxide:

\[ \text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5 + \text{NaOH} \longrightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \]

follows irreversible second-order kinetics: $-r_A = k C_A C_B$. For equimolar feeds ($C_{A0} = C_{B0}$), $C_A = C_B = C_{A0} (1 - X_A)$, yielding:

\[ -r_A = k C_{A0}^2 (1 - X_A)^2 \]

Substituting into the CSTR performance equation:

\[ \tau = \frac{C_{A0} X_A}{k C_{A0}^2 (1 - X_A)^2} = \frac{X_A}{k C_{A0} (1 - X_A)^2} \]

Rearranging into linear calibration form:

\[ \frac{X_A}{(1 - X_A)^2} = (k C_{A0}) \cdot \tau = S \cdot \tau \]

where $S = k C_{A0}$ is the kinetic slope in $\text{min}^{-1}$. Plotting $X_A / (1 - X_A)^2$ versus space time $\tau$ yields a straight line through the origin, from which the second-order rate constant $k$ is directly evaluated:

\[ k = \frac{S}{C_{A0}} \]

3. Conductimetric Reaction Tracking

Because ionic conductivity $\kappa$ is linear with species concentration (Kohlrausch's law), fractional conversion is evaluated directly from electrical conductivity without chemical quenching:

\[ X_A = \frac{\kappa_0 - \kappa}{\kappa_0 - \kappa_\infty} \]

where $\kappa_0$ is unreacted feed conductivity and $\kappa_\infty$ is full conversion conductivity.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How does electrical conductivity enable real-time tracking of saponification conversion?

The reaction replaces highly mobile hydroxide ions (limiting molar conductivity λ_OH ≈ 198 S·cm^2/mol) with bulkier, slower-moving acetate ions (λ_CH3COO ≈ 41 S·cm^2/mol); because sodium ions remain spectator cations, solution electrical conductivity decreases linearly with conversion.

What is the difference between space time τ and mean residence time t_mean in an ideal CSTR?

Space time τ = V / v_0 is the time required to process one reactor volume of feed; for constant-density liquid systems, space time equals the mean residence time t_mean.

Why does a CSTR require a larger volume than a PFR to achieve the same conversion for positive-order reactions?

In an ideal CSTR, the reaction takes place entirely at the lowest concentration (the exit concentration); for positive-order kinetics, this produces the minimum possible reaction rate throughout the vessel, requiring a larger reactor volume than a PFR where concentration starts high.

How do baffles inside a stirred reactor prevent vortex formation and air entrainment?

Vertical wall baffles (typically 4 baffles of width W = D/10) convert circumferential swirling flow into axial and radial circulation patterns, eliminating deep central surface vortices that would ingest air bubbles and degrade mixing.

What is the Damköhler number (Da) for a second-order reaction in a CSTR?

The Damköhler number Da_II = k · C_A0 · τ characterizes the ratio of characteristic reaction rate to convective flow rate; high Da (> 5) yields high conversion, while low Da (< 0.1) results in minimal conversion.

How does non-ideal mixing (such as dead zones or short-circuiting) affect CSTR conversion?

Dead zones reduce effective active reactor volume, while short-circuiting bypasses feed directly to the exit; both non-idealities degrade conversion below theoretical ideal CSTR predictions.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Sodium hydroxide solutions are alkaline; handle concentrated pellets with chemical splash goggles and nitrile gloves.
  • Ensure feed pumps are calibrated together to maintain an exact 1:1 volumetric stoichiometric ratio.
  • Allow at least 3 residence times (3τ) after altering pump speeds before recording steady-state conductivity.
  • Maintain vigorous impeller agitation (400 RPM) to ensure perfect macro-mixing.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (2.0 to 12.0 min).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
