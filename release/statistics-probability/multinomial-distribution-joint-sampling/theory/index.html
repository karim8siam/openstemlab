Probabilistic foundations of Multinomial Joint Sampling.

The Multinomial distribution is the fundamental multivariate probability model for categorical data analysis, discrete choice modeling, natural language processing, and genetics. Generalizing the binomial model to K > 2 discrete outcomes, it introduces intrinsic negative correlation among competing categories due to fixed sample size constraints. In this laboratory, we simulate categorical sampling, verify marginal binomial behavior, analyze the singular covariance matrix, and quantitatively confirm the pairwise covariance gradient m = 0.060. In mathematical statistics, empirical data science, and modern computational engineering, understanding the asymptotic sampling properties and theoretical behavior of Multinomial Distribution, Categorical Probabilities, and Covariance Structure provides crucial guarantees. Rigorous experimental validation connects foundational probability laws with observable empirical phenomena, ensuring that statistical inference remains valid under finite sample sizes and real-world data collection constraints.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How is the multivariate Multinomial distribution defined mathematically?

The Multinomial distribution is the multi-category generalization of the Binomial distribution. Suppose n independent trials are conducted, where each trial results in exactly one of K mutually exclusive outcomes with probabilities p_1, p_2, ..., p_K satisfying p_i ≥ 0 and ∑_{i=1}^K p_i = 1. Let X_i denote the number of times outcome i occurs, such that ∑_{i=1}^K X_i = n. The joint probability mass function is P(X_1 = x_1, ..., X_K = x_K) = (n! / (x_1! x_2! ··· x_K!)) p_1^(x_1) p_2^(x_2) ··· p_K^(x_K) for non-negative integers x_i summing to n.

Why are the off-diagonal covariance terms in a Multinomial distribution strictly negative?

Because the total number of trials is fixed at n = ∑_{i=1}^K X_i, the category counts are constrained by a linear conservation law. If category i occurs more frequently in a given experiment, fewer trials remain available for all other categories. Mathematically, taking the variance of the constant sum yields Var(∑ X_i) = Var(n) = 0. Expanding this variance gives ∑ Var(X_i) + 2 ∑_{i < j} Cov(X_i, X_j) = 0. Because individual variances Var(X_i) = n p_i (1 - p_i) > 0 are strictly positive, the cross-covariances must be negative on average. Specifically, Cov(X_i, X_j) = -n p_i p_j for i ≠ j.

What is the full structure of the Multinomial covariance matrix Σ?

The K × K covariance matrix Σ of a Multinomial random vector has diagonal entries Σ_{ii} = Var(X_i) = n p_i (1 - p_i) and off-diagonal entries Σ_{ij} = Cov(X_i, X_j) = -n p_i p_j for i ≠ j. In matrix notation, Σ = n (diag(p) - p p^T). Because the vector of all ones 1 satisfies Σ 1 = n (p - p(p^T 1)) = n(p - p(1)) = 0, the matrix has an eigenvalue of 0, meaning Σ is singular with rank K - 1. This singularity reflects the constraint that the counts reside in a (K - 1)-dimensional simplex.

What is the marginal distribution of any subset of Multinomial categories?

By collapsing categories, any single count X_i follows an exact Binomial distribution: X_i ~ Binomial(n, p_i). More generally, if we aggregate any subset of categories into a composite group with probability P_A = ∑_{i ∈ A} p_i and count X_A = ∑_{i ∈ A} X_i, the joint distribution of the remaining categories and X_A remains Multinomial. This aggregation invariance is a fundamental property of categorical processes.

What is the conditional distribution of X_1 given that X_2 = x_2?

Conditional on observing X_2 = x_2, the remaining n - x_2 trials must be distributed among the remaining K - 1 categories. The conditional distribution of X_1 given X_2 = x_2 is Binomial with trial count n - x_2 and renormalized success probability p_1 / (1 - p_2): X_1 | (X_2 = x_2) ~ Binomial(n - x_2, p_1 / (1 - p_2)). This conditional renormalization directly illustrates the negative dependency between categories.

How does the Multinomial distribution connect to the Poisson distribution via Poissonization?

If the total sample size N is not fixed, but is itself a random variable following a Poisson distribution N ~ Poisson(λ), and N items are subsequently sorted into K categories with probabilities p_1, ..., p_K, then the resulting category counts X_1, ..., X_K are mutually independent Poisson random variables with X_i ~ Poisson(λ p_i). This result, known as Poissonization, eliminates the negative covariance constraint and simplifies calculations in queuing networks and statistical physics.

How does the Multinomial distribution underpin the Pearson Chi-Squared goodness-of-fit test?

Under the null hypothesis that observed counts X = (X_1, ..., X_K) arise from a multinomial distribution with specified probabilities p, the Pearson goodness-of-fit statistic is Q = ∑_{i=1}^K (X_i - n p_i)^2 / (n p_i). By the multivariate central limit theorem, the standardized vector converges to a degenerate multivariate normal distribution on the simplex. Karl Pearson (1900) proved that the quadratic form Q converges in distribution to a Chi-Squared distribution with K - 1 degrees of freedom.

Experimental precautions and statistical checks

  • Ensure the category probability vector strictly satisfies ∑_{i=1}^K p_i = 1.0 within floating-point tolerance.
  • Verify that trials are mutually exclusive so that exactly one category increments on each step.
  • Account for the singular covariance matrix when performing multivariate normal approximations or matrix inversions.
  • Maintain sample sizes N ≥ 10,000 when computing empirical covariance terms to minimize Monte Carlo sampling noise.

Theoretical assumptions & limitations

  • The standard Multinomial model assumes constant category probabilities, which fails in urn models without replacement.
  • Overdispersed categorical data with clustering requires Dirichlet-Multinomial compound distributions.
