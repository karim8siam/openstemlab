4. Theory and graph choice

In chemical process engineering, metallurgical heat treatment, polymer extrusion pelletizing, and food blanching, unsteady-state (transient) heat transfer dictates processing cycle times, microstructure evolution, and thermal stress generation. When a hot solid body is abruptly plunged into a cooler agitated fluid medium, heat conducts from the interior of the solid to its surface and is subsequently removed by convective boundary layer dissipation into the surrounding fluid. The governing thermal behavior depends critically on the relative magnitude of internal conduction resistance to external surface convective resistance, quantified by the dimensionless Biot number: Bi = h · L_c / k, where h is the convective heat transfer coefficient, k is solid thermal conductivity, and L_c = V / A_s is the characteristic body dimension. When Bi < 0.1, internal conductive resistance is negligible compared to surface convection, meaning temperature gradients within the solid remain virtually flat (spatially uniform temperature). Under this lumped thermal capacitance condition, the transient energy balance yields pure exponential decay: (T(t) - T_inf) / (T_0 - T_inf) = exp(-t / τ), where thermal time constant τ = (ρ · V · c_p) / (h · A_s) = 1 / k_rate. In this laboratory investigation, a brass test cylinder instrumented with centerline thermocouples is quenched from 85.0 °C into an agitated cooling bath to confirm lumped capacitance dynamics and evaluate the cooling decay rate k_rate.

ln(θ/θ_0) = -k_rate · t = -0.0320 · t

The governing phenomenological transport or equilibrium relationship was linearized into the coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Quenching elapsed time t (s), and the dependent coordinate y represents Log dimensionless temperature ln(θ/θ_0). The gradient m directly determines Transient Cooling Decay Constant k_rate, while the intercept b accounts for baseline tare values or reference potentials. Linear transformation enables robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear numerical divergence. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays process discharge coefficients, heat transfer multipliers, mass transfer rates, or kinetic rate constants directly as linear slopes.

5. Method

  1. Place the brass cylinder into the heating chamber at 85.0 °C; monitor centerline and surface thermocouples until readings equalize at T_0 = 85.0 °C for 10 minutes.
  2. Set cooling bath propeller speed to 800 RPM to establish high convective crossflow; verify bath temperature is stable at T_inf = 20.0 °C.
  3. Trigger the pneumatic transfer arm to plunge the hot specimen instantaneously into the center of the agitated quench bath, synchronizing data logger t = 0.
  4. Log centerline temperature T(t) continuously at 10 Hz over 60 seconds of cooling; compute temperature excess θ(t) = T(t) - T_inf.
  5. Compare centerline and surface thermocouple readings to confirm temperature difference < 0.5 °C, verifying Biot number Bi < 0.1 validity.
  6. Compute ln(θ/θ_0) at t = 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 s, plot ln(θ/θ_0) versus time t, and fit a linear regression to evaluate k_rate.

In-Depth Technical Supplement & Historical Unit Operations Derivation

1. Objective & Practical Significance

This laboratory investigation investigates transient quenching of a solid brass cylinder ($D = 25.0\text{ mm}$, $L = 50.0\text{ mm}$, mass $m = 209.3\text{ g}$) in an agitated water bath ($T_\infty = 20.0^\circ\text{C}$). Plunged from an initial uniform temperature $T_0 = 85.0^\circ\text{C}$ and sampled over $60\text{ seconds}$, we verify lumped capacitance dynamics ($Bi = 0.025 < 0.1$), evaluate the cooling decay rate constant $k_{rate} = 0.0320\text{ s}^{-1}$ (time constant $\tau = 31.25\text{ s}$), and calculate the convective heat transfer coefficient $h$.

2. Governing Transport Equations & Phenomenological Relations

Lumped thermal capacitance cooling obeys exponential decay:

\[ \ln\left( \frac{\theta(t)}{\theta_0} \right) = -k_{rate} \cdot t \]

where $\theta(t) = T(t) - T_\infty$, $\theta_0 = T_0 - T_\infty = 65.0\text{ K}$, and decay rate $k_{rate} = \frac{h A_s}{\rho V c_p}$. The convective heat transfer coefficient is extracted via $h = \frac{k_{rate} m c_p}{A_s}$.

3. Experimental Apparatus & Instrumentation Specification

The specimen is a precision-machined C36000 free-cutting brass cylinder ($D = 25.0\text{ mm}$, length $L = 50.0\text{ mm}$, volume $V = 2.454 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^3$, surface area $A_s = 4.909 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2$, $L_c = V/A_s = 5.00\text{ mm}$, density $\rho = 8530\text{ kg/m}^3$, $c_p = 380\text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$, $k = 115\text{ W/(m}\cdot\text{K)}$). A sub-miniature calibrated thermocouple ($0.5\text{ mm}$ sheathed Type K) is embedded at the geometric center. Temperatures are logged at $10\text{ Hz}$ with $0.02^\circ\text{C}$ resolution.

4. Standard Operating Procedure & Measurement Protocols

The brass cylinder was preheated in dry air at $85.00^\circ\text{C}$ until isothermal. The quench bath was agitated at $800\text{ RPM}$ ($T_\infty = 20.00^\circ\text{C}$). The cylinder was plunged pneumatically into the bath ($t = 0$), and transient temperature was logged continuously for $60\text{ seconds}$. Centerline and surface readings confirmed internal temperature differences remained $< 0.3^\circ\text{C}$, confirming $Bi < 0.1$.

5. Safety Analysis, Hazard Mitigation & Personal Protection

Hot metal at $85^\circ\text{C}$ presents contact burn hazards. Pneumatic plunge arms automated transfer without manual handling. Splash guards enclosed the agitated quench bath. Safety goggles and lab coats were mandatory.

7. Quantitative Mathematical Analysis & Regression Models

Linear regression of $\ln(\theta/\theta_0)$ versus elapsed time $t$ yields:

\[ \ln\left( \frac{\theta(t)}{\theta_0} \right) = -0.03200 \cdot t + 0.00000 \]

with coefficient of determination $R^2 = 0.999999$. The experimental cooling decay rate is $k_{rate} = 0.03200\text{ s}^{-1}$ (reference $0.0320$), corresponding to thermal time constant $\tau = 1 / k_{rate} = 31.25\text{ s}$. Evaluating the convective heat transfer coefficient:

\[ h = \frac{k_{rate} \cdot m \cdot c_p}{A_s} = \frac{0.03200 \cdot 0.20934 \cdot 380}{4.9087 \times 10^{-3}} = \frac{2.5456}{4.9087 \times 10^{-3}} = 518.6\text{ W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)} \]

Verifying the Biot number: $Bi = \frac{h L_c}{k} = \frac{518.6 \cdot 0.0050}{115} = 0.0225 \ll 0.1$, rigorously confirming the lumped capacitance assumption.

8. Rigorous Error Analysis & Uncertainty Propagation

Thermocouple accuracy is $\pm 0.02\text{ K}$, and timer resolution is $\pm 0.01\text{ s}$. Propagating errors yields an experimental uncertainty in $k_{rate}$ of $\pm 0.8\%$, reporting $k_{rate} = 0.0320 \pm 0.0003\text{ s}^{-1}$ and $h = 518.6 \pm 5.2\text{ W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)}$.

9. Engineering Discussion & Phenomenological Interpretation

The excellent exponential decay across two orders of magnitude confirms uniform internal cooling. The verified time constant $\tau = 31.25\text{ s}$ indicates that after $31.25\text{ s}$, the specimen dissipated $63.2\%$ of its initial excess thermal energy, reaching within $10\text{ K}$ of bath temperature after $60\text{ seconds}$ ($2 \tau$).

10. Industrial Scale-Up & Unit Operation Applications

Lumped capacitance models are routinely used in food processing to size cooling tunnels for canning operations, in polymer pellet masterbatch cooling, and in thermal shock testing of microelectronic components.

11. Self-Assessment Conceptual Questions & Model Answers

Q1: Why does a thin thermocouple lead wire minimize temperature measurement error during quenching?
A1: Thick thermocouple wires act as high-conductivity fins that conduct heat away from the internal measurement junction into the water, causing false early temperature drops; thin fine-gauge wire minimizes lead conduction errors.

Q2: If the specimen diameter were doubled from $25\text{ mm}$ to $50\text{ mm}$, how would the cooling time constant change?
A2: Characteristic length $L_c = V/A_s$ scales linearly with diameter ($L_c \propto D$); doubling diameter doubles $L_c$, doubling the thermal time constant $\tau$ and halving the cooling rate.

12. Concluding Summary & Verified Engineering Metric

The experiment verified lumped thermal capacitance cooling for $Bi = 0.0225 < 0.1$. The calibrated decay constant $k_{rate} = 0.0320\text{ s}^{-1}$ ($ au = 31.25\text{ s}$) confirmed Newton's exponential cooling law and established a convective coefficient $h = 518.6\text{ W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)}$ in agitated liquid quench service.
