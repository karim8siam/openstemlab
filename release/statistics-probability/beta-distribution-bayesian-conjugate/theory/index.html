Probabilistic foundations of Beta Distribution & Priors.

The Beta distribution is the primary continuous probability model for quantities constrained to the unit interval [0, 1], such as probabilities, proportions, and market shares. In Bayesian statistics, it serves as the exact conjugate prior for the Bernoulli and Binomial likelihoods, providing an intuitive framework where prior knowledge and observed evidence combine through additive hyperparameter updating. In this laboratory, we examine the rich geometric spectrum of Beta densities, simulate Bayesian conjugate learning, calculate posterior credible intervals, and confirm the linear hyperparameter accumulation rate m = 4.000. In mathematical statistics, empirical data science, and modern computational engineering, understanding the asymptotic sampling properties and theoretical behavior of Beta Distribution, Shape Parameters, and Bayesian Conjugate Priors provides crucial guarantees. Rigorous experimental validation connects foundational probability laws with observable empirical phenomena, ensuring that statistical inference remains valid under finite sample sizes and real-world data collection constraints.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How is the continuous Beta distribution defined mathematically?

The Beta distribution Beta(α, β) with shape parameters α > 0 and β > 0 has probability density function f(θ; α, β) = (1 / B(α, β)) θ^(α - 1) (1 - θ)^(β - 1) for θ ∈ (0, 1), where B(α, β) = ∫_0^1 u^(α - 1) (1 - u)^(β - 1) du = Γ(α)Γ(β)/Γ(α + β) is the Euler Beta function. The expectation is E[θ] = α / (α + β), the mode is (α - 1) / (α + β - 2) for α, β > 1, and the variance is Var(θ) = α β / [ (α + β)^2 (α + β + 1) ].

What is conjugacy in Bayesian statistics, and why is the Beta distribution conjugate to the Binomial likelihood?

In Bayesian inference, a prior distribution is 'conjugate' to a likelihood function if the resulting posterior distribution belongs to the exact same parametric probability family as the prior. Let θ be the binomial success probability with prior p(θ) ~ Beta(α, β) ∝ θ^(α - 1) (1 - θ)^(β - 1). Given data y consisting of s successes and f = n - s failures, the binomial likelihood is L(θ; y) ∝ θ^s (1 - θ)^f. By Bayes' theorem, posterior p(θ | y) ∝ p(θ) L(θ; y) ∝ θ^(α + s - 1) (1 - θ)^(β + f - 1). This is recognized immediately as Beta(α + s, β + f), proving exact conjugacy.

How do different shape parameter choices affect the geometry of the Beta density?

The Beta distribution exhibits extraordinary geometric flexibility: (1) α = β = 1: Standard continuous Uniform distribution U[0, 1]; (2) α = β < 1: Symmetrical U-shaped density with infinite poles at 0 and 1 (e.g., Jeffreys prior α = β = 0.5); (3) α = β > 1: Symmetrical bell-shaped unimodal density centered at θ = 0.5; (4) α > β > 1: Negatively skewed unimodal density concentrated near 1; (5) β > α > 1: Positively skewed unimodal density concentrated near 0; (6) α = 1, β = 2: Linearly decreasing triangular density.

What is the interpretation of hyperparameters α and β as 'pseudo-counts'?

In conjugate Beta-Binomial updating, hyperparameters α and β function intuitively as prior 'pseudo-counts' of virtual successes and failures. The prior sample size is n_0 = α + β, representing the precision or confidence of prior belief. Observing empirical data simply adds real sample successes s and failures f directly to the prior tallies: α_post = α + s, β_post = β + f. The posterior mean can be written as a weighted average: E[θ | data] = (n_0 / (n_0 + n)) E_prior[θ] + (n / (n_0 + n)) (s / n).

What is Jeffreys prior for the Binomial model, and how is it derived from Fisher information?

Sir Harold Jeffreys proposed an objective, reparameterization-invariant prior proportional to the square root of the Fisher information: p(θ) ∝ √I(θ). For a Bernoulli model, log-likelihood is ln L = y ln θ + (1 - y) ln(1 - θ). The Fisher information is I(θ) = - E[∂^2 ln L / ∂θ^2] = 1 / [θ(1 - θ)]. Taking the square root gives p(θ) ∝ θ^(-1/2) (1 - θ)^(-1/2), which is precisely the Beta(0.5, 0.5) distribution (the arcsine distribution).

How is a Bayesian Credible Interval defined, and how does it differ fundamentally from a Frequentist Confidence Interval?

A 100(1 - α)% Bayesian credible interval [a, b] satisfies P(a ≤ θ ≤ b | data) = ∫_a^b p(θ | data) dθ = 1 - α. It directly expresses the posterior probability that the true unknown parameter θ lies within [a, b], treating θ as a random variable. In contrast, a frequentist confidence interval treats θ as a fixed, non-random constant; its 95% rating reflects the long-run coverage probability across hypothetically repeated experiments, not the probability for any single observed interval.

What is the relationship between order statistics of uniform variates and the Beta distribution?

If U_1, U_2, ..., U_n are independent identically distributed standard uniform random variables U_i ~ U[0, 1] and U_{(k)} represents the k-th smallest order statistic, then U_{(k)} follows an exact Beta distribution: U_{(k)} ~ Beta(k, n - k + 1). This fundamental theorem bridges non-parametric ranking theory with continuous parametric Beta modeling.

Experimental precautions and statistical checks

  • Ensure shape parameters α and β remain strictly positive (α > 0, β > 0) to avoid improper density integrals.
  • Utilize logarithmic beta function evaluations (lbeta) to calculate normalizing constants without numerical underflow.
  • Verify that empirical data consists of mutually exclusive counts (successes and failures) before updating.
  • Distinguish clearly between the posterior mean α/(α+β) and posterior mode (α-1)/(α+β-2) when reporting point estimates.

Theoretical assumptions & limitations

  • Conjugate Beta priors assume that prior beliefs can be adequately captured by pseudo-counts, which may restrict complex multi-modal priors.
  • In small-sample regimes, strongly informative priors can dominate empirical observations and bias posterior inference.
