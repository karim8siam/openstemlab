Theoretical Foundations of Freundlich Adsorption on Charcoal and Quantitative Chemical Metrology

Surface phenomena and adsorption at solid-liquid interfaces represent fundamental physicochemical processes underpinning industrial water purification, heterogeneous catalysis, chromatographic separations, and pharmaceutical detoxification. When a solid adsorbent is introduced into an aqueous solution of an organic solute, solute molecules partition between the bulk liquid phase and the solid surface until dynamic equilibrium is established. The relationship between the amount of solute adsorbed per unit mass of adsorbent (the specific adsorption capacity, q = x/m) and the equilibrium concentration of the solute in the bulk solution (C_eq) at constant temperature is known as an adsorption isotherm. In 1906, Herbert Freundlich formulated an empirical power-law equation, x/m = K_F · C_eq^(1/n), which was later proven to describe adsorption on energetically heterogeneous surfaces possessing an exponential distribution of site binding affinities. In this investigation, the adsorption of acetic acid from aqueous solution onto acid-washed activated coconut charcoal is quantitatively determined across six concentration levels at 25.0 °C. Following thermostatic equilibrium and filtration, residual acetic acid in the filtrate is determined by precision acid-base titrimetry with standardized 0.1000 M NaOH. Logarithmic transformation linearizes the isotherm, allowing ordinary least-squares regression of log10(x/m) against log10(C_eq) to extract the Freundlich heterogeneity exponent 1/n = 0.450 and capacity constant K_F = 2.50 mmol/g with high precision, confirming the heterogeneous microporous architecture of activated carbon.

TOPIC 01 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

What fundamental physical and thermodynamic differences distinguish physical adsorption (physisorption) from chemisorption?

Physisorption involves weak, non-specific long-range intermolecular forces (primarily London dispersion forces, dipole-dipole, and van der Waals attractions) between the adsorbate and the adsorbent surface, with low adsorption enthalpies (ΔH_ads typically −10 to −40 kJ/mol). It exhibits low activation energy, is readily reversible, and can form multimolecular layers at high relative pressures. In contrast, chemisorption involves true chemical bond formation (covalent, ionic, or coordination charge transfer) with high enthalpies (ΔH_ads −80 to −400 kJ/mol), requires activation energy, is usually irreversible, and is strictly restricted to a single monolayer on specific active sites.

TOPIC 02 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

What is the theoretical significance of the Freundlich adsorption isotherm equation and the heterogeneity parameter 1/n?

The Freundlich isotherm, x/m = K_F · C_eq^(1/n), was developed empirically and later justified theoretically by Zeldowitsch as an exponential distribution of adsorption site energies on heterogeneous surfaces. The parameter K_F relates to overall adsorption capacity, while 1/n (the Freundlich exponent) reflects the adsorption intensity and surface heterogeneity. For favorable physical adsorption on heterogeneous adsorbents, 1/n lies between 0.1 and 1.0. A value of 1/n < 1 indicates that high-energy adsorption sites are filled first, with adsorption affinity decreasing exponentially as surface coverage increases. A value of 1/n = 1 corresponds to linear Henry's law behavior, while 1/n > 1 signifies unfavorable cooperative adsorption.

TOPIC 03 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

What are the four fundamental postulates of Langmuir's monolayer adsorption model, and why does it break down on activated charcoal?

Langmuir's model assumes: (1) Adsorption occurs on a fixed number of localized, equivalent surface sites; (2) Each site can accommodate at most one adsorbate molecule (monolayer saturation); (3) All sites are energetically identical (homogeneous surface with constant adsorption enthalpy); and (4) There are no lateral interactions between adjacent adsorbed molecules. On activated charcoal, Langmuir's model fails because the carbon surface is structurally and energetically heterogeneous, possessing a vast pore size distribution (micropores < 2 nm, mesopores 2–50 nm) with varying localized surface potentials, functional groups (carboxyl, phenolic, lactone), and capillary condensation phenomena.

TOPIC 04 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Why is it critically necessary to discard the first 10 to 15 mL of filtrate before titrating the equilibrium solution?

Quantitative filter paper contains cellulose fibers possessing hydrophilic hydroxyl groups and capillary pores. When the initial charcoal suspension is poured onto the filter, the dry cellulose fibers can preferentially adsorb either acetic acid molecules or water molecules from solution. Discarding the first 15 mL of filtrate allows the filter paper to become completely pre-equilibrated with the solution, ensuring that subsequent collected filtrate accurately reflects the true bulk equilibrium concentration C_eq without chromatographic retention artifacts.

TOPIC 05 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

How does the micro-, meso-, and macroporous structure of activated carbon govern aqueous adsorption of small organic acids?

Activated carbon possesses an intricate hierarchy of pores: macropores (diameter > 50 nm) act as transport conduits allowing rapid hydrodynamic diffusion into the interior of carbon grains; mesopores (2–50 nm) provide transition channels; and micropores (< 2 nm) constitute over 90% of the total internal surface area (typically 800–1200 m²/g). Acetic acid has an effective molecular diameter of ~0.43 nm, allowing it to penetrate freely into the micropores where overlapping potential fields from opposite pore walls create enhanced adsorption potentials, resulting in the high capacity observed at low concentrations.

TOPIC 06 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Why is physical adsorption from solution an exothermic process (ΔH_ads < 0), and how does temperature affect adsorption capacity?

When an acetic acid molecule adsorbs onto the solid charcoal surface, its translational and rotational degrees of freedom are severely restricted, resulting in a decrease in entropy (ΔS_ads < 0). For the adsorption process to occur spontaneously under constant temperature and pressure, the Gibbs free energy change must be negative (ΔG_ads = ΔH_ads − T·ΔS_ads < 0). This requires that the enthalpy change must be negative (ΔH_ads < 0), meaning physisorption is universally exothermic. According to Le Chatelier's principle, increasing temperature shifts the equilibrium in the endothermic reverse direction, decreasing the amount of acid adsorbed at any given equilibrium concentration.

TOPIC 07 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

What is the role of surface oxygen functional groups in determining the adsorption of weak carboxylic acids on activated carbon?

Activated carbon surfaces contain various oxygenated functional groups formed during activation: acidic groups (carboxylic, lactonic, phenolic) and basic groups (pyrone, chromene). In aqueous solutions, neutral undissociated acetic acid (pKa = 4.76) adsorbs primarily via hydrophobic interactions and dispersion forces onto graphene basal planes, as well as hydrogen bonding with surface carbonyl and hydroxyl groups. At solution pH values below pKa, the acid exists predominantly as neutral CH3COOH, maximizing adsorption affinity compared to the hydrated, repulsive acetate anion (CH3COO⁻).

Connect the theory to experimental observations.

The physical chemistry simulation model evaluates the adsorption of aqueous acetic acid (CH3COOH, M = 60.05 g/mol) onto acid-washed activated charcoal (m = 2.000 g, V = 100.0 mL = 0.1000 L) at 25.0 °C (298.15 K). Theoretical Freundlich parameters: capacity parameter K_F = 2.500 mmol/g · (L/mol)^(1/n), heterogeneity exponent 1/n = 0.4500 (n = 2.222). Across the six equilibrium concentration coordinates C_eq = [0.030, 0.060, 0.120, 0.220, 0.350, 0.550] M, the corresponding specific adsorption capacities are q = x/m = [0.516, 0.705, 0.963, 1.265, 1.559, 1.910] mmol/g. Logarithmic coordinates are x = log10(C_eq) = [−1.5229, −1.2218, −0.9208, −0.6576, −0.4559, −0.2596] and y = log10(x/m) = [−0.2874, −0.1519, −0.0164, 0.1020, 0.1928, 0.2811]. Ordinary least-squares regression yields an empirical slope 1/n = 0.45002 and Y-intercept log10(K_F) = 0.39795 (K_F = 2.500 mmol/g) with Pearson correlation coefficient R² = 0.999999990. The empirical exponent 1/n = 0.450 exhibits near-perfect concordance within 0.005% of the 0.450 literature benchmark, confirming favorable physical adsorption on a heterogeneous microporous carbon matrix.
