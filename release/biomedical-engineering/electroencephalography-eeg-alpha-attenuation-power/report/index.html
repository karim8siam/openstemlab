1. Title and aim

EEG Spectral Power Density & Berger Alpha-Wave Attenuation. Quantify the reduction in occipital 8–12 Hz alpha-band spectral power during eye-opening (Berger effect) and determine visual sensory attenuation ratio using Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).

4. Theory and graph choice

Electroencephalography (EEG) records continuous postsynaptic potential fluctuations generated by pyramidal neurons in the cerebral cortex. Discovered by Hans Berger in 1929, the dominant rhythm of the relaxed, awake human brain with eyes closed is the alpha rhythm (8 to 12 Hz, amplitude 20 to 80 μV), most prominent over occipital and parietal electrode sites (O1, O2, Pz). Alpha oscillations originate from synchronized reciprocal thalamocortical reverberating loops that reflect a sensory idling or gating state of visual cortices. When an individual opens their eyes or engages in focused visual-spatial problem solving, visual photic input via the optic nerve and lateral geniculate nucleus desynchronizes these thalamocortical networks—a phenomenon known as the 'Berger effect' or alpha desynchronization (alpha blocking). In neuroengineering, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), and cognitive ergonomics, the attenuation of alpha power serves as a quantifiable metric of cortical sensory processing and mental workload. Across increasing ambient visual illuminance E_v, occipital alpha spectral power density P_alpha declines in a predictable linear relationship: P_alpha = -k_alpha · E_v + P_0. In this experiment, occipital EEG signals recorded using the International 10-20 system are digitized to analyze alpha band spectral power attenuation across calibrated photopic illumination levels.

P_alpha = -k_alpha · E_v + P_0 = -0.1250 · E_v + 52.0000

The governing physiological relationship was linearized into the standard regression coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Illuminance E_v (lux), and the dependent coordinate y represents Alpha band power P_alpha (μV²/Hz). The gradient m directly determines Photopic Attenuation Gradient k_alpha, while the intercept b accounts for baseline null offsets, pre-stress tares, or reference thresholds. Linear transformation allows robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear solver instability or convergence traps. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays sensor sensitivities, elastic moduli, hemodynamic resistances, or acoustic attenuation coefficients directly as linear slopes. This linear graphical formulation provides an immediate visual check of homoscedasticity, linearity, and potential sensor saturation, biological yield, or non-linear compliance.

5. Method

  1. Position Ag/AgCl electrodes at O1, O2, Cz, and linked earlobe references (A1, A2); verify all contact impedances are under 3.0 kΩ.
  2. Record 60 seconds of resting EEG with eyes closed in darkness; verify presence of synchronized 10.0 Hz occipital alpha rhythm.
  3. Instruct subject to open eyes and gaze at a neutral fixation cross; set photopic illuminance to initial test level of 50 lux.
  4. Record 30 seconds of artifact-free EEG; compute average power spectral density in the 8-12 Hz band (P_alpha in μV²/Hz).
  5. Stepwise increment ambient light through 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 lux, allowing 60 seconds for visual adaptation before logging P_alpha.
  6. Plot alpha band power P_alpha against illuminance E_v, performing least-squares linear regression to determine the photopic attenuation gradient k_alpha.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Instruct the subject to refrain from blinking or clenching their jaw during the 30-second EEG data recording epochs.

  • Use ripple-free DC power for lighting; standard AC fluorescent lamps induce 120 Hz optical flicker artifacts.
  • Enclose the recording station in a Faraday cage to eliminate 60 Hz mains interference.
