Civil engineering mechanics & structural foundations of Aggregate Sieve Analysis.

Aggregates constitute approximately 70% to 80% of total concrete volume, serving as an inert, dimensionally stable skeletal filler that reduces paste shrinkage, creep, and heat of hydration. The particle size distribution (gradation) of fine aggregates directly governs mix water demand, workability, packing density, and bleeding tendency. Under ASTM C136 and ASTM C33, an oven-dry sample of fine aggregate (minimum 500 g) is separated through a nested stack of standard woven-wire cloth sieves (4.75 mm No. 4, 2.36 mm No. 8, 1.18 mm No. 16, 600 μm No. 30, 300 μm No. 50, and 150 μm No. 100) mounted on a mechanical sieve shaker. The mass retained on each sieve is weighed to compute individual and cumulative percentages retained. A key empirical index is the Fineness Modulus (FM), calculated as the sum of cumulative percentages retained on standard sieves divided by 100. Standard concrete sands require an FM between 2.3 and 3.1: sand with FM < 2.2 is excessively fine and increases water demand, whereas sand with FM > 3.2 is coarse and harsh, leading to bleed channels and poor finishability. In this laboratory, sieve analysis is conducted to determine gradation compliance and evaluate the Fineness Modulus.

1. Grain Size Distribution & Aggregate Packing Theory

In concrete technology, aggregates are classified into discrete size fractions through dry mechanical sieving. The mathematical description of particle size distribution follows cumulative probability distribution functions. Fine aggregate particles range nominally from $75\,\mu\text{m}$ (No. 200 sieve) to $4.75\text{ mm}$ (No. 4 sieve). Maximum packing density occurs when void spaces between larger sand particles are progressively occupied by successively smaller particles without dilation, formulated by Andreasen and Andersen:

\[ P(d) = 100 \left( \frac{d}{D_{max}} \right)^q \]

where $P(d)$ is cumulative percent passing sieve aperture $d$, $D_{max}$ is maximum grain size ($4.75\text{ mm}$), and exponent $q \approx 0.25\text{ to }0.35$ for optimal packing.

2. Sieve Mesh Scaling & The ISO/ASTM Sieve Series

Standard sieves follow a geometric progression where aperture dimensions double at each consecutive step (modular ratio $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$ or $2.0$). The standard ASTM fine aggregate series comprises:

  • Sieve 1: $4.75\text{ mm}$ (No. 4)
  • Sieve 2: $2.36\text{ mm}$ (No. 8)
  • Sieve 3: $1.18\text{ mm}$ (No. 16)
  • Sieve 4: $600\,\mu\text{m}$ (No. 30)
  • Sieve 5: $300\,\mu\text{m}$ (No. 50)
  • Sieve 6: $150\,\mu\text{m}$ (No. 100)

3. Mathematical Formulation of Fineness Modulus (FM)

Fineness Modulus is defined as the sum of cumulative percentages retained on standard sieves divided by 100:

\[ FM = \frac{1}{100} \sum_{i=1}^{m} C_i \]

where $C_i$ is cumulative percentage retained on the $i$-th standard sieve. Because $C_i$ accumulates progressively across the geometric series, $FM$ represents a weighted logarithmic mean particle size. For a continuous linear progression across standard sieve index $k$ ($1 \le k \le 5$):

\[ C_k = k_{grad} \cdot k \]

where $k_{grad} = 28.50\%\text{/sieve}$, yielding a cumulative sum of $28.5 + 57.0 + 85.5 + 114.0 + 142.5 = 427.5\%$, corresponding to an $FM = 2.85$ when integrated over the standard six-sieve envelope.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How is the Fineness Modulus (FM) defined mathematically, and what does it represent physically?

Fineness Modulus is the sum of cumulative percentages retained on the standard sieve series (150 μm, 300 μm, 600 μm, 1.18 mm, 2.36 mm, 4.75 mm, 9.5 mm, 19 mm, 37.5 mm, 75 mm) divided by 100; physically, it represents the average sieve aperture index on which the aggregate is retained.

What are the ASTM C33 allowable limits for concrete fine aggregate Fineness Modulus?

ASTM C33 stipulates an FM between 2.3 and 3.1; if FM of consecutive shipments departs by more than 0.20 from the source design value, concrete proportions must be adjusted.

Why is the percentage of sand passing the 600 μm (No. 30) sieve considered critical in concrete mix design?

The fraction passing 600 μm (typically 25% to 60%) serves as the primary lubricant preventing harsh inter-particle friction; it controls paste bleeding, pumpability, and plastic finishability.

What is the consequence of having excess fine material passing the 75 μm (No. 200) sieve in sand?

Material passing 75 μm consists of silt and clay that drastically increases specific surface area and water demand, weakening paste-aggregate bond and causing excessive drying shrinkage.

Why must sieve shaking not be continued indefinitely beyond 10-15 minutes?

Excessive shaking can cause softer friable mineral particles to degrade mechanically through attrition against the hard wire mesh, generating artificial fines and skewing results.

How does well-graded sand improve the sustainability of concrete mixtures?

Well-graded sand maximizes packing density, reducing void space between aggregate particles and allowing lower cement paste volume for equivalent workability, lowering carbon footprint.

Experimental precautions and laboratory safety

  • Never overload sieves beyond maximum permitted mass capacities (e.g. 200 g for 200 mm diameter fine sieves) to prevent blinded mesh apertures.
  • Use soft brass or nylon brushes to clean wire cloth; never use sharp steel picks or knives that distort wire apertures.
  • Verify total recovered mass equals initial dry mass within 0.3%; otherwise, discard the run as particle loss or weighing error.
  • Ensure mechanical shaker clamping mechanism is securely tightened to prevent unseated sieves and dust ejection.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits
