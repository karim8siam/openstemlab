Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Finned Cylinder Convection.

In chemical process cooling towers, air-cooled condensers, reboilers, and electronic heat sinks, transferring heat to or from gases represents a severe transport bottleneck because gases possess low thermal conductivities and low heat capacities. Consequently, the gas-side convective heat transfer coefficient h is typically 10 to 50 times lower than liquid-side coefficients, dominating the overall thermal resistance. To compensate for this limitation, extended surfaces (fins) are bonded, extruded, or machined onto the gas-contacting perimeter, dramatically multiplying the effective heat transfer surface area. However, heat must conduct along the fin from the base to the tip while simultaneously dissipating convectively into the crossflow air stream. This continuous thermal dissipation causes a longitudinal temperature drop along the fin, meaning the fin surface operates at a lower average temperature than the bare cylinder base. This performance reduction is quantified by the fin efficiency η_f, defined as the actual heat dissipated by the fin divided by the ideal heat that would be dissipated if the entire fin remained at the base temperature T_b: η_f = tanh(m L_c) / (m L_c), where m = sqrt(2 h / (k t)). In crossflow forced convection, the convective coefficient h scales with free-stream velocity v_inf to the 0.6 power (Hilpert / Churchill–Bernstein correlation). In this laboratory investigation, an electrically heated aluminum finned cylinder is tested in a benchtop wind tunnel across air velocities from 1.0 to 6.0 m/s to determine the convective scaling slope S and evaluate fin efficiency.

h = S · (v_inf)^0.6 = 18.5000 · (v_inf)^0.6

1. Extended Surface Principles & Biot Number Criterion

In thermal process engineering, transferring heat between a solid boundary and a flowing gas presents a substantial transport barrier due to low gas-side convective coefficients ($h \approx 10\text{--}100\text{ W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)}$). Installing extended surfaces (fins) increases total exposed area $A_{tot}$, mitigating this resistance.

For a thin fin of thickness $t$, length $L$, and thermal conductivity $k$, the transverse Biot number $Bi = h (t/2) / k \ll 0.1$, justifying the classical one-dimensional conduction approximation along coordinate $x$ from base ($x = 0$) to tip ($x = L$).

2. The One-Dimensional Fin Equation

An energy balance on an infinitesimal fin element $dx$ under steady state yields:

\[ \frac{d^2 T}{dx^2} - \frac{h P}{k A_c} (T - T_\infty) = 0 \implies \frac{d^2 \theta}{dx^2} - m^2 \theta = 0 \]

where $\theta(x) = T(x) - T_\infty$, $P$ is fin perimeter, $A_c$ is cross-sectional area, and $m = \sqrt{\frac{h P}{k A_c}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2 h}{k t}}$ for a thin rectangular fin ($P \approx 2W$, $A_c = W t$).

3. Fin Efficiency & Total Heat Dissipation

Applying the convective tip boundary condition (or insulated tip with corrected length $L_c = L + t/2$):

\[ \eta_f = \frac{q_{actual}}{q_{ideal}} = \frac{\tanh(m L_c)}{m L_c} \]

The total heat dissipated from an array of $N$ fins sharing base area $A_b$ is:

\[ q_{tot} = h \eta_o A_{tot} (T_b - T_\infty) \]

where $\eta_o = 1 - \frac{N A_f}{A_{tot}} (1 - \eta_f)$ is overall surface efficiency. In crossflow forced convection, the convective coefficient $h$ scales with free-stream air velocity $v_\infty$ via the Hilpert correlation ($Nu = C Re^{0.6} Pr^{0.33}$):

\[ h = S \cdot (v_\infty)^{0.6} \]

where $S$ is the forced convection scaling slope in $\text{W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)/(m/s)}^{0.6}$.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the physical significance of fin efficiency η_f and why is it always less than 1.0?

Fin efficiency is the ratio of actual heat dissipated by the fin to the ideal heat that would be dissipated if the entire fin were isothermal at the base temperature; finite thermal conductivity k creates a temperature gradient along the fin length, ensuring η_f < 1.0.

How does the fin parameter m = sqrt(2 h / (k t)) govern temperature distribution along a fin?

The parameter m represents the ratio of convective surface conductance to conductive longitudinal resistance; a larger m (low k, thin fin, or high h) produces a steep temperature drop and low fin efficiency.

Why are extended surfaces almost exclusively installed on the gas side rather than liquid side?

Gases have very low convective coefficients (h ≈ 10–100 W/(m^2·K)) compared to liquids (h ≈ 1,000–10,000 W/(m^2·K)); adding fins to the gas side balances the thermal resistances and provides maximum overall heat duty improvement.

What is fin effectiveness ε_f and how does it differ from fin efficiency η_f?

Fin efficiency η_f compares actual fin heat transfer to ideal isothermal fin heat transfer; fin effectiveness ε_f is the ratio of heat transferred with the fin to heat transferred from the bare base area without the fin (fins are justified only if ε_f > 2).

Why does aluminum remain the dominant material for finned heat exchangers despite copper having higher conductivity?

While copper has higher conductivity (400 vs 205 W/(m·K)), aluminum has one-third the density, is significantly cheaper, easier to extrude into complex fin profiles, and provides superior heat transfer per unit mass and cost.

How does radiation heat transfer contribute to total heat dissipation from a finned cylinder?

At moderate base temperatures (< 100 °C), radiative dissipation to ambient surroundings typically accounts for 5% to 10% of total heat loss, calculated via q_rad = ε_emissivity σ A_tot (T_b^4 - T_surr^4).

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Allow at least 15 minutes of thermal stabilization after changing wind tunnel velocity before logging temperatures.
  • Ensure thermocouples are seated firmly in thermal grease to eliminate contact resistance errors.
  • Verify heater electrical power remains strictly constant at 150.0 W throughout all velocity steps.
  • Shield the test section from external drafts in the laboratory.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (1.0 to 6.0 m/s).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
