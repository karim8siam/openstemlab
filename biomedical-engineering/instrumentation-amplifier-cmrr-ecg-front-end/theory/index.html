Biomedical and physiological foundations of Instrumentation Amplifier CMRR.

In clinical biopotential recording (ECG, EEG, EMG), the target physiological signal is exceedingly small—typically 1.0 mV for ECG and under 50 μV for EEG. Simultaneously, the human body acts as an antenna that capacitively couples to 50/60 Hz powerline electric fields, inducing large common-mode interference voltages (often 1.0 V to 3.0 V peak-to-peak, thousands of times larger than the bioelectric signal). Extracting microvolt biopotentials in the presence of massive common-mode noise requires an analog front-end with an extraordinary Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR > 90 to 110 dB). The classic three-operational-amplifier instrumentation amplifier (INA) achieves this through a buffered input stage with gain R_G: differential gain is set by A_d = (1 + 2·R_1 / R_G) · (R_3 / R_2), while ideal common-mode gain is A_cm = 0. In physical hardware, small resistor mismatches (tolerance error ΔR/R) in the second-stage difference amplifier allow a fraction of common-mode input voltage V_cm to leak into the output: V_out,cm = A_cm · V_cm. The CMRR in decibels is defined as CMRR = 20 · log10(A_d / A_cm). In this laboratory experiment, a precision biomedical instrumentation amplifier (configured for differential gain A_d = 1000 V/V) is tested across regulated common-mode test voltages to quantify A_cm, verify linear leakage, and evaluate front-end CMRR.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How is Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR) defined mathematically?

CMRR is the ratio of differential voltage gain to common-mode voltage gain: CMRR = A_d / A_cm. Expressed in decibels: CMRR_dB = 20 · log10(A_d / A_cm). Higher values indicate superior suppression of common-mode interference.

Why is high CMRR indispensable in biomedical ECG amplifiers?

The human body picks up 1 to 3 V of 60 Hz electrostatic displacement current from power wiring, while the cardiac ECG signal is only ~1 mV. A CMRR of at least 100 dB (100,000:1) is required to reduce 1 V of noise to under 10 μV, preventing it from overwhelming the ECG.

What is the primary factor limiting CMRR in real-world instrumentation amplifiers?

Resistor mismatch in the subtractor difference stage. Even with 0.1% tolerance resistors, worst-case mismatch can limit second-stage CMRR to roughly 66 dB unless active laser trimming or high-gain input buffers are used.

How does the first input stage of a three-op-amp INA affect common-mode gain?

The two input buffer op-amps amplify differential signals by (1 + 2·R1/RG) while passing common-mode signals at unity gain (A_cm,1 = 1). This boosts differential amplitude before the difference stage, dramatically enhancing overall CMRR.

What is the Right Leg Drive (RLD) circuit and how does it improve clinical ECG recording?

The RLD circuit samples common-mode body noise, inverts it through an active op-amp, and feeds it back into the patient's right leg. This acts as an active electronic ground that cancels the body's common-mode voltage, reducing effective noise by 20 to 40 dB.

Why must biomedical amplifiers possess extraordinarily high input impedance (> 10 GΩ)?

Skin-electrode contact impedances are high and unequal (e.g., 5 kΩ vs 15 kΩ). High amplifier input impedance prevents these source imbalances from converting common-mode voltage into differential noise through voltage divider action.

Experimental precautions and patient safety

  • Verify both inputs receive identical common-mode voltage using matched shielded coaxial leads.
  • Ensure common-mode voltage does not exceed op-amp common-mode input range (±13 V on ±15 V rails).
  • Shield the test circuit in a grounded aluminum enclosure to prevent stray capacitive pickup from lighting.
  • Warm up instrumentation for 20 minutes to achieve thermal equilibrium before logging microvolt data.

Theoretical assumptions & biological limits

  • The linear leakage model applies within the linear common-mode input range (V_cm ≤ 3.0 V).
  • Assumes balanced low-impedance signal source; does not model source impedance unbalance degradation.
  • Valid for powerline frequencies (50/60 Hz); CMRR degrades at higher frequencies due to stray capacitance.
  • Neglects op-amp input bias current offset voltage drift over ambient temperature shifts.
