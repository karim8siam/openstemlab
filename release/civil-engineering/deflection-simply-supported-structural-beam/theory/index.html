Civil engineering mechanics & structural foundations of Beam Elastic Deflection.

In structural civil engineering, the serviceability limit state (deflection control) is just as critical as the ultimate strength limit state. Excessive beam deflections cause unsightly sagging, crack plaster and brittle architectural finishes, jam doors and operable windows, and induce alarming vibrations under human foot traffic. Classical Euler–Bernoulli beam theory dictates that the elastic deflection curve v(x) of a prismatic, homogeneous beam of flexural rigidity EI obeys the fundamental second-order differential equation d^2v/dx^2 = M(x) / (EI), where M(x) is internal bending moment. For a simply supported beam of span L subjected to a concentrated central point load P at midspan (x = L/2), double integration with boundary conditions (zero deflection at supports x = 0 and x = L) yields the famous midspan deflection expression: δ = P L^3 / (48 E I). Measuring midspan deflection δ as a function of load P produces a linear elastic compliance slope C = δ / P = L^3 / (48 E I), from which the experimental flexural rigidity EI and Young's modulus E can be deduced. In this laboratory, a structural steel I-beam is loaded across loads from 2 to 14 kN using digital dial indicators to verify beam flexure theory and evaluate flexural compliance.

δ = 0.4250 · P

1. The Euler–Bernoulli Differential Equation of Flexure

Consider a straight, prismatic beam of span $L$ possessing flexural rigidity $EI$ simply supported at $x = 0$ and $x = L$. Under the Euler–Bernoulli kinematic hypothesis, plane cross-sections remain plane and perpendicular to the deformed neutral axis. The curvature $\kappa(x)$ of the elastic curve $v(x)$ relates directly to internal bending moment $M(x)$:

\[ \kappa(x) = \frac{d^2 v / dx^2}{\left[ 1 + (dv/dx)^2 \right]^{3/2}} \approx \frac{d^2 v}{dx^2} = \frac{M(x)}{EI} \]

where small-slope approximations ($(dv/dx)^2 \ll 1$) are valid for structural serviceability deformations.

2. Double Integration for Central Point Load

Under a concentrated point load $P$ applied at midspan ($x = L/2$), the reaction forces at supports $A$ and $B$ are $R_A = R_B = P/2$. The internal bending moment distribution for the left half ($0 \le x \le L/2$) is:

\[ M(x) = \frac{P}{2} x \]

Integrating the governing differential equation once yields the angular slope $\theta(x) = dv/dx$:

\[ EI \frac{dv}{dx} = \int \frac{P}{2} x \, dx = \frac{P x^2}{4} + C_1 \]

By symmetry, the slope at midspan must be zero: $\frac{dv}{dx}\left( \frac{L}{2} \right) = 0$:

\[ \frac{P (L/2)^2}{4} + C_1 = 0 \implies C_1 = - \frac{P L^2}{16} \]

Integrating a second time yields the elastic deflection profile $v(x)$:

\[ EI v(x) = \int \left( \frac{P x^2}{4} - \frac{P L^2}{16} \right) dx = \frac{P x^3}{12} - \frac{P L^2 x}{16} + C_2 \]

Applying the pinned support boundary condition $v(0) = 0$ requires $C_2 = 0$. The maximum deflection occurs at midspan ($x = L/2$):

\[ v\left( \frac{L}{2} \right) = \frac{1}{EI} \left[ \frac{P (L/2)^3}{12} - \frac{P L^2 (L/2)}{16} \right] = \frac{1}{EI} \left[ \frac{P L^3}{96} - \frac{P L^3}{32} \right] = - \frac{P L^3}{48 EI} \]

The magnitude of downward midspan vertical deflection $\delta$ is therefore:

\[ \delta = \frac{P L^3}{48 EI} \]

3. Experimental Compliance & Rigidity Extraction

Plotting measured midspan deflection $\delta$ versus applied load $P$ produces a straight line through the origin with flexural compliance slope $C_b$:

\[ \delta = C_b \cdot P, \qquad C_b = \frac{L^3}{48 EI} \]

For span $L = 1200\text{ mm}$, $I = 4.235 \times 10^5\text{ mm}^4$, and $E = 200\text{ GPa}$ ($2.0 \times 10^5\text{ N/mm}^2$):

\[ C_b = \frac{(1200\text{ mm})^3}{48 \times 200,000\text{ N/mm}^2 \times 423,500\text{ mm}^4} = \frac{1.728 \times 10^9}{4.0656 \times 10^{12}} = 0.00042503\text{ mm/N} = 0.4250\text{ mm/kN} \]

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How is the midspan deflection formula δ = P L^3 / (48 E I) derived from bending moment distribution?

The bending moment equation for x ≤ L/2 is M(x) = (P/2) x; integrating d^2v/dx^2 = M / EI yields slope dv/dx = (P x^2) / (4 EI) + C_1; symmetry at midspan requires dv/dx(L/2) = 0, giving C_1 = - P L^2 / (16 EI); integrating again with v(0) = 0 gives v(x) = (P x^3) / (12 EI) - (P L^2 x) / (16 EI); evaluating at x = L/2 yields δ = P L^3 / (48 EI).

What fundamental assumptions underlie the Euler–Bernoulli beam bending theory?

Assumptions: (1) Plane sections normal to the neutral axis remain plane and normal after bending (shear deformation neglected); (2) Material is homogeneous, isotropic, and obeys Hooke's Law; (3) Deflections are small compared to beam depth; (4) The beam is prismatic with a vertical plane of symmetry.

What is the difference between Euler–Bernoulli beam theory and Timoshenko beam theory?

Euler–Bernoulli theory neglects transverse shear deformation and rotary inertia, which is accurate for slender beams (L/d > 10); Timoshenko beam theory includes shear deformation, which adds significant additional deflection in short, deep beams (L/d < 5).

Why is one support configured as a pinned knife-edge and the other as a free-rolling roller?

A roller support allows unrestrained axial longitudinal expansion/contraction during flexural curvature, preventing parasitic axial membrane tension forces from stiffening the beam and reducing deflection.

How does beam depth d influence flexural rigidity compared to beam width b?

Moment of inertia is I = b d^3 / 12; doubling beam width b doubles stiffness (2x), whereas doubling beam depth d increases stiffness by a factor of eight (2^3 = 8x), making depth the dominant geometric factor.

What are typical serviceability deflection limits for commercial floor beams in building codes?

IBC and AISC 360 specify maximum live-load deflection of L / 360 for floors supporting plastered ceilings, and L / 240 for general structural roof/floor framing to prevent architectural damage.

Experimental precautions and laboratory safety

  • Verify that the load is applied exactly at midspan (L/2) without lateral eccentricity to prevent torsional twisting.
  • Ensure the dial indicator stem is oriented perpendicular to the beam bottom flange and placed on the neutral axis projection.
  • Do not exceed the elastic limit load (yield moment M_y) to prevent permanent plastic deformation of the beam.
  • Verify roller support moves freely on its base plate without dirt or mechanical binding that would induce horizontal thrust.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The civil engineering physical models apply within the specified calibrated operational window (2.0 to 14.0 kN).
  • Assumes homogeneous, isotropic continuum material behavior without unmodeled micro-fracture anomalies.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • Extreme inelastic yielding or cyclic degradation requires non-linear constitutive continuum damage mechanics.
