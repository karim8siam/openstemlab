Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Batch Reactor Arrhenius Kinetics.

In chemical process engineering and fine chemical synthesis, batch reactors are the premier equipment choice for small-volume, high-value specialty products, pharmaceuticals, and kinetic rate data determination. In an isothermal batch reactor with uniform mixing, reactants are charged at t = 0, and reaction proceeds unsteady-state as concentrations decline continuously with time. For an equimolar second-order reaction A + B -> Products with C_A0 = C_B0, the species conservation balance yields: -dC_A / dt = k · C_A^2, which integrates analytically to: 1 / C_A - 1 / C_A0 = k · t. Measuring reactant concentration versus time allows precise evaluation of the reaction rate constant k at a given temperature. Svante Arrhenius (1889) formulated the universal law governing the temperature dependence of chemical reaction rate constants: k(T) = A · exp[-E_a / (R · T)], where A is the pre-exponential frequency factor, E_a is the activation energy (the minimum kinetic energy colliding reactant molecules must possess to overcome the transition state barrier), R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) is the universal gas constant, and T is absolute temperature in Kelvin. Taking natural logarithms yields the classic linear Arrhenius equation: ln(k) = - (E_a / R) · (1 / T) + ln(A). Plotting ln(k) versus inverse absolute temperature (1000 / T) yields a straight line with slope -E_a / (1000 R), from which activation energy E_a and pre-exponential factor A are determined. In this laboratory investigation, an instrumented 1.0-liter jacketed batch reactor is operated across temperatures from 20.0 °C to 45.0 °C to measure second-order rate constants k, verify Arrhenius temperature scaling, and determine activation energy E_a = 48.00 kJ/mol (slope S = E_a / (1000 R) = 5.7740 kK).

1. Chemical Kinetics in an Isothermal Batch Reactor

Batch reactors operate unstably in time with spatial uniformity. For an equimolar second-order irreversible reaction $A + B \rightarrow \text{Products}$ with initial concentrations $C_{A0} = C_{B0}$, the rate of consumption of reactant $A$ is:

\[ -\frac{dC_A}{dt} = k C_A C_B = k C_A^2 \]

Separating variables and integrating from $t = 0$ ($C_A = C_{A0}$) to time $t$ ($C_A$):

\[ -\int_{C_{A0}}^{C_A} \frac{dC_A}{C_A^2} = k \int_0^t dt \implies \frac{1}{C_A} - \frac{1}{C_{A0}} = k \cdot t \]

Plotting $1 / C_A$ versus time $t$ yields a straight line whose slope gives the rate constant $k$ at temperature $T$.

2. The Arrhenius Temperature Dependence

Svante Arrhenius (1889) formulated the fundamental relation linking rate constant $k$ to absolute temperature $T$:

\[ k(T) = A \exp\left( -\frac{E_a}{R T} \right) \]

where $A$ is the pre-exponential frequency factor, $E_a$ is activation energy ($\text{J/mol}$), and $R = 8.31446\text{ J/(mol}\cdot\text{K)}$. Taking natural logarithms:

\[ \ln(k) = -\left( \frac{E_a}{R} \right) \frac{1}{T} + \ln(A) = -\left( \frac{E_a}{1000 R} \right) \left( \frac{1000}{T} \right) + \ln(A) \]

Defining inverse temperature $X = 1000 / T$ and slope $S = E_a / (1000 R)$:

\[ \ln(k) = -S \cdot \left( \frac{1000}{T} \right) + \ln(A) \]

Plotting $\ln(k)$ versus $1000/T$ yields a straight line with slope $-S$. The activation energy $E_a$ is evaluated as:

\[ E_a = 1000 \cdot R \cdot S = 8314.46 \cdot S \]

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the physical meaning of the Arrhenius activation energy E_a?

Activation energy E_a represents the minimum kinetic energy threshold colliding reactant molecules must possess to overcome electrostatic repulsion and bond distortion, reaching the transition state complex to form products.

How is the pre-exponential frequency factor A interpreted in collision theory?

In collision theory, A = Z · p, where Z is the frequency of molecular collisions per unit volume and p is the steric factor representing the fraction of collisions with favorable spatial geometric orientation.

Why is the saponification of ethyl acetate ideal for teaching batch kinetics?

It is an irreversible, clean second-order reaction with no side reactions, operates safely at room temperature and pressure, has a convenient half-life (~5 min), and can be monitored non-invasively via electrical conductivity.

What is the rule of thumb that reaction rates double for every 10 °C temperature rise?

For reactions with typical activation energies of E_a ≈ 50 kJ/mol around room temperature (300 K), the ratio k(T+10)/k(T) ≈ exp[50,000 · 10 / (8.314 · 300 · 310)] ≈ 1.96, approximately doubling the rate.

How does an isothermal batch reactor maintain constant temperature during exothermic reactions?

Heat generated by reaction must be removed by circulating chilled utility coolant through the reactor jacket or internal cooling coils: q_cool = U A (T_rxn - T_cool) = V (-r_A) (-ΔH_rxn).

What is the reaction half-life t_1/2 for an equimolar second-order reaction?

Setting C_A = C_A0 / 2 in 1/C_A - 1/C_A0 = k t yields t_1/2 = 1 / (k · C_A0); unlike first-order reactions where half-life is constant, second-order half-life is inversely proportional to initial concentration.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Pre-thermostat both reactant solutions separately to the target reaction temperature before mixing to prevent initial thermal transients.
  • Maintain vigorous agitation (300 RPM) to ensure uniform composition and eliminate local concentration gradients.
  • Seal the reactor port tightly to prevent volatile ethyl acetate vapor escape over the 15-minute run.
  • Calibrate the conductivity probe at each temperature, as ionic mobility increases with temperature.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (20.0 to 45.0 °C).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
