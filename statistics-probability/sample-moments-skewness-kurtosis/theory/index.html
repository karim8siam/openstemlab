Probabilistic foundations of Sample Moments & Kurtosis.

In mathematical statistics, moments provide a quantitative framework for characterizing the probability distribution of a random variable. The first raw moment represents the expectation or central location; the second central moment represents the variance or dispersion; the third standardized moment quantifies skewness or asymmetry; and the fourth standardized moment quantifies kurtosis or tail weight. In this laboratory, we employ pseudo-random Monte Carlo sampling to compute empirical moments across standardized distribution domains, test the numerical stability of moment update recursions, and rigorously verify the theoretical moment sequence of continuous distributions. In mathematical statistics, empirical data science, and modern computational engineering, understanding the asymptotic sampling properties and theoretical behavior of Sample Moments, Variance, Skewness, and Kurtosis provides crucial guarantees. Rigorous experimental validation connects foundational probability laws with observable empirical phenomena, ensuring that statistical inference remains valid under finite sample sizes and real-world data collection constraints.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the theoretical definition of raw versus central moments, and how do they describe probability distributions?

The k-th raw moment of a continuous random variable X with probability density function f(x) is defined as μ_k' = E[X^k] = ∫ x^k f(x) dx. The first raw moment μ_1' is the mean μ, representing the center of mass. The k-th central moment is defined as μ_k = E[(X - μ)^k] = ∫ (x - μ)^k f(x) dx. The second central moment μ_2 is the population variance σ^2, measuring spread. The third central moment μ_3 measures directional asymmetry, and the fourth central moment μ_4 characterizes tail weight and peakedness. Together, the infinite moment sequence uniquely specifies the distribution under Carleman's condition.

How is the Fisher-Pearson standardized skewness coefficient formulated and interpreted?

The population skewness γ_1 is the third standardized central moment: γ_1 = E[((X - μ)/σ)^3] = μ_3 / σ^3. In empirical samples of size n, sample skewness g_1 = (m_3 / m_2^(3/2)) · [√(n(n-1)) / (n-2)]. A symmetric distribution has γ_1 = 0. A positive skewness (γ_1 > 0) indicates an elongated right-hand tail with mass concentrated to the left of the mean, whereas negative skewness (γ_1 < 0) indicates an elongated left-hand tail.

What is kurtosis, and how does excess kurtosis classify distributions?

Kurtosis is the fourth standardized central moment β_2 = E[((X - μ)/σ)^4] = μ_4 / σ^4. Excess kurtosis is defined as γ_2 = β_2 - 3. Mesokurtic distributions (such as the normal distribution) have γ_2 = 0. Leptokurtic distributions have positive excess kurtosis (γ_2 > 0), indicating fat tails and heavy outlier propensity (e.g., Student's t, Laplace). Platykurtic distributions have negative excess kurtosis (γ_2 < 0), indicating thin tails and bounded support (e.g., Uniform U[0, 1] with γ_2 = -1.2).

How are raw moments converted into central moments algebraically?

Using the binomial expansion (X - μ)^k = ∑_{j=0}^k (-1)^(k-j) (k choose j) X^j μ^(k-j) and taking expectations yields the transformation: μ_1 = 0; μ_2 = μ_2' - μ^2; μ_3 = μ_3' - 3μ μ_2' + 2μ^3; μ_4 = μ_4' - 4μ μ_3' + 6μ^2 μ_2' - 3μ^4. Computing central moments directly via these algebraic formulas can suffer from severe cancellation in floating-point arithmetic when the mean is large compared to the variance, motivating numerically stable recurrence algorithms like Welford's algorithm.

Why is Welford's algorithm essential for numerically stable variance and moment updates?

The naive textbook formula S = ∑ X_i^2 - (∑ X_i)^2 / n subtracts two nearly identical large numbers when the mean is large and variance is small, leading to catastrophic loss of precision or negative variance estimates in IEEE 754 arithmetic. Welford's recurrence updates the mean and sum of squared differences incrementally: M_k = M_{k-1} + (x_k - M_{k-1})/k and S_k = S_{k-1} + (x_k - M_{k-1})(x_k - M_k), maintaining 64-bit precision in a single pass without precision loss.

What is the theoretical moment sequence for a continuous uniform distribution U[0, 1]?

For X ~ U[0, 1], the probability density is f(x) = 1 for 0 ≤ x ≤ 1. The k-th raw moment is E[X^k] = ∫_0^1 x^k dx = [x^(k+1)/(k+1)]_0^1 = 1 / (k + 1). Inverting this expectation yields 1 / E[X^k] = k + 1, defining an exact linear relation y = x + 1 where x = k and y = 1/E[X^k], with an exact theoretical gradient of m = 1.000 and intercept b = 1.000.

What are L-moments and what advantages do they provide over classical product moments?

L-moments are expectations of linear combinations of order statistics. Because L-moments involve linear weights rather than squaring or cubing sample deviations, they are far less sensitive to sampling outliers, suffer from negligible finite-sample bias, and exist for heavy-tailed distributions whose conventional moments are infinite.

Experimental precautions and statistical checks

  • Utilize compensated Kahan summation when accumulating powers of data values to eliminate round-off drift.
  • Verify that sample size N ≥ 10,000 when estimating sample kurtosis to ensure asymptotic consistency.
  • Never apply naive variance formulas S = ∑ X^2 - (∑ X)^2/N to large offset data without mean centering.
  • Perform replication across multiple independent pseudorandom seeds to verify that estimator variance and bias bounds are invariant to initial generator states.

Theoretical assumptions & limitations

  • Higher-order moments (k ≥ 4) have large sampling variance and are volatile in small datasets.
  • Heavy-tailed distributions (e.g., Pareto with α ≤ 4) have infinite theoretical fourth moments, causing sample kurtosis to diverge.
