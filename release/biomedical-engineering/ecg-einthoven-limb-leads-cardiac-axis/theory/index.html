Biomedical and physiological foundations of Einthoven ECG Vector Axis.

The electrocardiogram (ECG) represents the spatial summation of electrical action potentials propagating through myocardial conduction pathways and ventricular muscle fibers during each cardiac cycle. Willem Einthoven in 1901 formalized the mathematical foundation of electrocardiology by conceptualizing the human torso as an equilateral triangle with the heart at the electrical center. By convention, bipolar limb leads measure potential differences between electrodes placed on the limbs: Lead I measures right arm to left arm (V_LA - V_RA), Lead II measures right arm to left leg (V_LL - V_RA), and Lead III measures left arm to left leg (V_LL - V_LA). Kirchoff's voltage law dictates Einthoven's fundamental triangle law: Lead II = Lead I + Lead III. During ventricular depolarization (QRS complex), the instantaneous spatial dipole vector can be projected onto the hexaxial frontal reference system to calculate the mean electrical cardiac axis (normally between -30° and +90°). Deviations from this normal axis provide crucial clinical diagnostic markers for left ventricular hypertrophy (left axis deviation), right ventricular strain, pulmonary embolism, and bundle branch conduction blocks. In this laboratory experiment, a multi-lead medical electrocardiograph is coupled to an anatomical thoracic cardiac vector phantom to record standard frontal limb leads, evaluate lead proportionality, and calculate the anatomical cardiac electrical axis.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

State Einthoven's triangle law and explain its theoretical physical basis.

Einthoven's law states that Lead II = Lead I + Lead III. Because Lead I is (V_LA - V_RA), Lead III is (V_LL - V_LA), and Lead II is (V_LL - V_RA), adding Lead I and Lead III algebraically yields (V_LA - V_RA) + (V_LL - V_LA) = V_LL - V_RA, which identically equals Lead II by Kirchhoff's voltage law.

What is the normal clinical range for the mean electrical cardiac axis in adults?

The normal mean electrical axis in adults lies between -30° and +90° in the frontal plane. An axis between -30° and -90° indicates Left Axis Deviation (LAD), while an axis between +90° and +180° indicates Right Axis Deviation (RAD).

How are augmented unipolar limb leads (aVR, aVL, aVF) derived?

Emmanuel Goldberger created augmented leads by disconnecting the explored limb from Wilson's central terminal, increasing signal amplitude by 50%. aVR explores the right arm, aVL explores the left arm, and aVF explores the foot (aVF = Lead II/2 + Lead III/2).

What physiological event corresponds to the QRS complex in the surface ECG?

The QRS complex represents rapid electrical depolarization of the right and left ventricles through the His-Purkinje conduction system, initiating mechanical ventricular systole and pumping blood into the pulmonary artery and aorta.

Why does left ventricular hypertrophy shift the electrical axis to the left?

In left ventricular hypertrophy, the muscular wall of the left ventricle thickens substantially. More electrical dipoles are generated by the enlarged muscle mass, skewing the net vector toward the left and superiorly (producing Left Axis Deviation).

What is Wilson's Central Terminal (WCT) and what role does it serve?

Wilson's central terminal connects the RA, LA, and LL electrodes through three equal 5.0 kΩ resistors to a common node. Because the sum of currents is zero, WCT provides a stable virtual electrical zero potential against which unipolar chest and augmented leads are referenced.

Experimental precautions and patient safety

  • Verify low electrode contact impedance (< 2 kΩ) across all four limbs to prevent differential unbalance noise.
  • Ensure patient/phantom cables are bundled and shielded to suppress magnetic induction loops.
  • Verify highpass filter is set to diagnostic bandwidth (0.05 Hz) to prevent ST-segment distortion and baseline tilt.
  • Check that the Right Leg Drive circuit is engaged and active to suppress 60 Hz powerline hum.

Theoretical assumptions & biological limits

  • The linear dipole model assumes an electrically homogeneous, isotropic volume conductor.
  • Assumes a single lumped equivalent dipole; does not model complex localized multipole cardiac fields.
  • Neglects respiratory baseline wander caused by diaphragm movement shifting the heart during breathing.
  • Applies to the frontal plane projection; spatial orientation requires 3D vectorcardiography.
