Civil engineering mechanics & structural foundations of Los Angeles Abrasion.

In civil highway and pavement engineering, coarse mineral aggregates comprise 90% to 95% of the total mass of asphalt concrete mixtures and up to 80% of Portland cement concrete pavements. During pavement construction (stockpiling, pneumatic mixing, vibratory roller compaction) and throughout service life under heavy pneumatic commercial truck wheel traffic, coarse aggregates are subjected to intense mechanical crushing, edge grinding, and impact degradation. Standardized under ASTM C131 / AASHTO T96, the Los Angeles (LA) Abrasion Test evaluates aggregate mechanical toughness and abrasion resistance. A clean, oven-dried 5000 g aggregate sample of specified gradation (Grading A, B, C, or D) is charged into a hollow steel drum (711 mm internal diameter, 508 mm length) along with an abrasive charge of 6 to 12 cast-iron or steel spheres (approx. 46.8 mm diameter, 390 to 445 g each). As the drum rotates at 30 to 33 rpm, an internal radial shelf catches the aggregates and steel spheres, carrying them upward until they drop 700 mm onto the opposite drum wall, inflicting severe impact crushing combined with mutual abrasive friction. After 500 complete revolutions, the aggregate charge is removed, dry-sieved over a 1.70 mm (No. 12) sieve, and washed to determine the loss percentage: LA Loss (%) = [(M_initial - M_retained) / M_initial] · 100. Highway agency specifications restrict LA loss to ≤ 25% for high-speed wearing courses and ≤ 40% for base courses. In this laboratory experiment, degraded fines mass is monitored across revolution increments from 100 to 500 revs to calibrate degradation rate slope = 2.4500 g/rev (LA loss = 24.5%).

Fines = 2.4500 · N

Mechanics of Aggregate Attrition and Impact Degradation

When coarse aggregates are subjected to cyclic tumbling in the Los Angeles drum, particles undergo two distinct degradation modes: (1) brittle fracture and cleavage crushing from high-energy kinetic impacts as the $450\text{ g}$ steel spheres drop $700\text{ mm}$, and (2) surface abrasion and edge attrition as sliding aggregate surfaces grind mutually against each other and the steel shell. The rate of fines generation ($\% < 1.70\text{ mm}$) scales with the number of revolution cycles $N$:

\[ M_{fines}(N) = k_{la} \cdot N \]

where $k_{la}$ is the empirical degradation rate (g/revolution). For an initial dry aggregate charge mass $M_{initial} = 5000.0\text{ g}$, the standard Los Angeles Abrasion Value after $N = 500$ revolutions is:

\[ \text{LA Loss (\%)} = \frac{M_{initial} - M_{retained}}{M_{initial}} \times 100 = \frac{M_{fines}(500)}{5000.0} \times 100 \]

Grading Classifications (ASTM C131)

Standard testing utilizes four standard gradings (A, B, C, and D) based on nominal maximum aggregate size (NMAS):

  • Grading A: $1250\text{ g}$ each of $37.5-25.0\text{ mm}$, $25.0-19.0\text{ mm}$, $19.0-12.5\text{ mm}$, and $12.5-9.5\text{ mm}$ (12 spheres).
  • Grading B: $2500\text{ g}$ each of $19.0-12.5\text{ mm}$ and $12.5-9.5\text{ mm}$ (11 spheres).
  • Grading C: $2500\text{ g}$ each of $9.5-6.3\text{ mm}$ and $6.3-4.75\text{ mm}$ (8 spheres).
  • Grading D: $5000\text{ g}$ of $4.75-2.36\text{ mm}$ (6 spheres).

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the physical significance of the 1.70 mm (No. 12) sieve in the LA Abrasion test?

The 1.70 mm (No. 12) sieve serves as the standardized threshold separating intact mineral aggregate particles from crushed, abraded dust and fractured micro-fines produced by impact and friction during drum rotation.

How do the steel abrasive spheres simulate field pavement distress mechanisms?

The cascading steel balls deliver repetitive heavy kinetic impacts (simulating heavy vibratory roller compaction and dynamic wheel tire pound) combined with attrition grinding between rolling spheres and drum walls (simulating interlock shear under moving wheel traffic).

Why is the aggregate washed over the 1.70 mm sieve after drum testing?

Fine micro-dust generated during grinding clings tenaciously to aggregate particle surfaces via electrostatic and moisture attraction; washing flushes all adherent fine dust through the sieve, ensuring accurate gravimetric loss measurement.

What are typical specification limits for LA Abrasion loss in highway construction?

AASHTO M283 and state DOT specifications typically mandate LA loss ≤ 25% to 30% for high-volume asphalt concrete wearing courses, ≤ 35% for concrete pavements, and ≤ 40% to 50% for crushed aggregate subbase layers.

What is the Micro-Deval test and how does it complement the LA Abrasion test?

The Micro-Deval test (ASTM D6928) abrades aggregates in the presence of water and smaller steel balls (9.5 mm); it evaluates wet abrasion and durability against moisture-induced polishing, whereas the dry LA test evaluates structural impact crushing toughness.

Why does a hollow drum without an internal shelf fail to evaluate aggregate toughness?

Without the internal radial shelf, the aggregate and ball charge would merely slide and roll along the bottom of the drum in gentle slip without being lifted 700 mm and dropped, eliminating the essential impact shock mechanism.

Experimental precautions and laboratory safety

  • Verify that the interior shelf plate of the LA drum is rigid, perpendicular to drum walls, and not worn below 22 mm height.
  • Ensure the steel abrasive charge mass matches ASTM C131 requirements (4584 ± 25 g for 11 spheres) using an analytical balance.
  • Wash the degraded aggregate over the 1.70 mm sieve until wash water is perfectly transparent to avoid underestimating abrasion loss.
  • Tighten the drum hatch clamp securely with gasket seal to prevent hazardous fine silica dust release into laboratory air.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The civil engineering physical models apply within the specified calibrated operational window (100.0 to 500.0 revs).
  • Assumes homogeneous, isotropic continuum material behavior without unmodeled micro-fracture anomalies.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • Extreme inelastic yielding or cyclic degradation requires non-linear constitutive continuum damage mechanics.
