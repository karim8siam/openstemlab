Photoelectric effect: sample lab report

Demonstration report—not a record of a physical experiment.

These five readings were generated with OpenLab’s ideal sodium model and the first zero-current setting at 0.01 V spacing. Use this as a format and calculation guide. Replace the observations with your own measurements and follow your department’s reporting requirements.

1. Title and aim

Determination of Planck’s constant using the photoelectric effect. Measure stopping potential at several known light frequencies for one emitting material, fit a straight line, and estimate Planck’s constant. Estimate the effective work function and threshold frequency from the intercept.

2. Abstract

Five simulated stopping potentials were obtained for wavelengths from 500 to 300 nm at fixed target material and 50% intensity. Linear regression gave a gradient of 4.15224 × 10⁻¹⁵ V s and intercept −2.28878 V, yielding h = 6.65263 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. The estimate differs by 0.401% from the reference value. These values demonstrate the analysis procedure; the simulator itself uses the reference h and is not an independent measurement.

3. Apparatus and settings

  • OpenLab ideal photoelectric simulator; sodium target with representative work function φ = 2.28 eV.
  • Monochromatic source: selected wavelengths 500, 450, 400, 350 and 300 nm; intensity fixed at 50%.
  • Evacuated photocell, collector-bias control and sensitive current meter represented schematically.
  • Collector voltage range: −5 to +2 V; control spacing: 0.01 V. There is no simulated instrument noise.
  • A real laboratory additionally needs the actual photocell, specified light source / filters, variable supply, voltmeter, current meter, connecting leads and the instrument manual.

4. Theory and working equations

A photon carries energy hf. Emission occurs when this energy can overcome the surface work function. The most energetic emitted electrons satisfy Einstein’s energy balance. A retarding voltage stops collection when its electric potential-energy barrier equals that maximum kinetic energy.

hf = φ + Kmax   ·   Kmax = eVs
Vs = (h/e)f − φ/e   ·   f = c/λ

Vs is a positive magnitude. The collector control is negative at the stopping point. For a fit Vs = mf + b with f in Hz, h = em and φ = −eb in joules. Numerically, φ in eV equals −b in volts. Threshold frequency f₀ = −b/m.

Constants: c = 299792458 m s⁻¹; e = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; reference h = 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. The SI values of these constants are exact; the fitted estimate is limited by the observations.

5. Procedure

  1. Select sodium, set intensity to 50%, and set collector voltage to 0 V.
  2. Select 500 nm and check that emission produces a nonzero current.
  3. Make the collector voltage increasingly negative until the collected current first becomes zero.
  4. Increase voltage one 0.01 V step toward zero to verify that current returns. Return to the first zero-current step.
  5. Record the wavelength, frequency, last nonzero voltage and current, first zero voltage, and Vs.
  6. Repeat at 450, 400, 350 and 300 nm while keeping material and intensity fixed.
  7. Plot Vs against f, perform a least-squares fit, calculate h, and inspect residuals and measurement limitations.

7. Worked calculation for one reading

For λ = 400 nm = 400 × 10⁻⁹ m:

f = 299792458 / (400 × 10⁻⁹) = 7.49481145 × 10¹⁴ Hz
Vs = |−0.82| = 0.82 V
Kmax, inferred = eVs = 1.31378484 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 0.82 eV

The boundary is bracketed by −0.81 V with a small nonzero current and −0.82 V with zero current. Thus 0.81 V < Vs,true ≤ 0.82 V for this ideal monotonic model. The notebook records the upper endpoint, 0.82 V.

9. Uncertainty and percentage difference

Percentage difference = |hfit − hreference| / hreference × 100 = 0.4008%. This is a comparison with a reference, not an uncertainty estimate.

Fit scatter only: for n = 5, Σr² = 2.27609 × 10⁻⁵ V². The residual standard deviation is s = √[Σr²/(n − 2)] = 0.00275444 V. The slope standard error on the scaled axis is sa = s/√Σ(x − x̄)² = 0.00087008, yielding sh = esa/10¹⁴ = 1.39402 × 10⁻³⁶ J s.

The regression-only result can be written h = (6.653 ± 0.014) × 10⁻³⁴ J s, where ± denotes one slope standard error. It assumes independent, equal-variance voltage errors and exact frequencies. These assumptions do not describe a complete uncertainty budget for these deterministic rounded data.

Resolution and endpoint bias: the true boundary lies in a 0.01 V interval. Recording its first zero-current endpoint biases each observation upward by between 0 and 0.01 V. Under a uniform-within-bracket approximation, a midpoint estimate would subtract 0.005 V and have standard uncertainty 0.01/√12 ≈ 0.00289 V. Subtracting the same 0.005 V from every reading changes the intercept, but not the fitted slope. This report retains the notebook’s endpoint convention.

In a physical lab, include wavelength uncertainty and bandwidth, voltage calibration, current-meter zero, dark current, contact potentials, surface condition and repeatability. They are omitted from this demonstration; the quoted fit error must not be reported as the full physical uncertainty.

10. Results and discussion

  • Estimated Planck’s constant: 6.653 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; regression-only standard error: 0.014 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.
  • Fitted effective work function: 2.289 eV; threshold frequency: 5.512 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
  • Stopping potential increases approximately linearly with frequency, consistent with the model’s energy balance.
  • The small residuals arise from the voltage step and first-zero convention. They do not demonstrate realistic instrument performance.
  • The fitted work function differs from the model input 2.28 eV because of rounding and the endpoint convention.

11. Precautions and sources of error

Keep the same emitting surface for all readings. Use one known wavelength at a time, distinguish a tiny current from exactly zero in this simulation, bracket the stopping boundary, and maintain a consistent sign convention. In a real laboratory, zero and calibrate instruments, account for background current, follow the equipment manual, and avoid direct exposure to UV sources or laser beams.

12. Conclusion

This worked example shows how stopping-potential observations lead to a graph-based estimate of Planck’s constant. The five simulated readings yield 6.653 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, differing by 0.401% from the reference. The exercise demonstrates the method and its analysis; because the simulator contains the reference constant, it does not independently verify its value.
