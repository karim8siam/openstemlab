Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Fluidized Bed Hydrodynamics.

Fluidized bed reactors and contactors represent pivotal unit operations in catalytic cracking, combustion, gasification, drying, and polymer synthesis. When fluid flows upward through a stationary packed bed of solid particles, viscous and inertial drag forces generate a pressure drop ΔP that escalates with superficial fluid velocity u_0 in accordance with the semi-empirical Ergun equation. As fluid velocity increases, aerodynamic drag on individual particles eventually balances the buoyant gravitational weight of the bed. At this critical juncture, known as the incipient or minimum fluidization velocity u_mf, particles unlock from static contact, the bed unlocks into a fluid-like state, and pressure drop plateaus at a constant value equal to the net buoyant weight per unit cross-sectional area: ΔP_max = (1 - ε_mf) · (ρ_s - ρ_f) · g · L_mf. Further increments in gas velocity cause bed expansion, bubble formation, and vigorous solids mixing with virtually no additional increase in overall pressure drop. In this laboratory investigation, upward air flow through a vertical column packed with glass ballotini particles is monitored to measure the pre-fluidization resistance slope S, identify u_mf, and verify Ergun hydrodynamics.

ΔP = S · u_0 = 0.4250 · u_0

1. Packed and Fluidized Bed Hydrodynamics

Fluid-solid contacting in fixed and fluidized beds represents a cornerstone of modern chemical processing, enabling catalytic reforming, fluidized catalytic cracking (FCC), coal gasification, and bulk drying. The hydrodynamics are governed by the balance between upward aerodynamic drag and gravitational forces acting on the particle inventory.

2. The Ergun Equation for Fixed Beds

For fluid passing through a stationary bed of randomly packed uniform spherical particles of diameter $d_p$ and void fraction $\varepsilon$, the pressure gradient is described by Sabri Ergun's celebrated 1952 equation:

\[ \frac{\Delta P}{L} = 150 \frac{(1 - \varepsilon)^2}{\varepsilon^3} \frac{\mu u_0}{d_p^2} + 1.75 \frac{1 - \varepsilon}{\varepsilon^3} \frac{\rho_f u_0^2}{d_p} \]

where $u_0$ is superficial fluid velocity (volumetric flow rate divided by empty column cross-sectional area $A$). At low particle Reynolds numbers ($Re_p = \rho_f u_0 d_p / \mu < 20$), the second (inertial) term is negligible, yielding the linear Kozeny–Carman viscous relationship:

\[ \Delta P = \left[ 150 \frac{(1 - \varepsilon)^2}{\varepsilon^3} \frac{\mu L}{d_p^2} \right] u_0 = S \cdot u_0 \]

where $S$ represents the fixed bed hydraulic resistance slope.

3. Condition of Incipient Fluidization

As velocity $u_0$ increases, pressure drop rises until the total upward drag force balances the net downward buoyant gravitational weight of the bed. The buoyant weight per unit cross-sectional area is:

\[ \frac{\Delta P_{max}}{A} = \frac{W_{buoyant}}{A} = (1 - \varepsilon_{mf}) (\rho_s - \rho_f) g L_{mf} \]

Equating this maximum pressure drop to the Ergun equation evaluated at minimum fluidization conditions ($u_0 = u_{mf}$) yields a quadratic relation for $u_{mf}$:

\[ 1.75 \frac{\rho_f d_p^2}{\varepsilon_{mf}^3 \mu^2} Re_{mf}^2 + 150 \frac{1 - \varepsilon_{mf}}{\varepsilon_{mf}^3} Re_{mf} = Ar \]

where $Ar = \frac{d_p^3 \rho_f (\rho_s - \rho_f) g}{\mu^2}$ is the dimensionless Archimedes number. For small particles ($Re_{mf} < 20$), neglecting the quadratic term yields the analytical expression:

\[ u_{mf} = \frac{d_p^2 (\rho_s - \rho_f) g}{150 \mu} \frac{\varepsilon_{mf}^3}{1 - \varepsilon_{mf}} \]

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the Ergun equation and what are its viscous and inertial components?

The Ergun equation models pressure drop through packed beds: ΔP/L = 150 μ (1-ε)^2 u_0 / (ε^3 d_p^2) + 1.75 ρ (1-ε) u_0^2 / (ε^3 d_p); the first term accounts for viscous shear (Kozeny–Carman), and the second for inertial kinetic losses (Burke–Plummer).

How is the minimum fluidization velocity u_mf determined experimentally from a ΔP vs u_0 plot?

It is identified as the intersection point between the rising fixed-bed pressure drop curve and the horizontal plateau corresponding to the buoyant bed weight.

What causes the pressure drop overshoot frequently observed before fluidization begins?

Inter-particle frictional interlocking in a settled bed requires an initial force peak to unlock and overcome static friction before transitioning into dynamic suspension.

How does particle Geldart classification (Groups A, B, C, D) influence fluidization behavior?

Geldart Group A (aeratable) expands significantly before bubbling; Group B (sand-like, our glass beads) bubbles directly at u_mf; Group C (cohesive fines) channels poorly; Group D (large particles) forms spouting beds.

What is the terminal settling velocity u_t and how does it relate to bed elutriation?

Terminal velocity u_t is the free-falling velocity of an isolated particle; if superficial gas velocity exceeds u_t, particles are carried out of the column (elutriation), defining the upper operating limit.

Why do fluidized beds achieve exceptionally high heat and mass transfer coefficients?

Intense solid particle circulation and continuous boundary layer renewal around suspended particles produce rapid thermal and mass equilibration, eliminating hot spots in exothermic catalytic reactions.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Ensure the distributor plate is level and clean to prevent maldistribution, channeling, or dead zones.
  • Increase air flow gradually to avoid sudden bed slugging or spitting beads into the exhaust cyclone.
  • Ground the acrylic column with a copper wire to dissipate electrostatic charges generated by particle tribocharging.
  • Always take bed height measurements at steady state to account for thermal and expansion dynamics.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (2.0 to 12.0 cm/s).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
