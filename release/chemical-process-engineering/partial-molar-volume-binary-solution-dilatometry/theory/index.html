Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Partial Molar Volume Dilatometry.

In chemical solution thermodynamics, extensive thermodynamic properties—including total volume V, enthalpy H, entropy S, and Gibbs free energy G—depend not only on temperature and pressure but also on mixture composition. For a non-ideal binary mixture, the total volume is not simply the sum of the pure component volumes due to differences in molecular packing, interstitial cavity filling, and intermolecular hydrogen bonding. The partial molar volume of component i, defined as V_bar_i = (∂V / ∂n_i)_{T, P, n_j≠i}, represents the change in total mixture volume per mole of component i added at constant temperature, pressure, and remaining component amounts. By Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions of degree 1, the total volume of a binary solution containing n_1 moles of solvent (water) and n_2 moles of solute (ethanol) is given by: V = n_1 · V_bar_1 + n_2 · V_bar_2. Dividing by total moles yields the mean molar volume: V_m = x_1 · V_bar_1 + x_2 · V_bar_2. In dilute and moderately concentrated solutions, experimental data are analyzed through the apparent molar volume phi_V: phi_V = (V - n_1 · V_1^*) / n_2 = (M_2 / ρ) - [(ρ - ρ_1^*) / (m · ρ · ρ_1^*)], where M_2 is solute molar mass, m is molality, and ρ and ρ_1^* are solution and pure solvent densities. In the dilute alcohol concentration regime (x_EtOH = 0.05 to 0.30), the apparent molar volume increases linearly with mole fraction according to the Redlich–Meyer and Mason relations: phi_V = phi_V0 + S · x_EtOH, where phi_V0 is the infinite-dilution partial molar volume of ethanol in water (representing an isolated ethanol molecule surrounded purely by a cage of hydrogen-bonded water molecules, often exhibiting hydrophobic hydration) and S is the volumetric interaction slope. In this laboratory investigation, precision Gay-Lussac pycnometers and capillary dilatometers are operated at 25.000 ± 0.005 °C to measure densities across x_EtOH from 0.05 to 0.30, evaluate phi_V, and determine the infinite-dilution volume phi_V0 = 54.2000 cm^3/mol and slope S = 14.5000 cm^3/mol.

phi_V = S · x_EtOH + phi_V0 = 14.5000 · x_EtOH + 54.2000

Thermodynamic Fundamentals of Partial Molar Quantities

An extensive thermodynamic property $Y$ (such as volume $V$, internal energy $U$, enthalpy $H$, entropy $S$, or Gibbs energy $G$) of a multicomponent homogeneous mixture depends on temperature $T$, pressure $P$, and the mole numbers of all constituents $n_1, n_2, \dots, n_C$. The partial molar property of component $i$ is defined as the partial derivative of $Y$ with respect to $n_i$ at constant $T$, $P$, and other component amounts:

\[ \bar{Y}_i = \left( \frac{\partial Y}{\partial n_i} \right)_{T, P, n_{j \neq i}} \]

Because $Y$ is a homogeneous function of degree 1 with respect to mole numbers, Euler's theorem dictates:

\[ Y = \sum_{i=1}^C n_i \, \bar{Y}_i \implies Y_m = \frac{Y}{n_t} = \sum_{i=1}^C x_i \, \bar{Y}_i \]

For a binary solution of solvent 1 (water) and solute 2 (ethanol), the total volume and mean molar volume are:

\[ V = n_1 \, \bar{V}_1 + n_2 \, \bar{V}_2, \quad V_m = x_1 \, \bar{V}_1 + x_2 \, \bar{V}_2 \]

Apparent Molar Volume $\phi_V$ Formalism

While partial molar volumes are defined differentially, experimentalists evaluate volumetric behavior through the apparent molar volume $\phi_V$, which assigns all volumetric deviation from pure solvent ($V_1^* = M_1 / \rho_1^*$) to the solute:

\[ \phi_V = \frac{V - n_1 V_1^*}{n_2} = \frac{V_m - x_1 V_1^*}{x_2} \]

Expressed in terms of measured solution density $\rho$, pure solvent density $\rho_1^*$, solute molar mass $M_2$, and solution molality $m$ (moles solute per kg solvent):

\[ \phi_V = \frac{M_2}{\rho} - \frac{1000 \, (\rho - \rho_1^*)}{m \, \rho \, \rho_1^*} \]

At infinite dilution ($x_2 \to 0$, $m \to 0$), apparent molar volume equals the infinite-dilution partial molar volume:

\[ \lim_{x_2 \to 0} \phi_V = \bar{V}_2^\infty = \phi_{V0} \]

Linear Concentration Dependence & Redlich–Meyer Scaling

In dilute and moderately concentrated alcohol-water solutions ($x_{\text{EtOH}} \le 0.30$), apparent molar volume increases linearly with mole fraction due to solute-solute hydrophobic pair interactions:

\[ \phi_V = S \cdot x_{\text{EtOH}} + \phi_{V0} = 14.5000 \cdot x_{\text{EtOH}} + 54.2000 \]

where $\phi_{V0} = 54.2000\text{ cm}^3\text{/mol}$ represents the partial molar volume of ethanol at infinite dilution and $S = 14.5000\text{ cm}^3\text{/mol}$ is the volumetric interaction slope.

The true partial molar volume of ethanol is related to apparent molar volume by:

\[ \bar{V}_2 = \phi_V + x_2 \, (1 - x_2) \left( \frac{\partial \phi_V}{\partial x_2} \right)_{T, P} = \phi_V + S \, x_2 \, (1 - x_2) \]

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the physical meaning of a partial molar property?

A partial molar property represents the contribution per mole of that component to the total extensive property of the mixture; it reflects how the thermodynamic property changes when an infinitesimal amount of that component is added at constant T, P, and other compositions.

Why is the partial molar volume of ethanol at infinite dilution (54.2 cm^3/mol) smaller than pure ethanol molar volume (58.7 cm^3/mol)?

When an ethanol molecule enters pure water, the small water molecules arrange into an ordered, clathrate-like cage around the hydrophobic ethyl tail (hydrophobic hydration), and the hydroxyl group forms strong hydrogen bonds that fit interstitially into the water network, causing net volume contraction.

How is the tangent intercept method used to determine both partial molar volumes from mean molar volume data?

By plotting mean molar volume V_m versus mole fraction x_2, drawing a tangent line at any composition x_2 intersects the y-axis at x_1=1 (x_2=0) at the partial molar volume V_bar_1, and intersects the vertical line at x_2=1 at the partial molar volume V_bar_2.

Why must buoyancy corrections be applied when weighing pycnometers on an analytical balance?

Air has a finite density (~0.0012 g/cm^3 at 20 °C); because the volume of the glass pycnometer and brass calibration weights displace air unequally, uncorrected weights introduce errors of 0.1% in density, which severely distorts apparent molar volumes.

What is excess molar volume V^E and what sign does it have for ethanol-water?

Excess molar volume V^E = V_m - (x_1 V_1^* + x_2 V_2^*) is the difference between actual solution volume and ideal solution volume; for ethanol-water mixtures, V^E is negative across the entire composition range (reaching a minimum of ~-1.1 cm^3/mol at x_EtOH ≈ 0.4) due to strong hydrogen bonding and interstitial packing.

How does temperature affect the partial molar volume of water and ethanol?

As temperature increases, thermal expansion increases both pure and partial molar volumes; however, because elevated thermal energy disrupts the rigid hydrogen-bonded water structure, the extent of excess volume contraction (|V^E|) decreases with rising temperature.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Ensure the pycnometer exterior is wiped completely dry with lint-free paper before weighing, avoiding warming the vessel with bare fingers.
  • Degas all deionized water and ethanol feedstocks under vacuum sonication to eliminate dissolved air bubbles that adhere to pycnometer walls.
  • Seat the capillary ground-glass stopper firmly with a single smooth axial motion to prevent trapping air bubbles inside the capillary bore.
  • Maintain the thermostatic water bath strictly within ±0.005 °C, as water-ethanol density changes by ~0.0008 g/cm^3 per degree Celsius.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (0.05 to 0.3 mol/mol).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
