Probabilistic foundations of Law of Large Numbers.

The Law of Large Numbers is a bedrock foundation of probability theory and statistical inference, guaranteeing that the arithmetic average of independent, identically distributed random variables converges to the population expectation. The Weak Law establishes convergence in probability, while the Strong Law guarantees almost sure convergence along individual sample-path trajectories. In this laboratory, we simulate thousands of independent random walks, monitor running sample means across diverse distributions, demonstrate the breakdown of convergence on heavy-tailed Cauchy variables, and experimentally verify the linear precision scaling 1/Var(X̄) ∝ N. In mathematical statistics, empirical data science, and modern computational engineering, understanding the asymptotic sampling properties and theoretical behavior of Weak and Strong Law of Large Numbers (Bernoulli & Khinchin) provides crucial guarantees. Rigorous experimental validation connects foundational probability laws with observable empirical phenomena, ensuring that statistical inference remains valid under finite sample sizes and real-world data collection constraints.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN), and how is it proven using Chebyshev's inequality?

The Weak Law of Large Numbers (Khinchin's theorem) states that if X_1, X_2, ... are i.i.d. random variables with finite mean μ and variance σ^2 < ∞, then for any ε > 0, lim_{n→∞} P(|X̄_n - μ| ≥ ε) = 0. This is convergence in probability (X̄_n →^P μ). Proof: E[X̄_n] = μ and Var(X̄_n) = σ^2 / n. Applying Chebyshev's inequality: P(|X̄_n - μ| ≥ ε) ≤ Var(X̄_n) / ε^2 = σ^2 / (n ε^2). As n → ∞, σ^2 / (n ε^2) → 0, establishing that the probability of any deviation strictly vanishes.

How does the Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN) differ fundamentally from the Weak Law?

The Strong Law of Large Numbers (Kolmogorov's theorem) establishes almost sure convergence: P(lim_{n→∞} X̄_n = μ) = 1. While the Weak Law states that for any given large n, the probability of finding a deviation is small (pointwise in time), the Strong Law guarantees that the entire sample path trajectory X̄_n stays confined within (μ - ε, μ + ε) for all n beyond some random index N_0 with probability 1. Almost sure convergence is a pathwise topological property and is strictly stronger than convergence in probability.

What is Kolmogorov's criterion for the Strong Law of Large Numbers?

Andrey Kolmogorov (1930) proved that for independent random variables X_n with variances σ_n^2, if ∑_{n=1}^∞ (σ_n^2 / n^2) < ∞, then the Strong Law of Large Numbers holds: (S_n - E[S_n]) / n → 0 almost surely. For i.i.d. variables, Kolmogorov proved that the existence of a finite first moment E[|X|] < ∞ is both necessary and sufficient for the Strong Law of Large Numbers to hold, requiring no assumptions about finite variance.

Why does the Law of Large Numbers fail for the Cauchy distribution?

The standard Cauchy distribution has probability density f(x) = 1 / [π(1 + x^2)]. The characteristic function of a Cauchy variable is φ_X(t) = exp(-|t|). For the sample mean X̄_n = (1/n) ∑ X_i, the characteristic function is φ_{X̄_n}(t) = [φ_X(t/n)]^n = [exp(-|t/n|)]^n = exp(-|t|). Because the characteristic function of the average of n Cauchy variables is identical to that of a single Cauchy variable, X̄_n has exactly the same Cauchy distribution for all n! As n increases, the sample mean does not concentrate around any constant; it continues to fluctuate wildly across the entire real line because the expected value integral diverges.

What is the Law of the Iterated Logarithm (LIL), and how does it describe the fluctuations of the sample mean?

Alexander Khinchin (1924) established the Law of the Iterated Logarithm, which describes the precise boundary of oscillations of partial sums S_n: limsup_{n→∞} (S_n - nμ) / √(2 n σ^2 ln(ln(n))) = +1 and liminf_{n→∞} (S_n - nμ) / √(2 n σ^2 ln(ln(n))) = -1 almost surely. This proves that the sample mean X̄_n fluctuates around μ within an envelope of width ± σ √(2 ln(ln(n)) / n). The term √(ln(ln(n))) grows with extreme slowness, forming the exact mathematical barrier between the central limit theorem rate (O(1/√n)) and the strong law.

What are empirical applications of the Law of Large Numbers in Monte Carlo integration and insurance?

In Monte Carlo numerical integration, an unknown multidimensional integral I = ∫ g(x) dx is estimated as the sample average (1/N) ∑ g(X_i). The LLN guarantees that this empirical average converges to the true integral as N → ∞. In actuarial science and insurance, while individual claims are unpredictable random events, an insurer pooling N independent policies experiences an average payout per policy X̄_N that converges to the expected loss E[X], allowing insurers to set stable premiums with minimal risk of insolvency.

How does the variance of the sample mean scale with sample size N for uncorrelated variables?

For any uncorrelated random variables with common variance σ^2: Var(X̄_N) = Var((1/N) ∑ X_i) = (1/N^2) ∑ Var(X_i) = (1/N^2) (N σ^2) = σ^2 / N. Taking the reciprocal defines estimation precision: Precision = 1 / Var(X̄_N) = (1 / σ^2) · N. When σ = 1, Precision = N, defining an exact linear relation y = 1.0000 · x with slope m = 1.0000 and intercept b = 0.0000.

Experimental precautions and statistical checks

  • Ensure that pseudo-random generators have sufficiently long cycle periods (e.g. 2^19937 - 1) to prevent periodic artifact correlation.
  • Verify that parent distributions possess finite mathematical expectations before testing LLN convergence.
  • Distinguish clearly between cross-sectional variance across replications and sequential sample path convergence.
  • Perform replication across multiple independent pseudorandom seeds to verify that estimator variance and bias bounds are invariant to initial generator states.

Theoretical assumptions & limitations

  • The LLN guarantees asymptotic convergence as N → ∞ but provides no error bounds for any single finite sample size N.
  • Correlated or non-stationary time series may violate the standard i.i.d. assumptions of the classical Law of Large Numbers.
