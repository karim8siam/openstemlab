Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Cake Filtration & Specific Resistance.

Solid–liquid filtration is an indispensable mechanical separation unit operation in chemical processing, mineral ore beneficiation, water clarification, and pharmaceutical crystallizer product recovery. In cake filtration, a slurry of insoluble solid particles is forced under pressure across a porous filter medium (woven cloth or membrane). While the medium initially captures particles, a porous permeable bed of accumulated solids—the filter cake—rapidly builds up on the cloth surface, becoming the dominant filtration medium. Flow of filtrate liquid through the growing porous cake obeys Darcy's law applied in series with the filter cloth. Burrows F. Ruth (1933) formulated the foundational parabolic filtration model for constant-pressure operation: dV/dt = [A^2 · ΔP] / [μ · (c · α · V + R_m · A)], where V is cumulative filtrate volume, A is filter area, ΔP is applied pressure drop, μ is filtrate dynamic viscosity, c is dry cake mass per unit filtrate volume, α is the average specific cake resistance (m/kg), and R_m is filter medium resistance (m^-1). Integrating this differential equation under constant differential pressure ΔP yields Ruth's celebrated linear relation: t / V = (K_p / 2) · V + B, where slope S = K_p / 2 = (μ · c · α) / (2 · A^2 · ΔP) and intercept B = (μ · R_m) / (A · ΔP). In this laboratory experiment, a constant vacuum (-60.0 kPa) is applied to filter an aqueous calcium carbonate slurry across a 150 mm circular filter leaf, logging t vs V across 0.5 to 3.0 L to evaluate slope S and determine specific cake resistance α.

t/V = S · V + B = 18.5000 · V + 4.2000

1. Mechanical Theory of Cake Filtration

Cake filtration is a pressure-driven separation process where solid particles in a slurry are arrested by a permeable medium, forming an expanding porous bed (filter cake). Subsequent filtrate liquid must percolate through both the accumulated cake and the underlying cloth medium.

2. Darcy's Law Applied to Cake and Medium Resistances

Flow of filtrate through the porous cake obeys Darcy's filtration law in series with the filter medium resistance:

\[ \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{A \Delta P}{\mu (R_{cake} + R_m)} \]

where $V$ is cumulative filtrate volume, $A$ is filter area, $\Delta P$ is total pressure drop, and $\mu$ is filtrate viscosity. The resistance of the cake layer $R_{cake}$ is proportional to the dry cake mass per unit area:

\[ R_{cake} = \frac{\alpha m_c}{A} = \frac{\alpha c V}{A} \]

where $\alpha$ is the average specific cake resistance ($\text{m/kg}$) and $c$ is dry cake mass deposited per unit filtrate volume ($\text{kg/m}^3$).

3. Derivation of Ruth's Constant-Pressure Filtration Equation

Substituting $R_{cake}$ into Darcy's law yields:

\[ \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{A \Delta P}{\mu \left( \frac{\alpha c V}{A} + R_m \right)} = \frac{A^2 \Delta P}{\mu c \alpha V + \mu A R_m} \]

Separating variables and integrating from $t = 0, V = 0$ to time $t$ and volume $V$ under constant pressure $\Delta P$:

\[ \int_0^V (\mu c \alpha V + \mu A R_m) dV = \int_0^t A^2 \Delta P dt \] \[ \frac{\mu c \alpha}{2} V^2 + \mu A R_m V = A^2 \Delta P \cdot t \]

Dividing both sides by $A^2 \Delta P \cdot V$ yields Ruth's linear equation:

\[ \frac{t}{V} = \left[ \frac{\mu c \alpha}{2 A^2 \Delta P} \right] V + \left[ \frac{\mu R_m}{A \Delta P} \right] = S \cdot V + B \]

where slope $S = \frac{\mu c \alpha}{2 A^2 \Delta P}$ and intercept $B = \frac{\mu R_m}{A \Delta P}$. Specific cake resistance $\alpha$ and medium resistance $R_m$ are directly determined from regression parameters $S$ and $B$:

\[ \alpha = \frac{2 S A^2 \Delta P}{\mu c}, \quad R_m = \frac{B A \Delta P}{\mu} \]

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the physical meaning of specific cake resistance α and what are its units?

Specific cake resistance α (units m/kg) represents the hydraulic resistance to fluid flow per unit dry mass of deposited cake per unit filtration area; it depends on particle size, shape, packing voidage, and cake compressibility.

How do compressible and incompressible filter cakes differ under increasing filtration pressure?

For an incompressible cake (e.g., sand, rigid CaCO_3), cake voidage and resistance α remain constant regardless of pressure (α = α_0); for a compressible cake (e.g., sewage sludge, protein precipitates), increasing pressure crushes pores, escalating α according to α = α_0 (ΔP)^s, where s is compressibility.

What is the role of filter aids (such as diatomaceous earth or perlite) in industrial filtration?

Filter aids consist of rigid, highly porous, microscopic particles added to gelatinous or compressible slurries to build an open, incompressible cake matrix, preventing cloth blinding and drastically reducing specific cake resistance.

Why does the Ruth plot (t/V versus V) have a non-zero intercept B?

The intercept B = (μ R_m) / (A ΔP) represents the initial hydraulic resistance of the clean filter cloth and support piping before any cake layer has accumulated (at V = 0).

How does a continuous rotary drum vacuum filter (RDVF) utilize the cake filtration model?

An RDVF submerges a rotating drum in a slurry trough, dividing the cycle into cake formation, cake washing, vacuum dewatering, and knife blade cake discharge; the Ruth equation governs the formation zone cycle time.

What causes filter cloth blinding and how is it mitigated?

Blinding occurs when fine particles penetrate into the pores of the filter cloth and lodge permanently, causing cloth resistance R_m to spike; it is mitigated by selecting proper cloth weave, pre-coating with filter aid, or periodic backwashing.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Maintain continuous slurry agitation to prevent particle sedimentation from altering feed concentration during filtration.
  • Ensure vacuum receiver pressure remains strictly constant at 60.0 kPa throughout the run.
  • Seat the filter cloth tightly under the clamp ring to prevent edge bypass leakage.
  • Record filtrate volume exactly at eye level to eliminate parallax errors.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (0.5 to 3.0 L).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
