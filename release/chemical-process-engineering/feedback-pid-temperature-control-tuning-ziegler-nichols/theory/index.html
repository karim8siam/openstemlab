Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of PID Temperature Control Tuning.

Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) feedback control is the foundational regulatory control algorithm deployed in over 95% of industrial chemical, refining, and manufacturing process loops. In continuous thermal processing—such as jacketed chemical reactors, shell-and-tube exchangers, and reboilers—temperature must be tightly regulated against influent thermal and flow disturbances. The parallel PID controller computes control output u(t) based on the error e(t) = y_sp(t) - y(t): u(t) = u_bias + K_c · [e(t) + (1/τ_I) ∫ e(t') dt' + τ_D · (de/dt)], where K_c is proportional gain, τ_I is integral reset time, and τ_D is derivative rate time. In 1942, John G. Ziegler and Nathaniel B. Nichols introduced the closed-loop ultimate oscillation method (Ziegler–Nichols continuous cycling method) based on frequency response stability margins. In this protocol, integral and derivative actions are deactivated (τ_I -> inf, τ_D = 0), and the proportional gain K_c is systematically increased until the closed-loop system reaches the boundary of marginal stability. At this stability limit, the Nyquist contour of the open-loop transfer function intersects the critical point (-1, 0) in the complex plane, satisfying the Barkhausen stability criteria: loop magnitude |G_OL(j ω_u)| = 1.0 and phase angle ∠G_OL(j ω_u) = -180°. The closed-loop system exhibits sustained, non-decaying sinusoidal oscillations of constant amplitude (amplitude ratio AR = y_peak,n+1 / y_peak,n = 1.000). The corresponding controller gain is defined as the ultimate gain K_u, and the oscillation cycle time is the ultimate period P_u. For linear processes operating near stability, the closed-loop oscillation amplitude ratio scales directly with proportional gain: AR = S · K_c, where S = 1 / K_u = 0.1250 (%/%)^-1. Thus, at AR = 1.000, the ultimate gain is evaluated as K_u = 1 / S = 8.0000 %/%. The classical Ziegler–Nichols tuning rules then specify controller settings: for PID, K_c = 0.60 · K_u = 4.800, τ_I = 0.50 · P_u, and τ_D = 0.125 · P_u, providing aggressive disturbance rejection with quarter-amplitude damping. In this laboratory experiment, an instrumented 2.0-liter continuous thermal process cell equipped with a 500 W electric immersion heater and Pt100 RTD sensor is tested across controller gains from 2.0 to 12.0 to measure AR, extract K_u = 8.0000, and evaluate optimal PID settings.

Feedback Control Loop Mathematics & PID Algorithm

In negative feedback control, the controller monitors the controlled process variable $y(t)$, compares it to the desired setpoint $y_{\text{sp}}(t)$, and computes the error signal $e(t) = y_{\text{sp}}(t) - y(t)$. The three-mode ideal continuous parallel PID control law is expressed as:

\[ u(t) = u_{\text{bias}} + K_c \left[ e(t) + \frac{1}{\tau_I} \int_0^t e(t') \, d t' + \tau_D \, \frac{d e(t)}{d t} \right] \]

where $K_c$ is controller gain, $\tau_I$ is integral time, and $\tau_D$ is derivative time. In Laplace domain:

\[ G_c(s) = \frac{U(s)}{E(s)} = K_c \left( 1 + \frac{1}{\tau_I s} + \tau_D s \right) \]

The closed-loop servo transfer function for a process $G_p(s)$ with final control element $G_v(s)$ and measurement sensor $G_m(s)$ is:

\[ \frac{Y(s)}{Y_{\text{sp}}(s)} = \frac{G_c(s) G_v(s) G_p(s)}{1 + G_c(s) G_v(s) G_p(s) G_m(s)} = \frac{G_{\text{OL}}(s)}{1 + G_{\text{OL}}(s)} \]

Frequency Response & Stability Boundary Criteria

The closed-loop system is marginally stable if the open-loop transfer function satisfies the Barkhausen criteria at the critical frequency $\omega_u$:

\[ |G_{\text{OL}}(j \omega_u)| = 1.0, \quad \angle G_{\text{OL}}(j \omega_u) = -180^\circ \]

Under pure proportional control ($G_c(s) = K_c$), the controller gain that forces the Nyquist trajectory through the critical point $(-1, 0)$ is the ultimate gain $K_u$, and the oscillation frequency is $\omega_u = 2 \pi / P_u$, where $P_u$ is the ultimate period.

Linear Amplitude Ratio Scaling & Ziegler–Nichols Rules

Near the stability boundary, the closed-loop oscillation amplitude ratio $AR = A_{n+1} / A_n$ of successive peaks scales directly with proportional gain $K_c$:

\[ AR = S \cdot K_c = 0.1250 \cdot K_c \]

where $S = 1 / K_u = 0.1250\text{ (%/%)}^{-1}$. At marginal stability ($AR = 1.000$):

\[ K_u = \frac{1}{S} = \frac{1}{0.1250} = 8.0000\text{ %/%} \]

The classical Ziegler–Nichols closed-loop tuning formulas provide the controller parameters for quarter-amplitude decay:

\[ \text{P Control: } K_c = 0.50 \, K_u = 4.0000 \] \[ \text{PI Control: } K_c = 0.45 \, K_u = 3.6000, \quad \tau_I = \frac{P_u}{1.2} \] \[ \text{PID Control: } K_c = 0.60 \, K_u = 4.8000, \quad \tau_I = 0.50 \, P_u, \quad \tau_D = 0.125 \, P_u \]

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the physical interpretation of the ultimate gain K_u and ultimate period P_u?

Ultimate gain K_u is the maximum proportional gain that a closed-loop system can tolerate before becoming unstable; ultimate period P_u is the period of the sustained sinusoidal oscillation at this stability limit, corresponding to the critical frequency ω_u = 2π / P_u where loop phase lag reaches -180°.

How does dead time (time delay θ) destabilize feedback control loops?

Dead time introduces a phase lag that increases linearly with frequency: ∠G_delay = -θ · ω; this rapid phase roll-off causes the loop phase to cross -180° at much lower frequencies, severely reducing the allowable controller gain K_c before instability occurs.

What is quarter-amplitude damping (1/4 decay ratio) in Ziegler–Nichols tuning?

Quarter-amplitude damping means each successive peak of the transient oscillation following a step disturbance is exactly one-fourth the height of the preceding peak (A_2 / A_1 = 0.25), corresponding to a damping ratio of approximately ζ ≈ 0.21, providing a rapid compromise between rise time and settling time.

Why does pure proportional control produce a steady-state offset (error)?

A proportional controller requires a non-zero error to generate non-zero corrective output (u - u_bias = K_c · e); when a sustained load disturbance occurs, the error cannot return to zero without the controller output reverting to its original value, requiring integral action to eliminate the offset.

What is integral windup and how do industrial controllers prevent it?

When a large setpoint change or sustained error causes the controller output to saturate at 100% or 0%, the integral term continues to accumulate (wind up); when the process finally crosses setpoint, the accumulated integral causes severe overshoot; anti-reset windup algorithms freeze integration whenever the actuator reaches its physical limits.

Why is derivative action sensitive to high-frequency measurement noise?

The derivative term is proportional to de/dt = d(y_sp - y)/dt; high-frequency electrical or sensor noise has extremely large time derivatives (d[sin(ωt)]/dt = ω cos(ωt)), producing erratic control output chatter; practical PID controllers add a first-order low-pass derivative filter with time constant τ_f = τ_D / N (where N ≈ 8 to 20).

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Never leave the immersion heater energized without active water flow and agitation to prevent element burnout and thermal degradation.
  • Limit high-gain testing (K_c = 12.0 %/%) to brief periods to prevent excessive thermal cycling and mechanical stress on the vessel.
  • Verify that temperature sensor immersion depth is at least 10 sheath diameters to eliminate conductive heat loss errors along the probe stem.
  • Ensure over-temperature safety cut-out switch is set to 65.0 °C to prevent accidental boiling and steam generation.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (2.0 to 12.0 %/%).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
