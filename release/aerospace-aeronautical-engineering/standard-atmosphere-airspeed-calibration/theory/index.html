Aerodynamic and physical foundations of ISA Airspeed Calibration.

Accurate flight telemetry and navigation require precise conversion between what the aircraft's Pitot-static instruments measure and the actual physical speed of the aircraft through the air mass. Pitot-static airspeed indicators measure dynamic pressure q = 0.5 * rho * V^2, but display airspeed calibrated against sea-level standard atmospheric density rho_0 = 1.225 kg/m^3. This raw reading, known as Indicated Airspeed (IAS), is subject to instrument position errors, yielding Calibrated Airspeed (CAS). Compressibility at high subsonic speeds causes dynamic pressure to exceed the incompressible Bernoulli value, requiring a compressibility correction to obtain Equivalent Airspeed (EAS): q = 0.5 * rho_0 * V_EAS^2. Finally, because ambient air density rho decreases with altitude according to the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA) barometric lapse rate, True Airspeed (TAS) relates to Equivalent Airspeed through the atmospheric density ratio sigma = rho / rho_0: V_TAS = V_EAS / sqrt(sigma). In this laboratory experiment, a flight test environmental chamber simulates atmospheric altitudes from sea level (0 m) to the tropopause (11,000 m). By measuring static pressure and temperature, students evaluate density ratio sigma and verify the linear scaling between V_TAS and the density parameter 1 / sqrt(sigma) at a constant EAS of 70.0 m/s.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the difference between Indicated Airspeed (IAS), Calibrated Airspeed (CAS), and True Airspeed (TAS)?

IAS is the raw reading from the cockpit airspeed indicator. CAS is IAS corrected for instrument and position (static port location) errors. TAS is the true speed of the aircraft relative to the surrounding air mass, obtained by correcting CAS for altitude density ratio (TAS = EAS / sqrt(sigma)).

What is Equivalent Airspeed (EAS) and why is it vital for structural load calculations?

EAS is the airspeed at sea level that would produce the same dynamic pressure q = 0.5 * rho_0 * V_EAS^2 as the aircraft experiences at altitude. Because aerodynamic lift, drag, and structural wing bending loads depend directly on dynamic pressure, aerodynamic limit speeds (such as V_NE and stall speed) remain nearly constant in terms of EAS.

How does the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA) define temperature variation with altitude in the troposphere?

The ISA defines a linear negative temperature lapse rate of -6.5 K per kilometer (-0.0065 K/m) from sea level (T_0 = 288.15 K) up to the tropopause at 11,000 meters (T = 216.65 K), above which temperature remains isothermal up to 20,000 meters.

Why does True Airspeed increase with altitude for a constant Indicated Airspeed?

As altitude increases, air density rho decreases. To generate the same dynamic pressure q = 0.5 * rho * V_TAS^2 required to maintain the indicated airspeed and support the aircraft's weight, the aircraft must physically fly faster through the thinner air: V_TAS = V_IAS / sqrt(sigma).

What is the 'coffin corner' in high-altitude aircraft flight?

As altitude increases, the stall speed (which depends on dynamic pressure and EAS) increases in terms of True Airspeed and Mach number. Simultaneously, the critical Mach number M_crit drops. At extreme altitudes, the low-speed stall boundary and high-speed shock-buffet boundary converge into a dangerously narrow operating airspeed corridor termed the 'coffin corner'.

How does an Air Data Computer (ADC) calculate Mach number from pitot-static measurements?

The ADC measures impact pressure q_c = P_0 - P and static pressure P. In subsonic flow, applying Bernoulli's compressible equation yields Mach number directly from the pressure ratio: M = sqrt(5 * [((q_c / P) + 1)^(2/7) - 1]), independent of ambient air temperature.

Experimental precautions and wind tunnel safety

  • Allow temperature inside the altitude chamber to equilibrate for 15 minutes after pressure reduction before recording LDA velocity.
  • Verify that vacuum seals around Pitot-static test duct lines are leak-free to avoid artificial position errors.
  • Ensure the Pitot probe is heated (Pitot heat active) if chamber temperatures drop below 0 C to prevent ice accretion.
  • Check that pressure transducers are zeroed at atmospheric pressure prior to each pump-down cycle.

Theoretical assumptions & flow limits

  • Tropospheric linear temperature lapse rate (-6.5 K/km) applies up to 11,000 m; isothermal conditions prevail in the lower stratosphere.
  • Compressibility effects on dynamic pressure are small at 70 m/s (Mach M < 0.25), but grow significant above 150 m/s.
  • Real atmospheric humidity slightly reduces air density compared to dry standard atmosphere assumptions.
  • Chamber wall boundary layers introduce minor acoustic reflections during high-speed blower operation.
