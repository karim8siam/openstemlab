Biomedical and physiological foundations of Gait Analysis Ground Reaction Force.

Instrumented human gait analysis provides objective, quantitative biomechanical data essential for diagnosing cerebral palsy crouch gait, evaluating total knee arthroplasty functional recovery, optimizing prosthetic foot design, and assessing athletic performance. When a human walks across a floor, the foot exerts forces against the ground, and by Newton's third law, the ground exerts an equal and opposite Ground Reaction Force (GRF) vector back upon the body. In a gait laboratory, flush-mounted multi-axis force plates equipped with triaxial piezoelectric quartz or strain gauge load cells measure three orthogonal force components: vertical (F_z), anteroposterior braking/propulsion (F_y), and mediolateral (F_x), as well as center of pressure (COP) coordinates. During the stance phase of walking, the vertical GRF displays a characteristic bimodal 'M-shape' profile: an initial peak corresponding to heel strike and weight acceptance, a mid-stance trough during contralateral swing phase, and a second peak during terminal stance push-off. As walking velocity v_w increases, greater downward momentum and leg acceleration elevate the peak vertical ground reaction force. When normalized to subject body weight (BW = m·g), empirical biomechanical data demonstrate that peak vertical GRF F_v/BW scales linearly with walking velocity: F_v/BW = k_v · v_w + F_v0, where k_v is the velocity sensitivity scaling factor (BW/(m/s)). In this laboratory investigation, an instrumented walkway tracks force plate telemetry across controlled walking speeds to quantify vertical force scaling.

F_v/BW = k_v · v_w + F_v0 = 0.2800 · v_w + 0.8200

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the biomechanical cause of the bimodal 'M-shape' vertical GRF curve in walking?

The first peak reflects downward deceleration of the body center of mass during heel strike and weight acceptance; the central dip corresponds to upward center-of-mass elevation during mid-stance vaulting; and the second peak reflects active ankle plantarflexion push-off.

Why must force plate data be normalized to body weight (BW)?

Absolute ground reaction forces scale directly with individual body mass; dividing forces by body weight (BW = m·g) yields dimensionless metrics that enable objective comparison across pediatric, adult, and prosthetic patient populations.

What is 'targeting' and why does it invalidate gait analysis data?

Targeting occurs when a subject visually notices the force plate and artificially alters their natural stride length, cadence, or foot placement to hit the platform, creating unphysiological kinematic and kinetic artifacts.

What do the anteroposterior (F_y) forces represent during the stance phase?

During the first half of stance, the foot pushes forward against the plate, creating a negative braking force that decelerates the body; during the second half, the foot pushes backward, generating a positive propulsion force that accelerates the body forward.

How does Center of Pressure (COP) inform postural stability and joint moment calculation?

COP represents the spatial centroid of all contact pressures beneath the foot sole; combining COP coordinates with the 3D GRF vector allows inverse dynamics calculations of ankle, knee, and hip net muscle moments.

How does running GRF differ from walking GRF?

Running has no double-support phase and exhibits a unimodal vertical GRF profile with a rapid heel-impact transient spike followed by a massive active peak reaching 2.5 to 3.0 times body weight.

Experimental precautions and patient safety

  • Ensure force platform is mounted flush and mechanically isolated from the surrounding walkway floor to avoid foot impact vibrations.
  • Discard any trial where the foot partially misses the plate or where visible step-targeting occurs.
  • Filter raw 1000 Hz force data with a low-pass zero-lag 4th-order Butterworth filter (cutoff 20 Hz) to eliminate high-frequency plate resonance.
  • Verify timing gate alignment and ensure subjects do not accelerate or decelerate within the 3-meter capture zone.

Theoretical assumptions & biological limits

  • The linear velocity model applies across normal walking speeds (0.8 to 1.8 m/s); running involves a discrete gait transition with different mechanics.
  • Assumes level overground walking, neglecting inclines, stair ambulation, or uneven outdoor terrain.
  • Force plates capture external kinetics only; calculating internal joint forces requires inverse dynamics with optical motion capture.
  • Subject footwear (athletic shoes vs. barefoot) alters impact transient magnitude and foot contact mechanics.
