Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Tubular PFR Kinetics.

Plug-flow reactors (PFRs or tubular reactors) represent the idealized opposite mixing extreme to the continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR). In an ideal PFR, fluid elements travel through an elongated conduit in orderly plug flow with zero axial mixing or back-dispersion, while exhibiting perfect radial mixing across any cross-section. Consequently, every fluid element resides in the reactor for exactly the same duration, equal to the space time τ = V / v_0. Reactants enter at maximum concentration, and reaction rate is highest at the entrance, declining progressively along the tube axis as reactants are consumed. This makes tubular PFRs substantially more efficient than CSTRs for positive-order reactions, requiring less reactor volume to achieve high fractional conversion. For a first-order or pseudo-first-order liquid reaction: -r_A = k · C_A = k · C_A0 · (1 - X_A), integrating the differential PFR design equation dX_A / dτ = -r_A / C_A0 yields the celebrated exponential relation: 1 - X_A = exp(-k · τ) -> ln[1 / (1 - X_A)] = k · τ. In this laboratory investigation, a coiled stainless steel tubular PFR submerged in a constant-temperature water bath is operated across space times from 1.5 to 9.0 min to measure conversion X_A, verify plug-flow kinetics, and evaluate rate constant k = 0.1850 min^-1.

ln(1 / (1 - X_A)) = k · τ = 0.1850 · τ

1. The Ideal Plug-Flow Reactor (PFR) Formulation

Tubular plug-flow reactors operate with continuous fluid motion along an elongated conduit. In the ideal plug-flow model, fluid velocity is radially uniform, and there is zero axial mixing between adjacent fluid slices. Each fluid plug acts as a miniature batch reactor moving through the tube at velocity $u = v_0 / A_c$.

2. Derivation of the PFR Performance Equation

Consider a differential reactor slice of volume $dV$ and length $dz$. A steady-state species conservation balance on reactant $A$ yields:

\[ F_A|_z - F_A|_{z+dz} + r_A dV = 0 \implies -dF_A = (-r_A) dV \]

Since $F_A = F_{A0} (1 - X_A)$, differentiating yields $dF_A = -F_{A0} dX_A$. Substituting into the balance:

\[ F_{A0} dX_A = (-r_A) dV \implies d\tau = \frac{dV}{v_0} = \frac{C_{A0} dX_A}{-r_A} \]

Integrating from the reactor entrance ($\tau = 0, X_A = 0$) to the exit ($\tau, X_A$):

\[ \tau = C_{A0} \int_0^{X_A} \frac{dX_A}{-r_A} \]

3. First-Order Reaction Integration

For a first-order or pseudo-first-order reaction with rate law $-r_A = k C_A = k C_{A0} (1 - X_A)$:

\[ \tau = C_{A0} \int_0^{X_A} \frac{dX_A}{k C_{A0} (1 - X_A)} = \frac{1}{k} \int_0^{X_A} \frac{dX_A}{1 - X_A} = \frac{1}{k} \ln\left( \frac{1}{1 - X_A} \right) \]

Rearranging into linear calibration form:

\[ \ln\left( \frac{1}{1 - X_A} \right) = k \cdot \tau = S \cdot \tau \]

where $S = k$ is the first-order reaction rate constant in $\text{min}^{-1}$. Plotting $\ln[1 / (1 - X_A)]$ versus space time $\tau$ yields a straight line through the origin whose slope directly provides $k$.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

Why does a PFR achieve higher conversion than an identical-volume CSTR for positive-order reactions?

In a PFR, reactants spend their initial residence time at high concentrations where reaction rates are maximized; in a CSTR, feed is instantly diluted to the lowest exit concentration, causing the entire reactor to operate at the slowest reaction rate.

What is the physical meaning of Taylor dispersion in real tubular reactors?

In laminar flow, parabolic velocity profiles cause fluid at the centerline to move twice as fast as the average velocity, while wall shear holds back fluid, creating axial spreading (Taylor dispersion) that causes real reactors to deviate from ideal plug flow toward mixed behavior.

How does coiling a tubular reactor into a helix suppress axial dispersion?

Centrifugal forces in a coiled tube induce secondary Dean vortices (helical circulation cells) that pump fluid radially across streamlines, flattening the axial velocity profile and restoring near-ideal plug-flow performance.

What is the residence time distribution (RTD) of an ideal PFR?

An ideal PFR has a Dirac delta exit age distribution E(t) = δ(t - τ); all fluid elements spend exactly time τ inside the reactor with zero variance (σ^2 = 0).

How do radial temperature gradients develop in non-isothermal tubular reactors?

For highly exothermic reactions, heat generated along the core must conduct radially outward across the tube wall; if tube diameter is too large, thermal runaway hot spots form along the centerline.

What criteria are used to choose between a PFR and a CSTR for a chemical process?

PFRs are chosen for high throughput, high conversion, gas-phase reactions, and when maximizing selectivity of intermediate products in series reactions (A -> R -> S); CSTRs are chosen for complex slurry reactions, long reaction times, and when tight temperature control is mandatory.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Ensure the inlet T-junction provides rapid turbulent micromixing to prevent segregation before entering the tube.
  • Maintain water bath temperature strictly at 25.00 ± 0.05 °C, as reaction rates are exponentially sensitive to temperature.
  • Allow at least 3 residence times at each flow rate before logging steady-state conversion.
  • Flush the coiled tube with deionized water after testing to prevent particulate buildup or scaling.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (1.5 to 9.0 min).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
