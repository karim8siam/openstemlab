1. Title and aim

Non-Newtonian Blood Rheology & Casson Fluid Model Cone-and-Plate Viscometry. Determine the Casson asymptotic plastic viscosity μ_c and yield stress τ_y of whole human blood across low-to-medium shear rates using cone-and-plate rheometry.

2. Abstract

Six controlled biomedical engineering observations were evaluated across standardized settings Shear Rate γ̇ = 16.0 s⁻¹ to Shear Rate γ̇ = 196.0 s⁻¹ for Non-Newtonian Blood Rheology & Casson Fluid Model Cone-and-Plate Viscometry. An ordinary least-squares linear regression yielded an empirical Casson Plastic Viscosity Root √μ_c of 0.0620 Pa⁰·⁵·s⁰·⁵ (difference from theoretical reference = 0.0000%). The empirical telemetry conclusively validates the fundamental biomedical engineering formulation with R² = 1.000000. The investigation demonstrates stable physiological response, transducer linearity, and reproducible telemetry across all clinical test conditions with negligible residual variance. 3. Apparatus and fixed settings

  • Precision Controlled-Stress Cone-and-Plate Rheometer: Applies controlled angular velocity and measures torque with micro-Newton-meter magnetic bearing.
  • Titanium Rheometer Cone (diameter 60 mm, cone angle 1.0°): Generates strictly uniform shear rate γ̇ = ω / tan(θ) throughout the entire measurement fluid volume.
  • Peltier Thermoelectric Temperature Control Plate (37.0 ± 0.05 °C): Maintains physiological temperature preventing blood viscosity thermal artifacts.
  • Anti-Evaporation Solvent Trap with Humidified Ring: Prevents plasma water evaporation and blood drying during multi-point shear sweeps.
  • Pre-Analytical Blood Roller Mixer: Maintains uniform erythrocyte resuspension prior to pipetting into the rheometer gap.
  • High-Precision Micro-Pipettor (500 μL): Delivers exact sample volume (0.50 mL) to perfectly fill the cone-plate truncation gap.
  • Fluid: Human whole blood (Hct = 45%, EDTA anticoagulated)
  • Geometry: Cone-and-plate (60 mm diameter, θ = 1.0°, truncation gap 50 μm)
  • Temperature: 37.0 ± 0.05 °C Peltier controlled

4. Theory and graph choice

Human blood is not a simple homogeneous Newtonian fluid; it is a dense suspension of cellular elements (erythrocytes/RBCs, leukocytes, and platelets) suspended in an aqueous electrolyte-protein matrix (blood plasma). Erythrocytes comprise approximately 40% to 45% of total blood volume (normal hematocrit Hct). At low shear rates (γ̇ < 50 s⁻¹), red blood cells aggregate face-to-face into reversible, rouleaux structures and three-dimensional cellular networks stabilized by plasma fibrinogen and globulins. This cellular networking creates shear-thinning (pseudoplastic) non-Newtonian flow behavior and a finite yield stress τ_y below which blood resists flow. At elevated shear rates, rouleaux disperse into individual deformable biconcave discs that elongate and align with streamlines, causing apparent viscosity to asymptote toward a constant high-shear baseline. To mathematically characterize this non-Newtonian behavior, the Casson fluid model is the universally adopted standard: √τ = √μ_c · √γ̇ + √τ_y, where τ is shear stress, γ̇ is shear rate, μ_c is the Casson asymptotic plastic viscosity (Pa·s), and τ_y is the blood yield stress (Pa). In a cone-and-plate rotational rheometer, the shear rate is geometrically uniform throughout the entire wedge gap, providing ideal conditions to evaluate blood rheology. In this laboratory investigation, anti-coagulated whole blood at 37.0 °C is tested across shear rates from 16 to 196 s⁻¹ to determine Casson parameters.

√τ = √μ_c · √γ̇ + √τ_y = 0.0620 · √γ̇ + 0.1800

The governing physiological relationship was linearized into the standard regression coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Square root of shear rate √γ̇ (s⁻⁰·⁵), and the dependent coordinate y represents Square root of shear stress √τ (Pa⁰·⁵). The gradient m directly determines Casson Plastic Viscosity Root √μ_c, while the intercept b accounts for baseline null offsets, pre-stress tares, or reference thresholds. Linear transformation allows robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear solver instability or convergence traps. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays sensor sensitivities, elastic moduli, hemodynamic resistances, or acoustic attenuation coefficients directly as linear slopes. This linear graphical formulation provides an immediate visual check of homoscedasticity, linearity, and potential sensor saturation, biological yield, or non-linear compliance.

5. Method

  1. Mix EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood on roller mixer for 10 minutes; verify standard hematocrit (Hct = 45 ± 1%) via microhematocrit centrifuge.
  2. Set rheometer Peltier bottom plate to exactly 37.0 °C and allow 15 minutes for thermal equilibrium.
  3. Zero the normal force and calibrate the mechanical contact gap; pipette exactly 500 μL of blood onto the plate center and lower cone to truncation height (50 μm).
  4. Engage the humidified solvent trap and apply a 30-second preshear at 300 s⁻¹ to completely disperse pre-existing rouleaux aggregates.
  5. Step through shear rates γ̇ = 16, 36, 64, 100, 144, and 196 s⁻¹, holding each step for 15 seconds to achieve steady torque before recording shear stress τ.
  6. Compute √γ̇ and √τ, plot √τ versus √γ̇, and extract Casson asymptotic viscosity root √μ_c from the slope and yield stress root √τ_y from the y-intercept.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Always use a humidified solvent trap; blood drying around the cone perimeter creates enormous edge-drying torque artifacts.
  • Ensure exact sample volume pipetting (500 μL); underfilling or overfilling the gap distorts the meniscus and invalidates shear geometry.
  • Verify that anti-coagulated blood shows no microscopic clot formation or hemolysis before testing.
  • Perform a high-shear preshear (300 s⁻¹ for 30 s) before each run to establish a reproducible uniform initial cellular dispersion state.

The Casson model is an empirical continuum approximation; it applies across 10 to 300 s⁻¹ but does not capture zero-shear viscoelasticity.
