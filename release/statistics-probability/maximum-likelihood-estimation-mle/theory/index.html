Probabilistic foundations of Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).

Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) is the preeminent parametric estimation framework in mathematical statistics, econometrics, and machine learning. Pioneered by Ronald A. Fisher, the method identifies parameter values that maximize the probability of having generated the observed empirical data. Through the score function, Hessian curvature, and Fisher information, MLE provides a unified apparatus for point estimation, asymptotic confidence intervals, and hypothesis testing. In this laboratory, we construct log-likelihood curves, solve score equations, evaluate Fisher information additivity, and confirm the linear information scaling gradient m = 2.000. In mathematical statistics, empirical data science, and modern computational engineering, understanding the asymptotic sampling properties and theoretical behavior of Maximum Likelihood Estimation, Score Function, and Fisher Information provides crucial guarantees. Rigorous experimental validation connects foundational probability laws with observable empirical phenomena, ensuring that statistical inference remains valid under finite sample sizes and real-world data collection constraints.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How is the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) formulated mathematically?

Let X_1, ..., X_n be independent, identically distributed random variables with probability density or mass function f(x; θ), where θ ∈ Θ is the unknown parameter vector. The likelihood function is the joint density viewed as a function of the parameter: L(θ; x) = ∏_{i=1}^n f(x_i; θ). The log-likelihood function is ℓ(θ; x) = ln L(θ; x) = ∑_{i=1}^n ln f(x_i; θ). The Maximum Likelihood Estimator θ̂_MLE is the parameter value that maximizes the likelihood over the parameter space: θ̂_MLE = argmax_{θ ∈ Θ} ℓ(θ; x).

What is the Score function, and why does its mathematical expectation equal zero?

The Score function S(θ) is the gradient (vector of first partial derivatives) of the log-likelihood function with respect to the parameter: S(θ) = ∇_θ ℓ(θ; x) = ∑_{i=1}^n ∇_θ ln f(x_i; θ). Under standard Cramér regularity conditions allowing differentiation under the integral sign: E[S(θ)] = ∫ [∇_θ f(x; θ) / f(x; θ)] f(x; θ) dx = ∫ ∇_θ f(x; θ) dx = ∇_θ ∫ f(x; θ) dx = ∇_θ (1) = 0. Thus, the expected value of the score function is identically zero at the true parameter value.

How is Fisher Information defined, and what is the Information Identity?

Fisher Information I_n(θ) quantifies the amount of information that an observable random sample carries about an unknown parameter θ. It is defined as the variance of the score function: I_n(θ) = Var(S(θ)) = E[S(θ) S(θ)^T]. Under regularity conditions where f(x; θ) is twice continuously differentiable, the Information Identity states that Fisher information equals the expected negative Hessian of the log-likelihood: I_n(θ) = - E[∇_θ^2 ℓ(θ; x)]. For i.i.d. observations, Fisher information is additive: I_n(θ) = n I_1(θ).

What are the asymptotic properties of the Maximum Likelihood Estimator?

Under standard regularity conditions, as sample size n → ∞: (1) Consistency: θ̂_n converges in probability to the true parameter θ_0 (θ̂_n →^p θ_0); (2) Asymptotic Normality: √n (θ̂_n - θ_0) converges in distribution to N(0, I_1(θ_0)^(-1)); (3) Asymptotic Efficiency: θ̂ achieves the Cramér-Rao Lower Bound asymptotically, meaning no consistent estimator has smaller asymptotic variance; (4) Invariance: if g(θ) is a one-to-one function, the MLE of g(θ) is g(θ̂_MLE).

What are the three classical asymptotic hypothesis tests based on likelihood?

The 'Holy Trinity' of likelihood-based hypothesis testing comprises: (1) Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT): compares maximum likelihoods under restricted and unrestricted models via λ = 2 [ℓ(θ̂) - ℓ(θ_0)] ~ χ²(r); (2) Wald Test: evaluates the distance of the unrestricted MLE from the null hypothesis normalized by curvature: W = (θ̂ - θ_0)^T I_n(θ̂) (θ̂ - θ_0) ~ χ²(r); (3) Score (Lagrange Multiplier) Test: evaluates how close the score gradient is to zero at the null: LM = S(θ_0)^T I_n(θ_0)^(-1) S(θ_0) ~ χ²(r). All three are asymptotically equivalent under H_0.

How does the Newton-Raphson optimization algorithm locate the MLE numerically?

When the likelihood equations S(θ) = 0 have no closed-form analytical solution (e.g., in logistic regression or Weibull models), iterative numerical optimization is used. Expanding S(θ) in a first-order Taylor series around current iterate θ^(t): 0 ≈ S(θ^(t)) + S'(θ^(t)) (θ^(t+1) - θ^(t)). Solving for θ^(t+1) yields the Newton-Raphson update: θ^(t+1) = θ^(t) - [∇^2 ℓ(θ^(t))]^(-1) ∇ ℓ(θ^(t)). In Fisher scoring, the observed Hessian is replaced by its negative expected value -I_n(θ^(t)), enhancing stability.

What are the limitations of MLE in non-regular or boundary parameter regimes?

Standard asymptotic optimality of MLE breaks down when regularity conditions are violated: (1) Parameter-dependent support: For U[0, θ], the score equation is non-differentiable; the MLE is the sample maximum X_{(n)}, which converges at rate n rather than √n and has a non-Gaussian Weibull limit; (2) Boundary parameters: If true θ lies on the boundary of parameter space Θ, the score does not vanish and the asymptotic distribution is a mixture; (3) Unbounded likelihood: In Gaussian mixture models with unequal variances, the likelihood can diverge to infinity around single data points.

Experimental precautions and statistical checks

  • Verify that the log-likelihood function is strictly concave (negative second derivative) to ensure a unique global maximum.
  • Account for boundary conditions when maximizing likelihood functions whose parameter space is bounded.
  • Use logarithmic transformations (ln L) to prevent numerical underflow when computing products of small probabilities.
  • Check that sample size n is sufficiently large (n ≥ 30) when utilizing asymptotic normal standard errors.

Theoretical assumptions & limitations

  • In small samples, maximum likelihood estimators can exhibit substantial finite-sample bias (e.g., s_MLE^2 = (n-1)/n s^2).
  • When the support of the distribution depends on the unknown parameter (e.g., Uniform U[0, θ]), standard asymptotic normality fails.
