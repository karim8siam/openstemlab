01Electromotive Force vs. Terminal Potential Difference

A fundamental distinction in electrical physics exists between the electromotive force (EMF, denoted ℰ) of a source and its terminal potential difference (V). Electromotive force represents the total non-electrostatic energy converted into electrical potential energy per unit charge that passes through the source. It is generated by chemical, mechanical, thermal, or photoelectric processes inside the device. In an electrochemical cell, spontaneous oxidation-reduction reactions at the electrode-electrolyte interfaces maintain a constant chemical potential difference between the cathode and anode. In contrast, the terminal potential difference V is the electrostatic potential drop across the external terminals of the cell. When the external circuit is open (zero current, I = 0), no potential is dropped across the internal materials of the source, and the terminal voltage equals the full open-circuit EMF: V = ℰ. However, as soon as a closed external path allows current I to flow, charge carriers must traverse the internal electrolyte and active mass of the battery. Because the internal medium possesses a finite electrical resistance r, an internal ohmic potential drop equal to I · r is consumed within the cell itself. Consequently, the terminal potential difference available to drive current through the external load drops linearly according to Ohm's generalized circuit law: V = ℰ − I · r. Thus, terminal voltage is always strictly less than EMF whenever current is being delivered to a load.

V = \mathcal{E} - I \cdot r \quad \iff \quad \mathcal{E} = V + I \cdot r = I(R_{ ext{load}} + r)
Under what circumstances can the terminal potential difference across a cell exceed its electromotive force?

The terminal voltage V exceeds the cell EMF ℰ when the cell is being recharged by an external power source that drives current into the cell in the reverse direction (from positive terminal to negative terminal internally). In this reverse-charging state, the current is negative relative to normal discharge, yielding V = ℰ − (−I)r = ℰ + I · r. The external charger must supply sufficient voltage to overcome both the chemical EMF and the internal ohmic drop.

02Electrochemical Origin of Internal Resistance

The internal resistance r of an electrochemical cell is not a lumped metallic resistor, but a distributed complex impedance arising from three distinct physical and chemical mechanisms within the cell: (1) Ohmic resistance of the electrolyte and separators: Ions (such as NH₄⁺, Zn²⁺, H⁺, or OH⁻) drifting through the aqueous solution or porous separator matrix experience viscous hydrodynamic drag and electrostatic collisions with solvent molecules and dissolved salts, establishing a finite ionic conductivity σ_ion. (2) Charge-transfer kinetic resistance: The electrochemical oxidation of zinc at the anode (Zn ⟶ Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻) and the reduction of manganese dioxide at the cathode require charge carriers to surmount activation energy barriers at the electrical double layer, governed by the Butler-Volmer kinetic relationship. (3) Mass-transport diffusion polarization: As discharge progresses, active reactant ions are depleted in the immediate vicinity of the electrode surface while reaction products accumulate, creating concentration gradients that produce a counter-EMF known as concentration polarization. Together, these effects constitute the total apparent internal resistance r. In fresh primary cells, r is typically between 0.1 Ω and 3 Ω, whereas in automotive lead-acid batteries designed for high cranking currents, r is engineered to be as low as 0.002 Ω to 0.005 Ω.

r_{ ext{int}} = r_{ ext{ohmic}} + r_{ ext{transfer}} + r_{ ext{diffusion}} = rac{L}{\sigma_{ ext{ion}} A} + rac{\eta_{ ext{act}}}{I} + rac{\eta_{ ext{conc}}}{I}
Why does the apparent internal resistance of a dry cell increase as it nears the end of its operational discharge life?

As a cell discharges, the active metallic anode is consumed, the electrolyte becomes chemically depleted of free reactant ions, insoluble reaction by-products (such as zinc diamminedichloride crystals or hydrogen gas pockets) form on the electrode surfaces reducing effective contact area, and moisture gradually evaporates. These structural changes drastically reduce ionic conductivity and slow reaction kinetics, causing the internal resistance r to escalate by one to two orders of magnitude.

03Linear Regression of the Terminal Characteristic (V vs. I)

The graphical determination of electromotive force and internal resistance relies on plotting the terminal voltage V against the discharge current I across a range of calibrated external load resistances. Rearranging the closed-circuit equation into standard slope-intercept form gives V = (−r) · I + ℰ, which represents a straight line y = m x + b with independent variable x = I (in amperes), dependent variable y = V (in volts), slope m = −r (in volts per ampere, or ohms), and vertical intercept b = ℰ (in volts). By applying ordinary least-squares (OLS) linear regression across six or more distinct load settings, experimental scatter from meter digit resolution and transient contact resistances is averaged out. The negative of the regression slope directly isolates the internal resistance r = −m, while the vertical intercept provides an unbiased estimate of the open-circuit EMF ℰ = b without requiring an infinitely high impedance electrometer. Furthermore, extrapolating the best-fit line to the horizontal axis where V = 0 yields the short-circuit current I_sc = ℰ / r = −b / m, which defines the absolute physical upper bound on current that the cell could theoretically discharge into a zero-resistance dead short.

Why is it dangerous and experimentally incorrect to measure the short-circuit current I_sc directly with an ammeter connected across the cell terminals?

Connecting an ammeter directly across cell terminals places a near-zero resistance (R_A ≈ 0.02 Ω) across the source, causing an enormous surge of current limited only by r. This violent discharge produces intense Joule heating (I²r), boiling the electrolyte, generating dangerous internal gas pressure that can rupture the cell casing, causing rapid electrode polarization that invalidates the measurement, and blowing the ammeter protection fuse.

04Maximum Power Transfer Theorem and Efficiency

An essential engineering consequence of internal resistance is the Maximum Power Transfer Theorem, which governs the delivery of energy from an electrical source to an external load resistor R_load. The electrical power delivered to the load is given by P_load = I² · R_load = [ℰ / (R_load + r)]² · R_load. To find the load resistance that maximizes power dissipation, we differentiate P_load with respect to R_load and set the derivative to zero: dP_load / dR_load = ℰ² · (r − R_load) / (R_load + r)³ = 0. This condition is satisfied uniquely when R_load = r. When the external load resistance matches the source internal resistance (impedance matching), the power delivered reaches its theoretical peak: P_max = ℰ² / (4r). However, at maximum power transfer, the terminal voltage is exactly half the open-circuit EMF (V = ℰ / 2), and exactly half of the total chemical energy released is dissipated as waste heat inside the cell itself (I²r = I²R_load), yielding an electrical transmission efficiency of exactly η = 50%. In power distribution networks, high efficiency (η > 95%) is prioritized over maximum power extraction by keeping source internal resistance vastly lower than load resistance (r ≪ R_load).

P_{ ext{load}}(R) = rac{\mathcal{E}^2 R}{(R + r)^2}, \quad P_{\max} = rac{\mathcal{E}^2}{4r} \quad ext{at} \quad R_{ ext{load}} = r, \quad \eta = rac{R_{ ext{load}}}{R_{ ext{load}} + r}
If a cell has ℰ = 1.50 V and r = 2.50 Ω, what is the maximum power it can transfer to an external load, and what is the load power when R_load = 10.0 Ω?

At matching load R_load = r = 2.50 Ω, P_max = (1.50)² / (4 × 2.50) = 2.25 / 10.0 = 0.225 W = 225.0 mW. When R_load = 10.0 Ω, current is I = 1.50 / (10.0 + 2.50) = 0.120 A, and load power is P = (0.120)² × 10.0 = 0.144 W = 144.0 mW (a 36% decrease in power, but with higher efficiency of η = 10.0 / 12.5 = 80%).

05Instrument Loading and Kelvin 4-Wire Sensing

Accurate experimental determination of cell parameters requires rigorous accounting for measurement circuit loading errors. A real voltmeter possesses a finite internal input impedance R_V (typically 10 MΩ for modern digital multimeters, but only 10 kΩ to 20 kΩ for vintage analog moving-coil meters). When connected across the cell, the voltmeter draws a small shunting current I_V = V / R_V. If R_V is not vastly larger than r, this current causes a parasitic internal potential drop, making the measured open-circuit voltage lower than the true EMF by factor R_V / (R_V + r). Similarly, an ammeter placed in series possesses a finite internal shunt resistance R_A (burden resistance), which adds directly to the total loop resistance: I_meas = ℰ / (R_load + r + R_A). Furthermore, lead wire resistance and contact resistance at terminal posts can introduce spurious offsets. To eliminate lead resistance errors, the voltmeter leads must be attached directly to the battery output lugs independently of the high-current discharge leads—a laboratory implementation of the Kelvin 4-wire potential sensing principle.

How does using a null-balance slide-wire potentiometer overcome the finite resistance limitations of standard voltmeters when measuring cell EMF?

A slide-wire potentiometer measures EMF by opposing the cell potential against an adjustable known potential drop along a calibrated slide-wire until a sensitive galvanometer detects zero current (null condition). Because exactly zero current is drawn from the test cell at the balance point, the internal drop I · r is strictly zero, allowing direct measurement of the pure open-circuit electromotive force ℰ with zero instrument loading.

06Series and Parallel Combinations of Primary Cells

When multiple electrical cells are combined into battery banks, their equivalent electromotive force and internal resistance combine according to basic circuit laws. For n identical cells connected in series aiding (positive terminal of one connected to negative terminal of the next), the individual chemical potential steps add algebraically, while the internal resistances add in series: ℰ_series = n · ℰ, and r_series = n · r. Series grouping multiplies available voltage and short-circuit power, but also multiplies internal resistance, making it optimal when driving high-resistance loads (R_load ≫ r). Conversely, for m identical cells connected in parallel (all positive terminals tied together and all negative terminals tied together), the terminal EMF remains unchanged, while the internal paths form parallel conductances: ℰ_parallel = ℰ, and r_parallel = r / m. Parallel grouping reduces internal resistance by factor m and divides the discharge current equally among the cells (I_cell = I / m), drastically mitigating polarization and extending operating lifetime when driving low-resistance loads (R_load ≪ r).

\mathcal{E}_{ ext{series}} = n \mathcal{E}, \quad r_{ ext{series}} = n r; \qquad \mathcal{E}_{ ext{parallel}} = \mathcal{E}, \quad r_{ ext{parallel}} = rac{r}{m}
For a battery of N = n × m identical cells arranged in m parallel branches of n series cells each, what is the optimal arrangement (values of n and m) to deliver maximum current to an external load R?

The total current is I = nℰ / [R + (n r / m)]. By setting dI/dn = 0 subject to the constraint n · m = N, the current is maximized when the battery equivalent internal resistance equals the external load resistance: n r / m = R. This demonstrates that internal resistance matching dictates optimal battery pack topology.

07Temperature Coefficients and Polarization Recovery Dynamics

Internal resistance is strongly temperature-dependent, exhibiting a negative temperature coefficient (dr / dT < 0) characteristic of liquid and gel electrolytes. As temperature rises, the thermal kinetic energy of the solvent reduces its dynamic viscosity η_solv according to the Arrhenius relation, increasing ionic mobility μ_ion = q / (6πη_solv r_ion) and electrical conductivity σ_ion. Consequently, warming a cold battery from 0 °C to 25 °C can cut its internal resistance in half, explaining why automotive lead-acid batteries and camera batteries exhibit sluggish performance in sub-zero winter environments. Furthermore, primary cells exhibit dynamic polarization recovery: drawing current produces temporary localized reactant depletion and hydrogen micro-bubble accumulation at the cathode (polarization), causing apparent r to rise during the pulse. When the discharge switch is opened, ionic diffusion from the bulk electrolyte paste replenishes the electrode interface and chemical depolarizers (such as manganese dioxide MnO₂) oxidize the hydrogen film, restoring the cell to its quiescent baseline EMF over a 30 to 60 second relaxation window.

Why is an intermittent pulse discharge protocol specified in this experiment rather than leaving the switch closed continuously?

Closing the circuit continuously produces cumulative concentration polarization and internal Joule heating, causing the apparent internal resistance to drift upwards and EMF to sag over time. Pulsing the switch for only 3 to 5 seconds provides stable readings while preserving chemical equilibrium and minimizing electrolyte exhaustion.
