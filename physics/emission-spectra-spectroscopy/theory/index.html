01The Balmer Formula and the Empirical Discovery of Spectral Regularities

In 1885, Swiss schoolteacher and mathematician Johann Jakob Balmer discovered a remarkable mathematical pattern hidden within the wavelengths of the four visible spectral lines of atomic hydrogen measured by Anders Jonas Ångström. Prior to Balmer’s insight, line spectra were viewed as arbitrary acoustic-like harmonics of complex mechanical atoms. Balmer showed that the wavelengths of the red (H_α, 656.3 nm), cyan (H_β, 486.1 nm), blue-violet (H_γ, 434.0 nm), and violet (H_δ, 410.2 nm) lines could be expressed by a single simple algebraic equation: λ = h · [m² / (m² − 4)], where m = 3, 4, 5, 6 and h = 3645.6 × 10⁻⁸ cm (the Balmer constant). In 1888, Swedish physicist Johannes Rydberg generalized Balmer's formula to encompass alkali metal series and all hydrogen transitions by expressing the relationship in terms of wavenumber (the reciprocal of wavelength, ν̃ = 1/λ): ν̃ = 1 / λ = R_H (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), where n₁ = 2 defines the Balmer series in the visible spectrum, and n₂ = 3, 4, 5, ... represents the higher energy states. Rydberg's fundamental constant of spectroscopy, R_H ≈ 1.09678 × 10⁷ m⁻¹, became one of the most precisely measured physical constants in history.

\tilde{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left( \frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{n^2} \right) = R_H \left( \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{n^2} \right), \quad n = 3, 4, 5, \dots, \quad R_H \approx 1.096776 \times 10^7 \; \text{m}^{-1}
How did Balmer’s empirical formula revolutionize early atomic physics before the development of quantum mechanics?

Balmer’s formula proved that atomic emission spectra are governed by simple, quantized integer mathematical laws rather than chaotic continuous vibration. This provided the crucial empirical clue that led Niels Bohr to postulate quantized electron angular momentum and discrete energy levels in 1913.

02The Bohr Model of the Hydrogen Atom and Theoretical Derivation of Rydberg Constant

In 1913, Danish physicist Niels Bohr synthesized Ernest Rutherford’s nuclear planetary atom with Max Planck’s quantum hypothesis and Albert Einstein’s photon concept. Bohr postulated that: (1) Conduction electrons orbit the massive proton in stationary circular orbits without radiating electromagnetic energy. (2) The orbital angular momentum of the electron is quantized in integer multiples of Dirac's reduced Planck constant: L = m_e v r = n ℏ = n h / (2π), where n = 1, 2, 3, ... is the principal quantum number. Equating the electrostatic Coulomb attraction to the required centripetal force: (1 / 4πε₀)(e² / r_n²) = m_e v_n² / r_n, yields the quantized orbital radius: r_n = n² [4πε₀ ℏ² / (m_e e²)] = n² a₀, where a₀ = 0.529177 Å is the Bohr radius. The total mechanical energy (kinetic plus potential) is quantized as: E_n = K_n + U_n = (1/2) m_e v_n² − e² / (4πε₀ r_n) = −m_e e⁴ / [32 π² ε₀² ℏ² n²] = −13.606 eV / n². (3) When an electron undergoes a quantum jump from initial orbit n₂ to lower orbit n₁, a single photon of frequency ν is emitted, conserving energy: h ν = E_{n₂} − E_{n₁}. Converting frequency to wavenumber ν̃ = ν/c directly derives the Rydberg constant from pure fundamental constants: R_∞ = m_e e⁴ / (8 ε₀² h³ c).

E_n = -\frac{13.606 \; \text{eV}}{n^2}, \quad h \nu = \Delta E = E_{n_2} - E_{n_1} \implies \frac{1}{\lambda} = \frac{m_e e^4}{8 \varepsilon_0^2 h^3 c} \left( \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right)
How does the Bohr model explain why hydrogen gas only absorbs or emits discrete wavelengths rather than a continuous rainbow spectrum?

Electrons can only occupy discrete, quantized energy levels (E_n). Because energy must be strictly conserved during radiative transitions, photons can only be absorbed or emitted with energy hν exactly matching the energy difference between two allowed orbits (ΔE = E₂ − E₁).

03Nuclear Finite Mass Correction and the Reduced Electron Mass

In Bohr's initial derivation, the atomic nucleus (proton) was assumed to be infinitely massive and stationary. However, in reality, both the electron (mass m_e) and the proton (mass M_p ≈ 1836.15 m_e) orbit their common center of mass. In classical two-body central-force mechanics, this motion is rigorously accounted for by replacing the bare electron rest mass m_e with the 'reduced mass' μ of the system: μ = (m_e M_p) / (m_e + M_p) = m_e / (1 + m_e / M_p) = 0.999455 m_e. Consequently, the actual Rydberg constant for atomic hydrogen (R_H) is slightly smaller than the theoretical infinite-nuclear-mass Rydberg constant R_∞: R_H = R_∞ / (1 + m_e / M_p) = (1.097373 × 10⁷ m⁻¹) / (1 + 1/1836.15) = 1.096776 × 10⁷ m⁻¹. In 1932, Harold Urey utilized this precise isotopic mass shift (Δλ ≈ 0.18 nm between hydrogen H¹ and deuterium H² at the H_α line) to discover heavy hydrogen (deuterium), a triumph of precision spectroscopy that earned the 1934 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

\mu = \frac{m_e M_p}{m_e + M_p} = \frac{m_e}{1 + m_e / M_p}, \quad R_H = \frac{R_\infty}{1 + m_e / M_p} = 1.0967758 \times 10^7 \; \text{m}^{-1}
Why is the experimentally measured Rydberg constant for hydrogen R_H slightly smaller than the theoretical Rydberg constant for an infinite-mass nucleus R_∞?

Because the proton is not infinitely massive, both the electron and proton orbit their mutual center of mass. Replacing the electron mass with the reduced mass μ = m_e / (1 + m_e/M_p) reduces the effective mass by 0.0545%, lowering R_H proportionally.

04The Electromagnetic Hydrogen Series: Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, Brackett, Pfund

The complete emission spectrum of atomic hydrogen is structured into distinct spectral series, each corresponding to quantum electronic transitions terminating on a specific lower principal quantum number n₁: (1) Lyman Series (n₁ = 1, n₂ ≥ 2): Transitions terminating in the ground state (E₁ = −13.6 eV), producing high-energy photons in the vacuum ultraviolet region (λ = 91.2 nm to 121.6 nm), discovered by Theodore Lyman in 1906. (2) Balmer Series (n₁ = 2, n₂ ≥ 3): Transitions terminating in the first excited state (E₂ = −3.40 eV), falling within the visible spectrum (λ = 364.6 nm to 656.3 nm), the primary focus of this experiment. (3) Paschen Series (n₁ = 3, n₂ ≥ 4): Transitions terminating on n₁ = 3 (E₃ = −1.51 eV), producing near-infrared radiation (λ = 820.4 nm to 1875 nm), discovered by Friedrich Paschen in 1908. (4) Brackett Series (n₁ = 4, n₂ ≥ 5, infrared, λ = 1.46 μm to 4.05 μm). (5) Pfund Series (n₁ = 5, n₂ ≥ 6, far infrared, λ = 2.28 μm to 7.46 μm). In all series, as n₂ → ∞, the spectral lines crowd closely together and converge toward a sharp 'series limit' (λ_limit = n₁² / R_H), where the photon energy corresponds to the complete ionization of the hydrogen atom from state n₁.

\lambda_{\text{limit}} = \frac{n_1^2}{R_H}, \quad \text{Balmer Limit } (n_1 = 2): \lambda_{\text{limit}} = \frac{4}{R_H} = 364.6 \; \text{nm} \quad (E_{\text{ion}} = 3.401 \; \text{eV})
What physical phenomenon occurs at the Balmer series convergence limit at λ = 364.6 nm?

The series limit represents a transition from an initial state where n₂ → ∞ (the electron is completely detached from the atom with zero kinetic energy) to n₁ = 2. Wavelengths shorter than 364.6 nm produce a continuous absorption or emission spectrum corresponding to ionization with excess kinetic energy.

05Kirchhoff’s Laws of Spectroscopy and Fraunhofer Absorption Lines

In 1859, Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen established the three foundational laws of spectroscopy: (1) A hot, dense solid, liquid, or highly compressed gas emits a continuous blackbody spectrum spanning all wavelengths without gaps. (2) A hot, low-density gas emits a discrete bright-line emission spectrum consisting of narrow, characteristic wavelengths unique to the atomic species. (3) When continuous white light passes through a cooler, low-density gas, the gas selectively absorbs the exact wavelengths that it would emit if heated, producing dark absorption lines superimposed on the continuous background. In 1814, Joseph von Fraunhofer cataloged 574 dark absorption lines in the solar spectrum, labeling the prominent features A through K. Kirchhoff realized that these dark lines are produced by cooler gaseous elements in the solar photosphere absorbing radiation emitted by the intensely hot solar interior. For example, the dark Fraunhofer C-line (656.3 nm) and F-line (486.1 nm) match the hydrogen H_α and H_β lines, while the dark D-line (589.0/589.6 nm) matches sodium, providing the first definitive proof of the elemental composition of the Sun.

\text{Fraunhofer C-line} \equiv H_\alpha (656.3 \; \text{nm}), \quad \text{F-line} \equiv H_\beta (486.1 \; \text{nm}), \quad \text{D-line} \equiv \text{Na doublet } (589.3 \; \text{nm})
Why do the dark Fraunhofer lines in the solar spectrum coincide exactly with the bright emission lines observed from laboratory gas discharge tubes?

Because atoms absorb and emit light through the identical quantum transitions between energy levels (ΔE = hν). Cooler hydrogen and sodium atoms in the Sun’s outer atmosphere absorb those exact photon wavelengths from the hot interior continuous radiation, creating dark absorption lines.

06Grating Dispersion, Resolving Power and Instrumental Broadening

In transmission grating spectrometry, the angular dispersion D_θ defines the angular separation produced per unit wavelength increment: D_θ = dθ / dλ = m / (d cos θ), where m is the spectral order, d is the grating slit spacing, and θ is the diffraction angle. A grating with 600 lines/mm (d = 1.667 μm) provides substantially higher dispersion than a glass prism, while maintaining a nearly linear dispersion relation (d sin θ = m λ ⟹ θ ≈ m λ / d for small angles), which prevents the focal-plane compression characteristic of prism spectroscopes in the red spectrum. The chromatic resolving power R defines the ability to separate two closely adjacent spectral lines of wavelength λ and λ + Δλ according to the Rayleigh criterion: R = λ / Δλ = m · N, where N is the total number of illuminated grating rulings. For a 25 mm grating illuminated across 15,000 rulings in the first order (m = 1), R = 15,000, easily resolving lines separated by less than 0.04 nm (such as the sodium doublet Δλ = 0.6 nm). Real spectral lines exhibit finite physical width governed by Doppler broadening from thermal atomic velocities (Δλ_D / λ ≈ √(8 k_B T ln 2 / M c²)), pressure broadening from interatomic collisions, and instrumental slit diffraction.

D_\theta = \frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \frac{m}{d \cos \theta}, \quad R = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta \lambda} = m \cdot N, \quad \Delta \lambda_{\text{Doppler}} = \lambda_0 \sqrt{\frac{8 k_B T \ln 2}{M c^2}}
Why is a diffraction grating spectroscope preferred over a glass prism spectroscope for measuring the Balmer series wavelengths?

A diffraction grating provides a nearly linear dispersion across the entire visible spectrum (θ ∝ λ), whereas a glass prism compresses the red region (where H_α lies) and excessively spreads the violet region due to non-linear Cauchy dispersion.

07Spectroscopic Diagnostics of Gas Discharge and Capillary Geissler Tubes

A Geissler spectrum tube operates on the principle of electrical glow discharge in low-pressure gases (~0.5 to 5 mmHg). High-voltage alternating or direct current applied across aluminum electrodes accelerates free electrons through the gas. When these electrons acquire kinetic energy exceeding the dissociation threshold of molecular hydrogen (4.52 eV) and the excitation threshold of atomic hydrogen (10.2 eV to 13.06 eV), inelastic electron-atom collisions kick bound electrons into excited states (n = 3, 4, 5, ...). Within nanoseconds (typical radiative lifetime τ ≈ 10⁻⁸ s), these excited atoms de-excite via spontaneous emission, radiating the characteristic Balmer photons. The central capillary bore constricts the cross-sectional area of the plasma, increasing current density J = I / A by two orders of magnitude compared to the electrode bulbs. This intense current density maximizes the electron-molecule collision rate, ensuring near-complete molecular dissociation into atomic H and producing brilliant, narrow Balmer emission lines with high signal-to-noise ratio.

e^- + \text{H}_2 \longrightarrow 2\text{H} + e^-, \quad e^- + \text{H}(n=1) \longrightarrow \text{H}^*(n) + e^-, \quad \text{H}^*(n) \xrightarrow{\tau \approx 10^{-8}\,\text{s}} \text{H}(n=2) + h\nu
Why is the bore of a Geissler spectrum tube constructed as a narrow capillary between two wider electrode bulbs?

The narrow capillary constricts the discharge current into a tiny cross-sectional area, vastly increasing the current density. This maximizes electron impact dissociation of H₂ molecules into atomic hydrogen and intensifies the emitted light along the optical line of sight.
