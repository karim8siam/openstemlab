FOLLOW THE METHOD

A reason behind every step.

Determine the optimum coagulant dosage (alum / polyaluminum chloride) and rapid/slow mixing conditions for maximum turbidity and suspended colloidal particle removal from raw surface water according to ASTM D2035. Standardized parameter settings: 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, 25.0, 30.0, 35.0 mg/L.

01

Raw Water Preparation & Characterization

Prepare 10 L of synthetic turbid water using bentonite clay; measure initial turbidity (48.0 NTU), pH (7.2), and alkalinity (85 mg/L as CaCO3); pour 1000 mL into each of six jar beakers.

02

Jar Tester Setup & Coagulant Dosing

Position beakers under jar test paddles; prepare calibrated alum doses (10, 15, 20, 25, 30, and 35 mg/L) in dosing syringes.

03

Rapid Mixing Phase (Coagulation)

Start paddles at 100 rpm (velocity gradient G ≈ 300 s⁻¹); simultaneously inject alum doses into all six jars; mix for exactly 60 seconds to disperse coagulant.

04

Slow Mixing Phase (Flocculation)

Reduce paddle speed to 30 rpm (velocity gradient G ≈ 30 s⁻¹); mix gently for exactly 20 minutes to promote inter-particle collisions and floc agglomeration.

05

Quiescent Settling Phase

Stop paddles and lift drive assembly cleanly; allow water to settle undisturbed for exactly 30 minutes; observe floc settling velocity and supernatant clarity.

06

Supernatant Sampling & Turbidity Measurement

Extract 25 mL samples from sampling ports located 2.0 cm below liquid surface; measure residual turbidity in calibrated nephelometer; plot turbidity versus dose.

THEORY AND STRUCTURAL CONTEXT

Civil engineering background & physical principles.

In civil environmental water treatment engineering, raw surface waters from rivers and reservoirs contain suspended silt, clay, pathogenic microorganisms, and natural organic matter (NOM) present as negatively charged microscopic colloids (0.001 to 1.0 µm). Due to electrostatic repulsion governed by negative zeta potentials (-15 to -30 mV), colloidal particles remain indefinitely suspended in stable Brownian motion and cannot settle by gravity alone. Coagulation and flocculation destabilize this colloidal suspension. Developed in the 1920s, the standard Jar Test (ASTM D2035) uses a multi-paddle stirrer to optimize chemical dosing in municipal water treatment plants. Adding hydrolyzing metal coagulants—such as aluminum sulfate (alum, Al₂(SO₄)₃·14H₂O) or ferric chloride (FeCl₃)—destabilizes colloids through two primary mechanisms: (1) Charge neutralization: trivalent cations (Al³⁺, Al(OH)²⁺, Al₇(OH)₁₇⁴⁺) compress the electrical diffuse double layer and neutralize negative surface charges; and (2) Sweep flocculation: at higher dosages and neutral pH (6.5 to 7.5), amorphous aluminum hydroxide Al(OH)₃ precipitates en masse, physically entrapping and sweeping suspended particles into settleable flocs. In this laboratory experiment, six 1-liter water samples are dosed with alum from 10 to 40 mg/L, subjected to 1-minute rapid mixing (100 rpm), 20-minute slow flocculation (30 rpm), and 30-minute quiescent settling to calibrate residual turbidity reduction and establish the optimum economic dosage.
