Simulation assumptions and limits

The virtual apparatus simulates an ideal helical spring obeying linear Hookean elasticity with uniform wire properties. In physical laboratory experiments, spring self-mass (which contributes an effective dynamic mass), initial coil winding tension, temperature dependence of the steel’s shear modulus, and minor support flexure contribute to real-world deviations. Instrument reading quantization is modeled at 0.1 mm, matching a millimeter mirror scale. The ideal teaching model obeys Hooke’s law δ = F/k across the tested loading range. Real laboratory springs often possess initial coil tension, requiring a small threshold force before extension commences, which manifests as a non-zero horizontal intercept. Fitting extension against force yields slope m = 1/k in SI units (m N⁻¹); the spring stiffness constant is recovered from the inverse gradient k = 1/m in N m⁻¹.

Know your apparatus.

Helical spring and rigid stand

Provide a linear elastic restoring force with uniform coil pitch and known proportional limit.

Mass hanger and calibrated slotted weights

Apply reproducible downward gravitational tensile forces in calibrated increments.

Vertical millimeter mirror scale

Measure pointer displacement accurately while eliminating optical parallax by line-of-sight reflection alignment.

Lightweight index pointer

Indicate spring elongation directly on the scale without adding significant parasitic tare mass.

FOLLOW THE METHOD

A reason behind every step.

Determine the spring stiffness constant and verify Hooke’s law of linear elasticity by measuring extension as a function of applied tensile load. Suggested independent settings: 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5 N.

1

Level the apparatus and zero the pointer

Ensure the support stand is vertically upright and stable. Suspend the spring and empty mass hanger freely. Note the initial position of the index pointer against the millimeter scale as the zero reference reading.

Why this step matters

A rigid, vertical mounting prevents lateral rubbing of the spring coils against the scale and establishes an accurate baseline for all subsequent elongation measurements.

2

Apply the first calibrated tensile load

Carefully add a 0.50 N load (approx. 51.0 g mass) to the mass hanger. Allow any vertical bouncing or swinging motion to subside completely.

Why this step matters

Static equilibrium must be fully established so that the downward gravitational pull mg is exactly balanced by the upward elastic restoring force kδ.

3

Eliminate parallax and record pointer position

Position your eye so that the tip of the pointer exactly covers its reflected image in the mirror scale. Read the pointer position to the nearest 0.1 mm.

Why this step matters

A mirror scale eliminates line-of-sight perspective distortion, ensuring the recorded scale reading corresponds to the true physical horizontal position of the pointer.

4

Measure extension across the incremental series

Systematically increase the stretching force in steps of 0.50 N up to 2.50 N (or 3.00 N maximum). At each step, record the total applied force and the corresponding pointer position.

Why this step matters

Collecting multiple observations over an evenly spaced force range provides the data needed for a robust least-squares linear regression and residual analysis.

5

Perform a decremental unloading verification

Gradually remove the weights in reverse order (2.50 N down to 0.50 N, and finally zero load). Check that the pointer returns to its initial unloaded position.

Why this step matters

Verifying that the pointer returns to its initial zero reading confirms that all measurements remained safely inside the elastic limit and checks for plastic deformation.

6

Fit extension against force and determine stiffness

Plot extension δ on the vertical axis against force F on the horizontal axis. Compute the best-fit line δ = mF + b using ordinary least squares. The spring constant is k = 1/m.

Why this step matters

Linear regression over all data points provides a more reliable estimate than single-point ratios and allows calculation of the slope standard error.
