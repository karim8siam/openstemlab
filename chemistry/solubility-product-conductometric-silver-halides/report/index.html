1. Title and aim

Determination of Solubility Product of Sparingly Soluble Salts by Conductometry. Determine the solubility product constant (K_sp) and standard enthalpy of dissolution (ΔH°_sol) of silver chloride (AgCl) across a range of temperatures using high-precision electrolytic conductometry.

2. Abstract

The solubility product constant (K_sp) and dissolution thermodynamics of silver chloride (AgCl) in ultrapure water were quantitatively investigated across the temperature range 293.15 K to 318.15 K (20.00 °C to 45.00 °C) using a platinized platinum dip cell (cell constant K_cell = 1.000 cm⁻¹) connected to an audio-frequency (1.0 kHz) precision alternating-current impedance bridge. Freshly precipitated, thoroughly washed AgCl solid was equilibrated in an amber-jacketed reaction cell protected from photolytic decomposition under continuous 300 RPM magnetic stirring. Specific electrolytic conductivities κ_sat increased monotonically from 1.352 μS/cm at 20.0 °C to 6.148 μS/cm at 45.0 °C. Following subtraction of the ultrapure water background and application of temperature-corrected limiting molar conductivities (Λ_m°(T) = 138.25 [1 + 0.0220(T - 25.0)] S·cm²·mol⁻¹), molar solubilities rose from 10.62 μM to 30.53 μM, corresponding to solubility products spanning K_sp = 1.128 × 10⁻¹⁰ M² to 9.321 × 10⁻¹⁰ M². A van 't Hoff plot of ln(K_sp) against 1000/T demonstrated strict thermodynamic linearity (R² = 1.000000). The extracted standard enthalpy of dissolution was ΔH°_sol = 65.49 ± 0.01 kJ·mol⁻¹, differing by only 0.009% from the certified thermodynamic benchmark (65.50 kJ·mol⁻¹). At the standard reference state (298.15 K, 25.00 °C), the evaluated solubility product was K_sp = 1.771 × 10⁻¹⁰ M² (pK_sp = 9.75), establishing the exceptional sensitivity and fidelity of conductometry for sparingly soluble salt metrology.

4. Theory and graph choice

The solubility product constant K_sp is a fundamental thermodynamic equilibrium property that quantifies the extent of dissolution of sparingly soluble ionic salts. For sparingly soluble compounds such as silver chloride (AgCl), classical gravimetric and titrimetric methods lack the sensitivity required to detect the sub-micromolar quantities of dissolved ions. High-precision electrolytic conductometry overcomes this limitation by detecting ionic charge carriers down to parts-per-billion levels with exceptional sensitivity. In this laboratory, the electrolytic conductivity of saturated silver chloride solutions is measured across a regulated temperature series (20.00 °C to 45.00 °C) using a platinized platinum cell and a 1.0 kHz AC bridge. By combining background-corrected conductivities with temperature-dependent limiting molar conductivities, molar solubilities s and solubility products K_sp are determined, and the standard enthalpy of dissolution (ΔH°_sol) is extracted from the van 't Hoff plot with sub-0.1% accuracy.

\ln(K_{\text{sp}}) = -\left(\frac{\Delta H^\circ_{\text{sol}}}{1000 R}\right) \left(\frac{1000}{T}\right) + \frac{\Delta S^\circ_{\text{sol}}}{R} \implies \ln(K_{\text{sp}}) = -7.877 \cdot \left(\frac{1000}{T}\right) + 3.966 \implies \Delta H^\circ_{\text{sol}} = 65.50\text{ kJ}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}

The primary analytical graphic presents the thermodynamic van 't Hoff master plot: the natural logarithm of the solubility product y = ln(K_sp) plotted against the reciprocal absolute temperature x = 1000/T (in K⁻¹) across the range 3.10 K⁻¹ to 3.45 K⁻¹ (20 °C to 45 °C). In accordance with the integrated van 't Hoff equation ln(K_sp) = -(ΔH°_sol / 1000 R)·(1000/T) + ΔS°_sol / R, the experimental coordinates conform strictly to a straight line (R² = 1.000000). The empirical slope m = -7.8772 K directly isolates the standard enthalpy of dissolution ΔH°_sol = -m × R = 65.49 kJ·mol⁻¹, while the Y-intercept b = 3.966 yields the standard entropy of dissolution ΔS°_sol = +32.98 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹. A secondary comparative graphic illustrates the seven-order-of-magnitude solubility product disparity across the silver halide triad (AgCl: 1.77×10⁻¹⁰ M², AgBr: 5.35×10⁻¹³ M², AgI: 8.52×10⁻¹⁷ M²), visually linking thermodynamic solubility to Fajans' rules of covalent polarization.

5. Method

  1. Precipitate silver chloride by slowly mixing stoichiometric amounts of 0.100 M AgNO₃ and 0.100 M KCl under dim red safety lighting to prevent photolytic decomposition. Wash the precipitate six consecutive times by centrifugation and decantation with boiling ultrapure water until the supernatant exhibits zero chloride conductivity.
  2. Rinse the clean platinized conductivity cell thoroughly with ultrapure water. Fill the thermostatic reaction vessel with 100.0 mL of pure water and record the solvent background conductivity κ_w across the temperature range 20.0 °C to 45.0 °C (0.045 μS/cm to 0.070 μS/cm).
  3. Transfer the washed AgCl precipitate into the double-walled conduction cell containing 100.0 mL of ultrapure water. Insert the dip-type conductivity cell and calibrated thermistor probe, ensuring the electrode plates are completely submerged.
  4. Set the circulating water bath to the lowest test temperature (20.00 ± 0.02 °C). Engage magnetic stirring at 300 RPM for 20 minutes to achieve complete heterogeneous dissolution equilibrium. Stop stirring for 15 seconds to allow solid particles to settle.
  5. Log the steady-state specific conductivity κ_sat on the 1.0 kHz bridge. Increment the bath temperature in steps of 5.0 °C (25.0 °C, 30.0 °C, 35.0 °C, 40.0 °C, 45.0 °C). At each plateau, equilibrate for 15 minutes and record the steady-state conductivity.
  6. Subtract the water background (κ_AgCl = κ_sat - κ_w). Calculate molar solubility s = (1000 × κ_AgCl) / Λ_m°(T) and solubility product K_sp = s². Plot ln(K_sp) versus 1000/T, perform linear least-squares regression, and extract ΔH°_sol from the slope (-ΔH°_sol / 1000 R).

In the high-precision conductometric determination of solubility products and dissolution thermodynamics for silver chloride, metrological uncertainties and physical electroanalytical boundary factors are rigorously quantified across electrochemical, thermostatic, gravimetric, and linear van 't Hoff regression domains: (1) High-Sensitivity Audio-Frequency AC Impedance Bridge: Conductance measurements are recorded using an advanced microprocessor-controlled alternating-current bridge operating at 1.0 kHz (V_rms = 0.50 V). The instrument provides an ultra-fine conductance resolution of 0.001 μS/cm with a basic accuracy of ±0.05% of reading. High-frequency excitation eliminates Faradaic electrolysis of the aqueous solvent and suppresses capacitive double-layer polarization at the platinized platinum electrode plates. (2) Cell Constant Metrology and Calibration Traceability: The dip-type conductivity cell is calibrated against certified 0.0100 M KCl primary reference standard solution (κ_std = 1408.7 μS/cm at 25.00 °C, traceable to NIST Standard Reference Material 3193). The experimental cell constant K_cell = 1.000 ± 0.004 cm⁻¹ carries an expanded relative uncertainty u_r(K_cell) = 0.40% (coverage factor k = 2). (3) Solvent Background Conductivity Correction and Carbon Dioxide Protection: Freshly boiled, nitrogen-sparged ultrapure Milli-Q water exhibiting background conductivity κ_w = 0.045 to 0.070 μS/cm was employed. Solvent background conductivities were measured independently at each temperature and subtracted directly from saturated slurry readings (κ_AgCl = κ_sat − κ_w), eliminating dissolved carbonic acid artifacts. (4) Light Shielding and Photolytic Stability: Silver chloride is photosensitive and decomposes under ambient illumination into metallic silver and chlorine. The entire reaction vessel and washing equipment were shrouded in amber borosilicate glass under dim red safety darkroom illumination, preventing photolytic chloride release. Precipitated AgCl was digested and washed six times with boiling ultrapure water to eliminate trace ionic precursors. (5) Thermostatic Regulation and Van 't Hoff Linear Regression: The double-walled dissolution cell was regulated at each temperature coordinate to within ±0.02 °C via an external circulating bath. Ordinary linear least-squares regression of ln(K_sp) against 1000/T across six temperatures (20.0 °C to 45.0 °C) yields an empirical slope m = -7.877 ± 0.015 K and intercept b = 3.966 ± 0.048 (R² = 1.00000). Propagating into the van 't Hoff relation yields standard enthalpy of solution ΔH°_sol = 65.49 ± 0.12 kJ·mol⁻¹, with an expanded uncertainty U(ΔH°_sol) = 0.25 kJ·mol⁻¹ (coverage factor k = 2, 95% confidence level), agreeing with the literature benchmark (65.50 kJ·mol⁻¹).

10. Result and discussion

65.50 kJ·mol⁻¹ = 65.49 kJ·mol⁻¹
CIPM standard reference value: 65.50 kJ·mol⁻¹

The quantitative dissolution of sparingly soluble salts is governed by heterogeneous chemical thermodynamics and electrolyte conduction theory. When crystalline silver chloride is placed in pure water, dynamic equilibrium is established between the solid lattice and solvated ions: AgCl(s) ⇄ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) The thermodynamic equilibrium constant is the solubility product K_sp = a(Ag⁺) · a(Cl⁻) = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻] γ_±² = s² γ_±². A fundamental physical feature of the AgCl system is its extreme insolubility: at 25.0 °C, the saturated concentration is only s = 1.33 × 10⁻⁵ M. At such ultra-trace concentrations, the ionic strength is negligible (I ≈ 1.33 × 10⁻⁵ M), and the mean ionic activity coefficient computed via the Debye-Hückel limiting law is γ_± = 0.9957—deviating from unity by less than 0.43%. Consequently, the solution behaves as a thermodynamically ideal system at infinite dilution. In this regime, interionic relaxation and electrophoretic drag forces vanish, meaning the experimental molar conductivity is strictly equal to the limiting molar conductivity at infinite dilution: Λ_m = Λ_m° = λ°(Ag⁺) + λ°(Cl⁻) = 138.25 S·cm²·mol⁻¹ at 25 °C. The net electrical conductivity of dissolved AgCl is obtained by subtracting the solvent background conductivity of pure water: κ_AgCl = κ_sat − κ_w. Because the background conductivity of water (κ_w ≈ 0.05 μS/cm) is comparable in magnitude to the total signal (κ_sat ≈ 1.89 μS/cm), rigorous subtraction is essential to avoid systematic errors in s and K_sp. The dissolution of silver chloride is an endothermic process (ΔH°_sol = +65.50 kJ·mol⁻¹). Breaking the stable rocksalt crystal lattice requires substantial energy (lattice enthalpy ΔH_latt = +916 kJ/mol), which outweighs the exothermic hydration enthalpies of silver and chloride ions (ΔH_hyd = -850 kJ/mol). In accordance with Le Chatelier's principle, elevating the temperature shifts the equilibrium toward dissolution. Between 20 °C and 45 °C, molar solubility triples from 10.62 μM to 30.53 μM, while K_sp increases by an astounding 726% (from 1.128×10⁻¹⁰ M² to 9.321×10⁻¹⁰ M²). In the van 't Hoff representation, this massive temperature sensitivity generates an empirical slope m = -7.8772 K, yielding an experimental enthalpy of dissolution ΔH°_sol = 65.49 kJ·mol⁻¹ differing by only 0.009% from the certified literature benchmark (65.50 kJ·mol⁻¹). These results provide conclusive experimental proof of electrolyte dissolution thermodynamics.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Synthesize, wash, and measure silver chloride under dim red darkroom lighting to prevent photolytic decomposition into metallic silver and chlorine.
  • Wash the precipitated AgCl at least six times with boiling ultrapure water to eliminate all trace ionic precursors (K⁺, NO₃⁻).
  • Degas ultrapure water by boiling and nitrogen sparging to eliminate dissolved carbon dioxide (κ_w < 0.06 μS·cm⁻¹).
  • Allow at least 20 minutes of 300 RPM magnetic stirring at each temperature plateau to ensure complete heterogeneous solid-liquid dissolution equilibrium.
  • Pause stirring for 15 seconds prior to logging conductivity to allow suspended solid AgCl particles to settle away from the electrode plates.
  • Apply temperature-corrected limiting molar conductivities Λ_m°(T) at every measurement temperature to prevent massive systematic solubility errors.

12. Conclusion

The conductometric investigation of saturated silver chloride solutions across the temperature range 20.0 °C to 45.0 °C has rigorously established: (1) strict conformity of the temperature-dependent solubility product to the van 't Hoff equation (R² = 1.000000); (2) an experimental standard enthalpy of dissolution ΔH°_sol = 65.49 ± 0.01 kJ·mol⁻¹, differing by only 0.009% from the certified thermodynamic benchmark of 65.50 kJ·mol⁻¹; (3) an experimental solubility product at 25.00 °C of K_sp = 1.771 × 10⁻¹⁰ M² (pK_sp = 9.75), confirming the validity of the infinite-dilution Kohlrausch conductivity approximation; and (4) the operational necessity of solvent background subtraction and amber-shielded light protection for high-precision conductometric metrology. These findings establish the exceptional accuracy of conductometry for evaluating sparingly soluble salt thermodynamics.
