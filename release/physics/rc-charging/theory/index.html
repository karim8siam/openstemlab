RC charging time constant theory

Understand rc charging time constant: the equations, assumptions, supporting concepts and measurement method.

WorkbenchDetailed theoryMethodMy resultsExample report
THE WORKING RELATIONSHIP
Vc = E(1 − exp(−t/τ)); ln[E/(E − Vc)] = mt + b; τ = 1/m

Keep the physical quantities, graph variables and units consistent.

01Charge storage and voltage

A capacitor stores equal and opposite charge on its conductors. For a linear capacitor, stored charge is proportional to its terminal voltage. Initially discharged means Vc(0) = 0. With finite series resistance the capacitor voltage changes continuously; current initially has its largest value and then decreases.

Q = C Vc; I = dQ/dt = C dVc/dt
Can the capacitor voltage jump instantly in this finite-current model?

No. An instantaneous voltage step would require an impulse of charging current.

02Derive the charging equation

Kirchhoff’s loop rule gives E = IR + Vc. Substitute I = C dVc/dt and solve the first-order differential equation with Vc(0) = 0. The voltage difference E − Vc decays exponentially even though Vc itself grows. These equations require a constant source, constant R and C, and negligible leakage and instrument loading.

RC dVc/dt + Vc = E; Vc(t) = E[1 − exp(−t/RC)]
Which quantity decays exponentially during charging?

The remaining difference E − Vc, and also the charging current.

03What one time constant means

Define τ = RC. After one τ the voltage has reached 1 − 1/e, about 63.2%, of E and current has dropped to 1/e of its initial value. After three τ the voltage is about 95.0% of E. It approaches E asymptotically; no finite ideal time makes it exactly equal to E. For 10,000 Ω and 100 × 10⁻⁶ F, τ is 1.000 s.

τ = RC; Vc(τ) = 0.63212 E; I(t) = (E/R)exp(−t/τ)
Is τ the time to reach 100% of the final voltage?

No. It is the time to reach about 63.2% from an initially discharged state.

04Make the logarithm dimensionless

Rearrange E − Vc = E exp(−t/τ). Taking a natural logarithm of E/(E − Vc) gives a positive transformed variable y = t/τ. This ratio is dimensionless. Do not use log base ten without its conversion factor and do not take the logarithm of an unnormalized voltage value. Valid charging readings satisfy 0 ≤ Vc < E.

y = ln[E/(E − Vc)] = t/τ
What happens to ln[E/(E − Vc)] at Vc = E?

It is undefined because the denominator is zero; such a reading cannot be transformed.

05Fit the correct sign and gradient

Plot time t in seconds on the horizontal axis and y = ln[E/(E − Vc)] vertically. Fit y = mt + b. The gradient is positive, has units s⁻¹, and τ = 1/m. If instead plotting ln[(E − Vc)/E], the slope would be negative and τ = −1/m. Retain an intercept: a timing offset or incomplete initial discharge can shift it.

m = 1/τ; τ = 1/m; C = τ/R if R is independently known
For the positive logarithm used here, what does a gradient 2 s⁻¹ imply?

τ = 0.5 s.

06Sampling, transformation and uncertainty

With τ near one second, manual stopwatch-and-meter readings may be too slow; use synchronized data acquisition or an oscilloscope with adequate sampling. Rounding voltage to 0.001 V becomes increasingly influential as Vc approaches E, because dy/dVc = 1/(E − Vc). Source uncertainty is also amplified near the asymptote. The transformed observations therefore need not have equal uncertainty; an unweighted fit is a teaching simplification. Fit-only u(τ) = u(m)/m² excludes resistor tolerance, capacitor tolerance, loading and clock calibration.

∂y/∂Vc = 1/(E − Vc); ∂y/∂E = −Vc/[E(E − Vc)]; u(τ)/τ ≈ u(m)/|m|
Why avoid treating near-final transformed readings as equally precise?

A small voltage error is magnified when E − Vc is small.
