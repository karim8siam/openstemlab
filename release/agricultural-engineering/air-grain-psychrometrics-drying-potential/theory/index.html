Agricultural and physical foundations of Air-Grain Psychrometrics.

Psychrometrics—the thermodynamic study of moist air mixtures—governs all agricultural grain drying, greenhouse climatization, livestock barn ventilation, and cold storage operations. In a convection grain dryer, ambient air is heated sensibly before passing through the crop bed. During sensible heating, the dry-bulb temperature rises while absolute humidity ratio W (mass of water vapor per unit mass of dry air, g/kg) remains constant, causing relative humidity to drop dramatically and expanding the air's vapor pressure deficit. As this warm, dry air flows through moist grain, it undergoes an adiabatic evaporative cooling process along a constant wet-bulb (or constant enthalpy) line on the psychrometric chart: sensible heat from the air supplies the latent heat of vaporization (approx. 2400 kJ/kg H₂O), causing air dry-bulb temperature to fall while humidity ratio increases until the air reaches saturation (or equilibrium with grain moisture). The drying potential evaporative capacity ΔW = W_sat - W_in (g H₂O/kg dry air) represents the maximum water a kilogram of air can absorb in a single pass. In this laboratory investigation, an instrumented psychrometric duct evaluates evaporative drying potential across temperatures from 35°C to 70°C.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the difference between dry-bulb, wet-bulb, and dew-point temperatures?

Dry-bulb temperature is the true thermodynamic temperature of moist air. Wet-bulb temperature is the lowest temperature attainable by adiabatic evaporative cooling of water into air. Dew-point temperature is the temperature at which moist air must be cooled at constant pressure to reach 100% saturation and initiate condensation.

Describe the psychrometric path of air passing through a grain dryer.

The process has two stages: (1) Sensible heating through the heater, moving horizontally to the right on the psychrometric chart (increasing T_db at constant W), followed by (2) Adiabatic evaporative cooling through the grain bed, moving upward and to the left along a constant wet-bulb/enthalpy line as sensible heat converts into latent heat of vaporization.

How does heating ambient air increase its drying capacity?

Heating air increases its saturation vapor pressure exponentially. While the absolute moisture W remains constant during heating, the relative humidity drops sharply, creating a massive vapor pressure deficit between the dry air and moist grain that accelerates evaporation.

What is the latent heat of vaporization of water in grain compared to free water?

At free water surfaces, latent heat of vaporization is approx. 2400 kJ/kg at 25°C. In grains, water is bound by adsorptive forces to starch and protein; at low moisture contents, latent heat can be 10% to 25% higher (up to 2800 kJ/kg), requiring additional thermal energy to vaporize.

Explain Shedd's curves in agricultural grain aeration.

Shedd's curves (ASABE D272) plot the static pressure drop per unit depth of grain (Pa/m) against airflow velocity (m³/s per m² of floor area). They are used to calculate the fan static pressure required to force drying air through deep grain beds.

What is the consequence of overdrying grain in the bottom layers of a bin dryer?

In deep-bed drying, a drying front moves upward slowly. The bottom grain near the hot air inlet dries to extremely low moisture (< 8%) before the top layer begins to dry, causing energy waste, kernel cracking, and economic loss from selling grain below commercial market weight.

Experimental precautions and agricultural field safety

  • Ensure wet-bulb wicks are clean, saturated with distilled water, and aspirated at air velocities > 3.5 m/s to prevent radiation errors.
  • Allow thermal equilibrium to stabilize for at least 15 minutes after adjusting electric heater settings.
  • Verify airtight duct seals; ambient air infiltration downstream of the heater skews psychrometric mass balances.
  • Do not exceed 70°C drying temperature to avoid scorch and thermal degradation of grain endosperm.

Theoretical assumptions & biological limits

  • Assumes ideal adiabatic drying conditions; heat losses through uninsulated duct walls cause slight departures from constant enthalpy lines.
  • As grain moisture drops below 14%, grain water activity drops, preventing exhaust air from reaching 100% saturation.
  • Constant enthalpy model neglects the sensible heating of the grain kernel mass itself during initial startup.
  • Psychrometric chart equations assume standard atmospheric pressure (101.325 kPa); elevation adjustments are required at high altitudes.
