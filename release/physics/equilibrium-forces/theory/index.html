Parallel forces & beam equilibrium theory

Understand parallel forces & beam equilibrium: the equations, assumptions, supporting concepts and measurement method.

01General Conditions for Static Rigid-Body Equilibrium

In classical statics, an extended rigid body differs fundamentally from an idealized point particle because external forces can produce both linear translation and angular rotation. For a system of coplanar forces acting in the xy-plane, complete mechanical equilibrium demands that two independent vector conditions be fulfilled simultaneously. First, the vector sum of all external linear forces acting on the body must equal zero: Σ F = 0, which decomposes in two dimensions into two scalar equations: Σ F_x = 0 and Σ F_y = 0. This translational equilibrium condition guarantees that the center of mass experiences zero linear acceleration (a_cm = 0). Second, the algebraic sum of all external torques (moments) about any arbitrary axis perpendicular to the plane of action must equal zero: Σ τ_O = 0. This rotational equilibrium condition guarantees that the body experiences zero angular acceleration (α = 0). In a parallel force system oriented vertically along the y-axis, the horizontal force condition Σ F_x = 0 is satisfied trivially, leaving two non-trivial independent governing equations: Σ F_y = 0 and Σ τ_O = 0.

\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} \iff \sum F_x = 0, \; \sum F_y = 0; \quad \sum \boldsymbol{\tau}_O = \mathbf{0} \iff \sum \tau_z = 0
Why are two independent conditions required for rigid-body equilibrium, whereas only one is required for a point mass?

A point mass has no spatial extent, so all forces are automatically concurrent and cannot generate torque. An extended rigid body has physical dimensions, so forces applied at different locations create rotational moments that can accelerate the body in rotation even if the net linear force is zero (such as in a force couple).

02Non-Concurrent Coplanar Parallel Force Systems

A force system is classified as non-concurrent when the lines of action of the acting forces do not all intersect at a single common point. When all lines of action are mutually parallel, the forces can never intersect in Euclidean space. In our experimental beam apparatus, four parallel vertical forces act on the rigid horizontal beam: two upward contact reaction forces R_A and R_B exerted by the suspension dynamometers, and two downward gravitational forces W_bar (the distributed beam weight acting through the center of gravity x_cg) and W_load (the concentrated load acting at position x). Because all forces act vertically, translational equilibrium requires: Σ F_y = R_A + R_B - W_bar - W_load = 0. Rearranging gives the fundamental conservation of vertical load: R_A + R_B = W_bar + W_load. This equation establishes that the two supports must share the entire gravitational burden of the system, but translational equilibrium alone cannot determine how much force is borne by each individual support. The problem is statically indeterminate without invoking the second condition of equilibrium: the principle of moments.

\sum F_y = R_A + R_B - W_{\text{bar}} - W_{\text{load}} = 0 \implies R_A + R_B = W_{\text{total}}
Why is the translational equilibrium condition Σ F_y = 0 insufficient by itself to determine the two individual support reactions R_A and R_B?

Because a single algebraic equation contains two unknown variables (R_A and R_B). An infinite number of pairs (R_A, R_B) could satisfy the sum R_A + R_B = W_total. A second independent equation—provided by rotational moment equilibrium—is required to form a determinate 2×2 system with a unique physical solution.

03Selection of Moment Axes and Torque Invariance

A fundamental theorem of rigid-body statics proves that when a body is in translational equilibrium (Σ F = 0), the total external torque is completely independent of the choice of reference origin O. If the net torque is zero about one point, it is guaranteed to be zero about every point in the universe: τ_O' = τ_O + r_{O'/O} × (Σ F) = τ_O + 0 = 0. While any arbitrary point could theoretically be chosen as the origin for taking moments, selecting a point along the line of action of an unknown force is a powerful analytical technique. Choosing support A (x = x_A) as the moment axis eliminates reaction force R_A from the moment equation because its lever arm is zero: r_A = x_A - x_A = 0. Taking counter-clockwise moments as positive and clockwise moments as negative, rotational equilibrium about A yields: Σ τ_A = R_B · (x_B - x_A) - W_bar · (x_cg - x_A) - W_load · (x - x_A) = 0. Defining the support span d_AB = x_B - x_A, the single unknown reaction R_B is isolated directly, eliminating the need to solve simultaneous linear equations.

\sum \tau_A = R_B d_{AB} - W_{\text{bar}} (x_{\text{cg}} - x_A) - W_{\text{load}} (x - x_A) = 0
What happens if moments are evaluated about support B instead of support A?

Evaluating moments about support B eliminates R_B and yields an identical physical result for R_A: R_A d_AB = W_bar (x_B - x_cg) + W_load (x_B - x). Combining this with R_A + R_B = W_total gives the exact same expressions for both reactions, confirming torque invariance.

04Derivation of the Linear Support Reaction Equation

By expanding the rotational equilibrium condition about support A, we obtain a precise mathematical relationship describing how reaction force R_B varies as the concentrated load moves along the beam: R_B · d_AB = W_load · (x - x_A) + W_bar · (x_cg - x_A). Dividing both sides by the fixed support span d_AB gives: R_B(x) = (W_load / d_AB) · (x - x_A) + [W_bar · (x_cg - x_A) / d_AB]. Expanding the product and grouping terms with respect to the coordinate variable x yields the standard linear form: R_B(x) = S · x + b. Here, the slope is S = W_load / d_AB (in N m⁻¹), and the intercept is b = [W_bar · (x_cg - x_A) - W_load · x_A] / d_AB (in N). This derivation reveals that the right support reaction is a strictly linear function of load coordinate x. The derivative dR_B/dx = W_load / d_AB is constant, meaning that every equal increment of load translation shifts an identical increment of load force from support A to support B, perfectly reflecting the mechanical lever principle.

R_B(x) = \left(\frac{W_{\text{load}}}{d_{AB}}\right) x + \left[\frac{W_{\text{bar}}(x_{\text{cg}} - x_A) - W_{\text{load}} x_A}{d_{AB}}\right] = S x + b
Why does the rate of change of support reaction dR_B/dx depend only on the load weight W_load and the span d_AB, and not on the beam weight W_bar?

Because the beam self-weight W_bar is stationary and has a fixed center of gravity, contributing only a constant tare offset to the reactions. As the concentrated load translates by distance dx, the change in moment is entirely d(τ) = W_load · dx, so the shift in reaction force is dR_B = (W_load / d_AB) · dx.

05Parameter Extraction via Least-Squares Regression

By collecting experimental pairs (x_i, R_{Bi}) across multiple load stations and performing ordinary least-squares (OLS) regression, we extract the two physical parameters of the system with rigorous statistical error budgeting. The regression slope S minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals: S = [Σ (x_i - x̄)(R_{Bi} - R̄_B)] / [Σ (x_i - x̄)²]. From the slope S, the concentrated load weight is calculated directly as: W_load = S · d_AB = S · 0.800 N. Furthermore, from the regression intercept b = R̄_B - S · x̄, the self-weight of the beam can be independently recovered: W_bar = [b · d_AB + W_load · x_A] / (x_cg - x_A). For our symmetric geometry where x_A = 0.100 m, x_cg = 0.500 m, and d_AB = 0.800 m, this reduces to W_bar = [0.800 b + 0.100 W_load] / 0.400 = 2 b + 0.25 W_load. This linear least-squares analysis completely decouples the moving load from the stationary beam mass, verifying both static equilibrium criteria simultaneously.

W_{\text{load}} = S \cdot d_{AB}, \quad W_{\text{bar}} = \frac{b \cdot d_{AB} + W_{\text{load}} x_A}{x_{\text{cg}} - x_A}
How does least-squares regression prevent random scale-reading errors from biasing the calculated load weight?

Random reading errors at individual stations fluctuate symmetrically above and below the true line. OLS regression minimizes the variance across all five stations, averaging out random errors and reducing the standard uncertainty of the slope by a factor of approximately 1/√n.

06Metrological Uncertainties and Dynamometer Calibration

Experimental measurements of beam support reactions involve several quantifiable sources of metrological uncertainty. The primary uncertainty contributors are: (1) reading resolution of the tubular spring dynamometers (least count ±0.01 N), leading to a standard reading uncertainty u(R_B) = 0.01 / √12 ≈ 0.0029 N; (2) position scale resolution on the graduated beam (±0.001 m); (3) span measurement uncertainty u(d_AB); and (4) spring calibration non-linearities and thermal drift. According to the ISO Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM), the combined relative standard uncertainty in the estimated load weight satisfies: (u_W / W_load)² = (u_S / S)² + (u_d / d_AB)², where u_S is the standard error of the fitted slope. Crucially, tubular dynamometers must be zeroed in the vertical suspension orientation before taking measurements to eliminate internal spring self-weight tare errors.

\left(\frac{u_W}{W_{\text{load}}}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{u_S}{S}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{u_{d_{AB}}}{d_{AB}}\right)^2, \quad u_S = \sqrt{\frac{s^2}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}}
Why is it essential to zero the dynamometers in the vertical orientation rather than horizontally?

In the vertical orientation, gravity acts on the internal dynamometer spring, hook, and indicator rod. Zeroing vertically tares out this internal gravitational weight, ensuring that the scale reads zero only when zero external tension is applied.

07Engineering Applications: Simply Supported Beams and Bridge Design

The equilibrium of parallel non-concurrent forces on a supported beam is the fundamental conceptual model for structural and civil engineering design of simply supported bridges, floor joists, and overhead gantry cranes. When a heavy vehicle traverses a bridge girder, it acts as a moving concentrated load W_load. Structural engineers construct Influence Lines and Shear Force / Bending Moment Diagrams to determine the internal stresses produced throughout the beam. The internal shear force V(x) represents the algebraic sum of all vertical forces acting to one side of a cross-section: V(x) = R_A - Σ W_i. The internal bending moment M(x) represents the algebraic sum of moments: M(x) = R_A · x - Σ W_i · (x - x_i). The maximum bending moment M_max occurs where shear force changes sign (V = 0), dictating the required section modulus and flange thickness of steel I-beams or prestressed concrete girders to prevent structural failure.

V(x) = R_A - \sum_{x_i < x} W_i, \quad M(x) = R_A x - \sum_{x_i < x} W_i (x - x_i), \quad \frac{dM}{dx} = V(x)
Where does the maximum bending moment occur in a simply supported beam carrying a single concentrated load, and why?

The maximum bending moment occurs directly underneath the concentrated load. Mathematically, the derivative of the bending moment is the shear force (dM/dx = V). Underneath the load, the shear force abruptly changes sign from +R_A to -R_B, passing through zero, which defines the local mathematical maximum of M(x).
