THEORY AND UNIT OPERATIONS CONTEXT

Process engineering background & physical principles.

In real chemical reactors, packed absorption towers, and tubular contactors, fluid flow never achieves the idealized extremes of pure plug flow or perfect backmixing. Instead, non-ideal flow phenomena—such as velocity profile shearing, molecular diffusion, turbulent eddy mixing, stagnant dead zones, and channeling—cause fluid elements to spend varying amounts of time inside the vessel. P. V. Danckwerts (1953) formulated the foundational theory of Residence Time Distribution (RTD) to diagnose and quantify these non-idealities. When a narrow Dirac delta pulse of an inert non-adsorbing tracer (e.g., concentrated potassium chloride solution) is injected at the reactor inlet at t = 0, the exit tracer concentration curve C(t) defines the exit age distribution: E(t) = C(t) / ∫ C(t) dt. The first moment of E(t) yields the mean residence time: t_mean = ∫ t · E(t) dt = V_p / Q, where V_p is the internal liquid pore volume and Q is volumetric throughput. Consequently, the reciprocal mean residence time scales strictly linearly with flow rate: 1 / t_mean = (1 / V_p) · Q = S · Q. The second central moment gives the distribution variance σ^2 = ∫ (t - t_mean)^2 · E(t) dt, which quantifies the degree of axial backmixing. Fitting the one-dimensional Taylor-Aris axial dispersion model determines the dimensionless vessel Peclet number Pe = u · L / D_ax: σ^2 / t_mean^2 = 2 / Pe - 2 / Pe^2 · [1 - exp(-Pe)]. In this laboratory investigation, an instrumented packed column is tested with pulse tracer injections across flow rates from 50.0 to 300.0 mL/min to measure RTD curves, verify 1/t_mean = 0.00250 · Q (V_p = 400.0 mL), and determine the Peclet number Pe.
