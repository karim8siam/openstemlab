Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Excess Enthalpy of Mixing ITC.

Excess enthalpy of mixing (H^E, also referred to as heat of mixing or excess enthalpy of solution) is a critical thermodynamic property in chemical engineering process design, heat exchanger sizing, reactor thermal management, and flash separation calculations. For an ideal solution, the enthalpy of mixing is strictly zero (ΔH_mix^ideal = 0), meaning mixing pure components at constant temperature and pressure releases or absorbs no heat. In real mixtures, however, breaking like-molecule intermolecular bonds (A-A and B-B) and creating unlike-molecule bonds (A-B) results in an energy imbalance. In the binary ethanol-water system, mixing is exothermic at ambient temperatures (H^E < 0) due to strong cross-association hydrogen bonding between water molecules and ethanol hydroxyl groups, releasing significant heat. The molar excess enthalpy is modeled using the Redlich–Kister polynomial expansion: H^E = x_1 · x_2 · [A_0 + A_1 · (x_1 - x_2) + A_2 · (x_1 - x_2)^2 + ...], where x_1 and x_2 = 1 - x_1 are mole fractions and A_k are temperature-dependent empirical interaction coefficients. In a symmetric first-approximation formulation, excess enthalpy scales directly with the product of the mole fractions: H^E = A_0 · [x_1 · (1 - x_1)], where A_0 is the leading Redlich–Kister parameter. Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC) provides the highest precision method for measuring heats of mixing. Operating under active power-compensation feedback, the ITC sample cell (volume 1.40 mL) is maintained at strictly constant temperature (25.000 ± 0.0001 °C) while automated precision syringe injections (10.0 μL) deliver titrant into the stirred sample cell. Thermal compensation power pulses are integrated to quantify the enthalpy change of each injection with sub-microjoule resolution. In this laboratory experiment, an automated ITC unit measures the heat of mixing across composition products x_1·(1 - x_1) from 0.05 to 0.25 at 25.000 °C to determine the Redlich–Kister parameter A_0 = -3450.0000 J/mol.

H^E = A_0 · [x_1 · (1 - x_1)] = -3450.0000 · [x_1 · (1 - x_1)]

Thermodynamics of Solution Mixing & Excess Enthalpy

The thermodynamic properties of real liquid solutions deviate from ideal solution behavior. For an extensive property $M$, the excess property $M^E$ is defined as the difference between the actual solution property and the property of an ideal solution at the same temperature, pressure, and composition:

\[ M^E = M - M^{\text{ideal}} \]

Because an ideal solution exhibits zero enthalpy of mixing ($\Delta H_{\text{mix}}^{\text{ideal}} = 0$), the total enthalpy of mixing is identical to the molar excess enthalpy $H^E$:

\[ H^E = \Delta H_{\text{mix}} = H_m - \sum_{i=1}^C x_i H_i^* \]

where $H_m$ is the molar enthalpy of the mixture and $H_i^*$ is the molar enthalpy of pure component $i$. From the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation, excess enthalpy directly governs the temperature sensitivity of the excess Gibbs energy and activity coefficients:

\[ \left[ \frac{\partial (G^E / T)}{\partial T} \right]_P = -\frac{H^E}{T^2} \implies \left[ \frac{\partial \ln \gamma_i}{\partial T} \right]_P = -\frac{\bar{H}_i^E}{R T^2} \]

where $\bar{H}_i^E = \left( \frac{\partial(n_t H^E)}{\partial n_i} \right)_{T, P, n_{j \neq i}}$ is the partial molar excess enthalpy of component $i$.

Redlich–Kister Representation & Power-Compensation ITC

The composition dependence of excess enthalpy in binary mixtures is conventionally correlated by the Redlich–Kister expansion:

\[ H^E = x_1 \, x_2 \sum_{k=0}^{M} A_k (x_1 - x_2)^k = x_1 (1 - x_1) \left[ A_0 + A_1 (x_1 - x_2) + \dots \right] \]

For symmetric systems or across moderate composition ranges, the leading parameter $A_0$ captures the predominant magnitude:

\[ H^E = A_0 \cdot [x_1 \cdot (1 - x_1)] \]

In power-compensation ITC, the injection of titrant volume $\Delta V_{\text{inj}}$ containing $\Delta n_i$ moles produces an energy change $q_{\text{inj}}$ measured by feedback power adjustment:

\[ q_{\text{inj}} = \int (P_{\text{baseline}} - P(t)) \, d t \]

Cumulative summation of $q_{\text{inj}}$ divided by total solution moles yields the molar excess enthalpy $H^E$, allowing direct linear regression against $x_1 (1 - x_1)$ to evaluate $A_0 = -3450.0000\text{ J/mol}$.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How does power-compensation ITC measure endothermic and exothermic heats of mixing?

The ITC applies a small continuous baseline electrical power to the sample cell heater; when an exothermic reaction releases heat, the feedback controller reduces heater power to maintain zero differential temperature, and integrating the power drop yields the heat released.

Why is the heat of mixing for ethanol and water exothermic at 25 °C but endothermic at elevated temperatures?

At 25 °C, exothermic hydrogen bonding between water and ethanol and hydrophobic hydration dominate; at higher temperatures (above ~60 °C), thermal agitation disrupts hydrogen bonds, and energetic penalties of hydrocarbon disruption dominate, making H^E positive.

What is the relationship between excess Gibbs energy G^E and excess enthalpy H^E?

The Gibbs–Helmholtz relation states: [∂(G^E / T) / ∂T]_P = -H^E / T^2; this means excess enthalpy is directly responsible for the temperature dependence of liquid-phase activity coefficients.

Why must the first injection in an ITC titration experiment be discarded or minimized?

During syringe insertion and thermal equilibration, solute diffuses across the needle tip into the cell, making the effective concentration of the initial injection uncertain.

How does Redlich–Kister parameter A_0 relate to the infinite-dilution partial molar excess enthalpy?

At infinite dilution, the partial molar excess enthalpy of solute 1 in solvent 2 is obtained from the limit H_1^E,inf = lim(x_1->0) [∂(n_t H^E) / ∂n_1] = A_0 + A_1 + A_2 + ...; for a symmetric 1-parameter model, H_1^E,inf = A_0.

Why is degassing reagents under vacuum essential before performing ITC measurements?

Dissolved air in liquids comes out of solution when heated or mixed, forming microbubbles that create sharp baseline spikes, alter thermal conductivity, and generate severe acoustic cavitation noise.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Degas all liquids thoroughly prior to loading into the sample cell and syringe to prevent microbubble formation during stirring.
  • Inspect the injection needle tip for microscopic burrs or droplets before insertion to ensure accurate injection volumes.
  • Verify that the sample cell and syringe are cleaned with ethanol and deionized water and dried between successive runs.
  • Allow the calorimeter to achieve a flat, stable baseline drift (< 0.05 μcal/s/h) before initiating automated injection sequences.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (0.05 to 0.25 dimensionless).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
