4. Theory and graph choice

Artificial thermal drying of grain is essential to safely store crops harvested at high moisture, preventing post-harvest respiration, insect infestation, and fungal spoilage. In deep-bed grain dryers (cross-flow, concurrent-flow, or in-bin radial systems), the drying process is modeled mathematically by summing the performance of successive thin layers of grain. A thin layer is defined as a grain layer one or two kernels thick where drying air temperature, humidity, and velocity remain uniform throughout. Thin-layer drying kinetics are governed by internal moisture diffusion through the endosperm, germ, and pericarp according to Fick's second law. The moisture ratio MR = (M_t - M_e) / (M_0 - M_e) describes the fraction of removable moisture remaining at time t. While Newton's exponential model MR = exp(-k·t) assumes pure surface resistance, agricultural grains exhibit super-exponential diffusion behavior best captured by Page's empirical model: MR = exp(-k_p·t^n_p), where k_p is the drying rate constant and n_p is Page's exponent. Linearizing into ln[-ln(MR)] = n_p·ln(t) + ln(k_p) enables precise parameter estimation. In this laboratory, a laboratory thin-layer drying tunnel with continuous load-cell logging records drying curves across controlled temperatures.

ln(-ln(MR)) = n_p · ln(t) + ln(k_p) = 1.2500 · ln(t) - 4.5000

The governing physical relationship was linearized into the standard regression coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent axis x represents ln(Drying time t in min), and the dependent axis y represents ln(-ln(Moisture ratio MR)). The gradient m directly determines Page Model Time Exponent n_p, while the intercept b accounts for zero-load offsets, baseline tares, or reference thresholds. Linear transformation allows robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear solver instability or convergence failure. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays soil resistance gradients, flow exponents, drying kinetics, or power conversion efficiencies directly as linear slopes. This linear graphical formulation provides an immediate visual check of homoscedasticity, linearity, and potential soil shear failure, flow cavitation, or saturation limits.

5. Method

  1. Turn on the tunnel blower and PID heater, setting airflow to 1.2 m/s and air temperature to 50.0°C, allowing the empty duct to reach steady thermal equilibrium for 20 minutes.
  2. Determine initial grain moisture content M_0 via rapid moisture tester (24.5% d.b.). Tare the suspended perforated tray on the continuous balance.
  3. Evenly spread 300.0 g of moist paddy rice in a single kernel layer across the tray, immediately inserting it into the drying air stream and zeroing the timer at t = 0.
  4. Log instantaneous tray mass m(t) at 10, 15, 25, 40, 60, and 90 minutes as moisture evaporates into the conditioned air stream.
  5. Convert instantaneous mass into dry-basis moisture M_t, calculate Moisture Ratio MR = (M_t - M_e) / (M_0 - M_e), and compute the double logarithm ln[-ln(MR)].
  6. Plot ln[-ln(MR)] against ln(t), perform linear regression, and extract Page's drying exponent n_p and drying rate constant k_p.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Ensure grain is spread in a true monolayer; overlapping kernels create deep-bed temperature gradients that violate thin-layer assumptions.
  • Maintain continuous airflow during weighing; never remove the tray from the air stream during data logging.
  • Ensure drying air velocity is sufficient (> 0.5 m/s) to ensure that external boundary layer resistance is negligible compared to internal diffusion.
  • Do not exceed 55°C when drying seed grain to avoid denaturing seed proteins and destroying germination viability.

Page's model is purely empirical; its parameters n_p and k_p do not directly represent fundamental physical transport properties like Fickian diffusivity. Evaluated using rough paddy rice (initial moisture 24.5% d.b., equilibrium moisture 9.2% d.b.) dried under forced air at 50°C and 1.2 m/s face velocity.
