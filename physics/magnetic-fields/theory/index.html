Magnetic Fields & Tangent Galvanometer theory

Understand magnetic fields & tangent galvanometer: the equations, assumptions, supporting concepts and measurement method.

01The Biot-Savart Law and Magnetic Fields of Electric Currents

The quantitative law governing magnetic fields produced by electric currents was deduced empirically by Jean-Baptiste Biot and Félix Savart in 1820. For an infinitesimal filamentary current element I dℓ carrying steady electric current I, the differential magnetic field dB at a field point located at position vector r relative to the element is given by: dB = (μ₀ / 4π) [I (dℓ × r̂) / r²], where μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T m A⁻¹ is the magnetic permeability of free space. To evaluate the magnetic field produced at the center of a flat circular coil of radius R having N tightly wound turns, we integrate around the circumference. At every point on the circle, the current tangent vector dℓ is perpendicular to the radial vector r̂ (dℓ · r̂ = 0, |dℓ × r̂| = dℓ). Since every element is at the constant distance r = R, the integral evaluates as: B_center = (μ₀ N I / 4π R²) ∮ dℓ = (μ₀ N I / 4π R²) (2πR) = (μ₀ N I) / (2R). The direction of the magnetic field vector is strictly normal to the plane of the coil, directed along the coil axis in accordance with the right-hand grip rule (curling the fingers of the right hand in the direction of conventional current leaves the thumb pointing along the magnetic field). Defining the magnetic coil constant G = (μ₀ N) / (2R), the central field is simply B_coil = G I. Along the central axis at distance z from the coil plane, the field diminishes according to the axial formula: B_z(z) = [μ₀ N I R²] / [2 (R² + z²)³/²]. At large distances (z ≫ R), this expression simplifies to the dipole field B_z ≈ (μ₀ / 4π) (2m / z³), where m = N I A = N I (π R²) is the magnetic dipole moment of the coil.

d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I (d\vec{\ell} \times \hat{r})}{r^2}, \qquad B_{\text{center}} = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2 R} = G I, \qquad B_z(z) = \frac{\mu_0 N I R^2}{2 (R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}
Why does the magnetic field at the center of a circular coil increase linearly with the number of turns N?

By the principle of linear superposition in magnetostatics, each individual turn carries the same current I in the same rotational direction, contributing an identical magnetic field dB = μ₀ I / (2R). Summing over N closely wound turns yields B = N (μ₀ I / (2R)) = μ₀ N I / (2R).

02The Tangent Law of Magnetism and Galvanometer Metrology

The operational principle of the tangent galvanometer is based on the tangent law of magnetism, which governs the equilibrium orientation of a magnetic compass needle subjected to two orthogonal magnetic fields. Let a small magnetic needle of magnetic dipole moment M and pole strength p be freely pivoted at its center on an agate jewel bearing. When the coil of the galvanometer is aligned with its plane parallel to the magnetic meridian, Earth's horizontal magnetic field B_H exerts a restoring torque on the needle that attempts to align it with magnetic North: τ_Earth = M B_H sin θ, where θ is the deflection angle away from the meridian. Simultaneously, when direct current I passes through the coil windings, it produces an axial magnetic field B_coil strictly perpendicular to the magnetic meridian (directed East-West). This coil field exerts a deflecting torque on the needle: τ_coil = M B_coil cos θ. In static equilibrium, the two opposing torques must balance exactly: M B_coil cos θ = M B_H sin θ. Dividing both sides by M cos θ yields the tangent law: B_coil = B_H tan θ. Substituting the Biot-Savart expression B_coil = (μ₀ N I) / (2R) gives: (μ₀ N I) / (2R) = B_H tan θ. Rearranging for current I yields: I = [2 R B_H / (μ₀ N)] tan θ = K tan θ, where K = (2 R B_H) / (μ₀ N) is called the reduction factor of the tangent galvanometer. The reduction factor K represents the specific current required to produce an angular deflection of exactly 45° (since tan 45° = 1). For the tangent law to remain strictly valid, the needle must be short compared to the coil radius (2ℓ ≪ R) so that its poles remain within the uniform central field zone.

B_{\text{coil}} = B_H \tan\theta \iff \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2 R} = B_H \tan\theta \implies I = K \tan\theta, \quad K = \frac{2 R B_H}{\mu_0 N} = \frac{B_H}{G}
Why must the compass needle at the center of a tangent galvanometer be very short in comparison to the diameter of the coil?

The Biot-Savart formula B = μ₀ N I / (2R) holds strictly at the exact geometric center of the coil. A short needle (length ≪ R) ensures that both magnetic poles remain entirely within the uniform central field region without experiencing non-uniform field gradients near the wire turns.

03Earth's Geomagnetic Field and the Magnetic Meridian

Earth acts as a giant magnetic dipole tilted at an angle of approximately 11.3° relative to its rotational axis, generated by turbulent magnetohydrodynamic convection of molten iron and nickel in the outer core (the geodynamo). At any point on Earth's surface, the total geomagnetic field vector B can be decomposed into two orthogonal components: a horizontal component B_H directed along the local magnetic meridian toward the magnetic North pole, and a vertical component B_V directed downward into the Earth in the Northern Hemisphere (or upward in the Southern Hemisphere). The angle that the total field vector makes with the horizontal plane is the magnetic dip (inclination angle I_d), related by: tan I_d = B_V / B_H, with B = √(B_H² + B_V²). At the magnetic equator, B_V = 0 and I_d = 0°, so the entire field is horizontal; at the magnetic poles, B_H = 0 and I_d = 90°, so the field is purely vertical. In tangent galvanometer measurements, only the horizontal component B_H acts on the horizontally pivoted compass needle, because the vertical component B_V exerts a vertical downward force that is absorbed by the mechanical reaction of the jewel pivot. The magnetic meridian is the vertical plane passing through the magnetic North and South poles at the observation site. Aligning the vertical plane of the galvanometer coil precisely parallel to the magnetic meridian is an absolute prerequisite: if the coil is misaligned by angle α, the coil field is no longer perpendicular to B_H, distorting the tangent relation into tan θ = (B_coil cos α) / (B_H ± B_coil sin α).

\vec{B} = \vec{B}_H + \vec{B}_V, \qquad \tan I_d = \frac{B_V}{B_H}, \qquad B = \sqrt{B_H^2 + B_V^2}, \qquad B_H \approx 3.50 \times 10^{-5} \text{ T}
Why must the plane of the tangent galvanometer coil be aligned strictly parallel to the magnetic meridian before taking measurements?

The tangent law (B_coil = B_H tan θ) requires the magnetic field of the coil B_coil to be strictly perpendicular to Earth's horizontal field B_H. Aligning the coil plane in the magnetic meridian ensures B_coil (which is perpendicular to the coil plane) is exactly orthogonal to B_H.

04Galvanometer Sensitivity and the Optimum Deflection Window

The precision of a tangent galvanometer depends strongly on the angular deflection θ at which readings are collected. The absolute current sensitivity S_I is defined as the change in angular deflection per unit change in current: S_I = dθ / dI. Differentiating the tangent relation I = K tan θ with respect to θ yields: dI / dθ = K sec² θ, so S_I = dθ / dI = 1 / (K sec² θ) = (cos² θ) / K. This shows that the absolute sensitivity is highest near zero deflection (cos 0° = 1) and drops to zero as θ approaches 90°. However, experimentalists are primarily concerned with minimizing the relative fractional error in current measurement: dI / I. Dividing dI = K sec² θ dθ by I = K tan θ yields: dI / I = (sec² θ / tan θ) dθ = [1 / (sin θ cos θ)] dθ = [2 / sin 2θ] dθ. To minimize the fractional current error dI / I for a given pointer reading resolution dθ, the denominator sin 2θ must be maximized. The sine function reaches its absolute maximum of 1 when 2θ = 90°, which corresponds to an optimum deflection angle of θ = 45°. At θ = 45°, dI / I = 2 dθ. In contrast, for very small deflections (e.g. θ = 10°, sin 20° = 0.342), dI / I = 5.85 dθ (nearly three times worse); for large deflections (e.g. θ = 80°, sin 160° = 0.342), dI / I is similarly degraded. Therefore, rigorous laboratory protocol dictates adjusting the series rheostat so that galvanometer deflections fall strictly within the optimum window between 30° and 60° (where sin 2θ ≥ 0.866).

S_I = \frac{d\theta}{dI} = \frac{\cos^2\theta}{K}, \qquad \frac{dI}{I} = \frac{2}{\sin 2\theta} d\theta \implies \left(\frac{dI}{I}\right)_{\text{min}} \iff \theta = 45^\circ
At what deflection angle θ does a tangent galvanometer have maximum fractional current measurement accuracy, and why?

Maximum accuracy occurs at θ = 45°. The fractional current error is dI / I = (2 / sin 2θ) dθ, which reaches its minimum when sin 2θ is maximized (sin 2θ = 1 at 2θ = 90°, hence θ = 45°).

05Commutation, Parallax Elimination, and Systematic Error Cancellation

High-accuracy magnetic measurements require systematic cancellation of mechanical, optical, and orientation artifacts. The tangent galvanometer incorporates three primary metrological safeguards: bilateral pointer reading, an anti-parallax mirror, and a Pohl current reversing commutator. First, the compass needle carries a long, lightweight aluminum pointer mounted perpendicular to the magnetic needle. Both ends of the pointer are read on the circular protractor scale (East and West tips). Averaging the two tips cancels any eccentricity error arising from the jeweled pivot not being situated at the exact geometric center of the degree circle. Second, beneath the glass window of the compass box lies a concentric plane mirror ring. When taking a reading, the observer must position their eye so that the fine tip of the pointer directly covers its own optical reflection in the mirror, eliminating sighting parallax error. Third, a Pohl four-terminal commutator switch is connected in the circuit. After recording readings for direct current flow (θ₁), the commutator is thrown to invert current through the coil, causing the needle to deflect in the opposite direction (θ₂). Averaging the four pointer readings [θ = (θ₁_left + θ₁_right + θ₂_left + θ₂_right) / 4] cancels: (1) any zero-offset bending of the aluminum pointer, (2) residual leveling tilt of the coil, and (3) small residual angular misalignment between the coil plane and the magnetic meridian.

\theta = \frac{\theta_{1,\text{east}} + \theta_{1,\text{west}} + \theta_{2,\text{east}} + \theta_{2,\text{west}}}{4}, \qquad \delta\theta_{\text{parallax}} \to 0, \qquad \delta\theta_{\text{eccentricity}} \to 0
Why is a current reversing commutator used to take readings with both direct and reversed currents in each trial?

Reversing the current and reading both ends of the pointer across four positions cancels out: (1) pointer zero-offset bending error, (2) residual coil leveling tilt, and (3) minor misalignment between the coil plane and the magnetic meridian.

06Linear Regression Formulation and OLS Parameter Isolation

To determine Earth's horizontal field B_H and the galvanometer reduction factor K with statistical rigor, experimentalists vary the coil current I across a calibrated series (e.g. 50.0 to 200.0 mA) and measure the corresponding mean deflection angle θ. From the fundamental tangent relation tan θ = [μ₀ N / (2 R B_H)] I, designating current x = I (in amperes) as the independent variable and y = tan θ (dimensionless) as the dependent variable transforms the physical law into the canonical linear regression model: y = m x + b. Here, the regression slope m represents the ratio of the geometric coil constant G to Earth's horizontal field: m = G / B_H = (μ₀ N) / (2 R B_H). Inverting this relation isolates Earth's horizontal field directly: B_H = G / m = (μ₀ N) / (2 R m). Furthermore, the reduction factor of the galvanometer is simply the reciprocal of the regression slope: K = 1 / m. The regression intercept b serves as an indispensable diagnostic: theoretically b = 0, so any statistically significant non-zero intercept detects residual magnetic fields from nearby DC wiring, uncompensated stray iron magnetization, or imperfect commutator symmetry. Ordinary least-squares (OLS) regression minimizes the sum of squared tangent residuals, averaging out random pointer sighting errors and providing unbiased slope and standard error estimates.

\tan\theta = m I + b \implies m = \frac{\mu_0 N}{2 R B_H} = \frac{G}{B_H} \iff B_H = \frac{\mu_0 N}{2 R m}, \qquad K = \frac{1}{m}
When plotting tan θ on the vertical axis against current I on the horizontal axis, what does the regression slope m represent?

The slope m = tan θ / I equals the coil constant divided by Earth's horizontal field: m = G / B_H = (μ₀ N) / (2 R B_H). Dividing the known coil constant G by the slope m directly isolates Earth's horizontal field B_H = G / m.

07Helmholtz Coils, Magnetic Shielding, and Modern Applications

While a single circular coil produces a localized magnetic field that falls off rapidly along the axis (B ∝ 1 / z³), Hermann von Helmholtz demonstrated in 1849 that pairing two identical coaxial circular coils separated by an axial distance equal to their radius (d = R) creates an extraordinarily uniform magnetic field throughout the central inter-coil volume. Expanding the axial magnetic field in a Taylor series about the central midpoint (z = 0), symmetry ensures that all odd derivatives vanish: dB/dz = 0, d³B/dz³ = 0. By choosing the coil separation d = R, the second derivative also vanishes identically: d²B/dz² = 0. Consequently, the first non-zero spatial variation occurs at the fourth derivative (d⁴B/dz⁴), providing a uniform magnetic field volume with spatial homogeneity exceeding 99.9%. Today, Helmholtz and Maxwell coil systems are essential tools in modern science and engineering: they are used to cancel Earth's magnetic field in magnetically shielded cleanrooms for atomic clock fountain experiments, calibrate fluxgate and atomic optical magnetometers on space exploration probes, test magnetometers for aerospace guidance, and provide homogeneous bias fields in electron spin resonance (ESR) and low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy.

B_{\text{Helmholtz}} = \left(\frac{4}{5}\right)^{3/2} \frac{\mu_0 N I}{R} \approx 0.7155 \frac{\mu_0 N I}{R}, \qquad \left.\frac{d^2 B_z}{dz^2}\right|_{z=0} = 0 \iff d = R
How does the Helmholtz coil configuration improve upon the single circular coil of a tangent galvanometer?

Helmholtz coils place two identical coils of radius R separated by distance d = R. This geometry makes both the first and second spatial derivatives of the axial field vanish (dB/dz = 0 and d²B/dz² = 0) at the center, creating an extremely uniform magnetic field over a much larger volume.
