Aerodynamic and physical foundations of Hohmann Orbital Transfer.

In astrodynamics and space mission design, transferring a spacecraft between two coplanar circular orbits with minimum propellant consumption is achieved using the classical Hohmann transfer ellipse, proposed by Walter Hohmann in 1925. The transfer orbit is an elliptical trajectory tangent to both the initial orbit of radius r_1 and the final target orbit of radius r_2. The semi-major axis of the transfer ellipse is a_trans = (r_1 + r_2) / 2. The transfer is executed using two instantaneous tangential thrust burns (delta-v impulses). The first burn Delta V_1 at periapsis accelerates the spacecraft from circular orbit velocity v_1 = sqrt(mu / r_1) onto the transfer ellipse: Delta V_1 = v_1 * [sqrt(2 * r_2 / (r_1 + r_2)) - 1]. After coasting through a true anomaly of 180 degrees (one-half of an orbit, taking transfer time t_trans = pi * sqrt(a_trans^3 / mu)), the spacecraft reaches apoapsis at radius r_2. The second burn Delta V_2 circularizes the orbit at final velocity v_2 = sqrt(mu / r_2): Delta V_2 = v_2 * [1 - sqrt(2 * r_1 / (r_1 + r_2))]. Total mission delta-v is Delta V_tot = Delta V_1 + Delta V_2. The propellant mass required scales exponentially with total delta-v according to Konstantin Tsiolkovsky's Rocket Equation: Delta m_fuel = m_0 * (1 - exp(-Delta V_tot / (I_sp * g_0))). In this laboratory experiment, an orbital maneuver simulator computes first-burn velocity increments Delta V_1 across transfer radius parameters from 0.08 to 0.38, linearizing the scaling against baseline LEO circular velocity v_1 = 7.780 km/s.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is a Hohmann transfer orbit and why is it propellant-optimal for coplanar circular orbits?

A Hohmann transfer is a two-impulse semi-elliptical orbital maneuver tangent to both the departure and arrival circular orbits. Because the thrust burns occur precisely tangential to the velocity vector at periapsis and apoapsis, they maximize the rate of mechanical energy change per unit delta-v (dE/dt = v * a_thrust), making it the most fuel-efficient two-burn transfer for radius ratios r_2 / r_1 < 11.94.

How does Tsiolkovsky's Rocket Equation determine the propellant required for a given delta-v?

Tsiolkovsky's equation relates delta-v to the mass ratio of the rocket: Delta V = I_sp * g_0 * ln(m_initial / m_final). Solving for propellant mass yields m_propellant = m_initial * [1 - exp(-Delta V / (I_sp * g_0))]. Because fuel mass scales exponentially with delta-v, minimizing mission delta-v is the paramount objective in astrodynamics.

What is the transfer time for a Hohmann orbit from Earth to Mars?

The semi-major axis of the Sun-centered transfer ellipse is a_trans = (r_Earth + r_Mars) / 2 approx (1.0 AU + 1.524 AU) / 2 = 1.262 AU. By Kepler's Third Law, the period of the full ellipse is T_trans = a_trans^(3/2) = 1.417 years. The one-way transfer from perihelion to aphelion takes half of this period: t_transfer = T_trans / 2 = 0.708 years approx 259 days (~8.5 months).

When is a three-burn bi-elliptic transfer superior to a two-burn Hohmann transfer?

When the ratio of final to initial orbit radius exceeds r_2 / r_1 = 11.94 (such as transfers to deep outer space), a bi-elliptic transfer—which fires a first burn to send the apoapsis far beyond the target orbit, a second burn at immense distance to raise periapsis, and a third burn to circularize—can require lower total delta-v than a Hohmann transfer, at the cost of vastly longer flight times.

How is an orbital plane change (inclination change) executed most efficiently?

An orbital plane change Delta i requires a velocity vector rotation Delta V_plane = 2 * v * sin(Delta i / 2). Because plane-change delta-v is directly proportional to orbital velocity v, it is vastly more efficient to execute plane changes at the highest possible altitude (lowest orbital velocity), typically combining the plane change with the apoapsis circularization burn of a Hohmann transfer.

What is a patched-conic approximation in interplanetary mission design?

The patched-conic approximation simplifies complex multi-body interplanetary trajectories by dividing the mission into three distinct two-body regimes: (1) a hyperbolic departure orbit inside Earth's gravitational Sphere of Influence (SOI); (2) a heliocentric Keplerian transfer ellipse around the Sun; and (3) a hyperbolic arrival capture orbit inside the target planet's SOI.

What is gravitational Oberth effect and how does it amplify rocket thrust efficiency?

The Oberth effect demonstrates that burning rocket propellant at high orbital speed (deep in a gravitational potential well at periapsis) generates vastly more mechanical energy than burning the same propellant at low speed. Because kinetic energy scales as v^2, applying an impulse Delta V yields Delta E = 0.5 * (v + Delta V)^2 - 0.5 * v^2 = v * Delta V + 0.5 * Delta V^2, which scales linearly with velocity v.

Experimental precautions and wind tunnel safety

  • Ensure the first burn is executed precisely tangential to the velocity vector; radial velocity components waste propellant delta-v.
  • Verify that finite-burn duration losses (gravity losses) are accounted for if using low-thrust electric or long chemical burns.
  • Confirm that the target body (e.g. Moon or planet) phase angle is correctly aligned at departure (Hohmann launch window).
  • Calculate combined plane-change delta-v at the nodal crossing to avoid expensive separate inclination burns.

Theoretical assumptions & flow limits

  • Hohmann transfer assumes impulsive (infinitesimally short) velocity changes; continuous low-thrust ion engines require spiral trajectories.
  • For radius ratios r_2 / r_1 > 11.94, a three-burn bi-elliptic transfer can be more propellant-efficient than a two-burn Hohmann transfer.
  • Assumes coplanar circular orbits; real planetary transfers require 3D patched-conics and interplanetary orbital eccentricity corrections.
  • Does not account for atmospheric drag perturbations during LEO departure.
