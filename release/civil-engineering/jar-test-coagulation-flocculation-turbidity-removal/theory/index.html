Civil engineering mechanics & structural foundations of Jar Test Coagulation.

In civil environmental water treatment engineering, raw surface waters from rivers and reservoirs contain suspended silt, clay, pathogenic microorganisms, and natural organic matter (NOM) present as negatively charged microscopic colloids (0.001 to 1.0 µm). Due to electrostatic repulsion governed by negative zeta potentials (-15 to -30 mV), colloidal particles remain indefinitely suspended in stable Brownian motion and cannot settle by gravity alone. Coagulation and flocculation destabilize this colloidal suspension. Developed in the 1920s, the standard Jar Test (ASTM D2035) uses a multi-paddle stirrer to optimize chemical dosing in municipal water treatment plants. Adding hydrolyzing metal coagulants—such as aluminum sulfate (alum, Al₂(SO₄)₃·14H₂O) or ferric chloride (FeCl₃)—destabilizes colloids through two primary mechanisms: (1) Charge neutralization: trivalent cations (Al³⁺, Al(OH)²⁺, Al₇(OH)₁₇⁴⁺) compress the electrical diffuse double layer and neutralize negative surface charges; and (2) Sweep flocculation: at higher dosages and neutral pH (6.5 to 7.5), amorphous aluminum hydroxide Al(OH)₃ precipitates en masse, physically entrapping and sweeping suspended particles into settleable flocs. In this laboratory experiment, six 1-liter water samples are dosed with alum from 10 to 40 mg/L, subjected to 1-minute rapid mixing (100 rpm), 20-minute slow flocculation (30 rpm), and 30-minute quiescent settling to calibrate residual turbidity reduction and establish the optimum economic dosage.

Colloid Stability and the DLVO Theory

Colloidal particles in natural waters remain in suspension due to electrostatic repulsion. According to Derjaguin, Landau, Verwey, and Overbeek (DLVO) theory, total inter-particle potential energy $V_T$ is the sum of attractive van der Waals forces $V_A$ and repulsive electrostatic double-layer forces $V_R$:

\[ V_T = V_A + V_R = -\frac{A_H}{12 \pi s^2} + 2 \pi \epsilon_0 \epsilon_r d \psi_0^2 \ln\left( 1 + e^{-\kappa s} \right) \]

where $A_H$ is the Hamaker constant, $s$ is separation distance, and $\kappa$ is the Debye-Hückel reciprocal screening length:

\[ \kappa = \left( \frac{2 e^2 N_A I}{\epsilon_0 \epsilon_r k_B T} \right)^{1/2} \]

Ionic strength $I = \frac{1}{2} \sum c_i z_i^2$ depends quadratically on ion valence $z_i$. The Schulze-Hardy rule dictates that trivalent ions ($Al^{3+}, Fe^{3+}$) are $z^6 \approx 700$ to $1000$ times more effective at compressing the double layer than monovalent ions ($Na^+$).

Hydrolysis Chemistry and Alkalinity Stoichiometry

When alum is dissolved in water, it hydrolyzes rapidly across successive aquo-complexes:

\[ \text{Al}^{3+} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{Al(OH)}^{2+} + \text{H}^+ \] \[ \text{Al(OH)}^{2+} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{Al(OH)}_2^+ + \text{H}^+ \] \[ \text{Al(OH)}_2^+ + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{Al(OH)}_3(s) + \text{H}^+ \]

Each mole of alum consumes 6 moles of bicarbonate alkalinity, releasing carbon dioxide:

\[ \text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3 \cdot 14\text{H}_2\text{O} + 6 \text{HCO}_3^- \to 2 \text{Al(OH)}_3(s) + 3 \text{SO}_4^{2-} + 6 \text{CO}_2 + 14 \text{H}_2\text{O} \]

Stoichiometrically, $1.0\text{ mg/L}$ of commercial alum consumes $0.50\text{ mg/L}$ of alkalinity as $\text{CaCO}_3$.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the distinction between coagulation and flocculation?

Coagulation is the rapid chemical process (seconds) where coagulant hydrolyzes, compresses the electrical double layer, and neutralizes negative colloidal surface charges; flocculation is the slow physical process (minutes) where gentle mixing promotes collisions between destabilized particles, forming visible, settleable flocs.

What are the four primary mechanisms of chemical coagulation?

(1) Electrical double layer compression; (2) Adsorption and charge neutralization; (3) Inter-particle polymer bridging; and (4) Sweep flocculation (entrapment within metal hydroxide precipitate).

Why is water alkalinity critical during alum coagulation?

Alum reacts with natural water bicarbonate alkalinity: Al₂(SO₄)₃·14H₂O + 6HCO₃⁻ → 2Al(OH)₃(s) + 3SO₄²⁻ + 6CO₂ + 14H₂O; if alkalinity is insufficient, the reaction consumes all buffering capacity, dropping pH below 5.5, which inhibits hydroxide precipitation and leaves toxic dissolved aluminum in treated water.

What is the Camp-Stein velocity gradient G, and why must it differ between rapid mix and flocculation?

Velocity gradient G = √(P / (μ·V)) quantifies mixing shear intensity; rapid mix requires high G (300 to 800 s⁻¹) for instantaneous chemical dispersion before hydrolysis completes; flocculation requires low G (20 to 50 s⁻¹) to promote particle collisions without shearing fragile aggregate flocs apart.

What happens if a water treatment plant overdoses coagulant?

Excess coagulant positively charges colloidal particle surfaces (charge reversal or restabilization), creating positive-positive repulsion that causes turbidity to rise again, wasting expensive chemicals and producing excessive chemical sludge.

How does the optimum pH window differ between alum and iron-based coagulants?

Alum operates within a narrow pH window of 6.0 to 7.8 (above pH 8, soluble aluminate Al(OH)₄⁻ forms); ferric salts operate over a much broader pH range of 4.5 to 9.5 and produce denser, faster-settling flocs.

Experimental precautions and laboratory safety

  • Inject coagulant aliquots simultaneously across all six test jars at the exact instant rapid mixing commences.
  • Extract supernatant turbidity samples strictly from 2.0 cm below the liquid surface to ensure consistent settling velocity cut-offs.
  • Avoid disturbing the settled bottom sludge layer when inserting the sampling pipettes.
  • Wipe nephelometric glass sample cuvettes with lint-free optical paper and silicone oil to eliminate fingerprint scattering.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The civil engineering physical models apply within the specified calibrated operational window (10.0 to 40.0 mg/L).
  • Assumes homogeneous, isotropic continuum material behavior without unmodeled micro-fracture anomalies.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • Extreme inelastic yielding or cyclic degradation requires non-linear constitutive continuum damage mechanics.
