Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Solid–Liquid Leaching Kinetics.

Solid–liquid extraction (leaching) is a fundamental separation operation utilized in metallurgical hydrometallurgy (copper and gold cyanidation), food processing (oil extraction from soybeans, coffee and tea brewing, sucrose extraction from sugar beets), and pharmaceutical botanical drug extraction. In leaching, a liquid solvent contacts a porous solid matrix, dissolving soluble solute species and transferring them into the bulk liquid extract. Leaching mechanisms involve three sequential rate-limiting steps: (1) solvent penetration and dissolution of solute within internal pores, (2) molecular intra-particle diffusion of dissolved solute through tortuous fluid-filled pores to the solid surface, and (3) convective mass transfer across the external fluid boundary layer into the bulk solvent. When leaching is performed in crosscurrent stages with fresh solvent additions, the residual solute mass fraction remaining in the underflow solid matrix x_R decreases exponentially with cumulative solvent-to-solid mass ratio S/F: x_R = x_0 · exp[-k · (S/F)], where k is the characteristic solvation and washing rate constant. In this laboratory investigation, oil extraction from flaked oilseed meal using food-grade hexane solvent is studied in a benchtop jacketed stirred extractor across solvent-to-solid ratios from 1.0 to 6.0 kg/kg to measure residual solute fraction x_R and evaluate the solvation rate constant k.

ln(x_R) = -k · (S/F) + c = -0.4200 · (S/F) - 0.1500

1. Transport Mechanics of Solid–Liquid Leaching

Solid–liquid leaching extracts soluble target components from an insoluble solid matrix. Key industrial examples include vegetable oil extraction from crushed seeds, caffeine extraction from coffee beans, and leaching gold from ore slurries using cyanide solutions.

2. Rate-Limiting Transport Steps

The overall extraction process comprises three steps in series:

  1. Internal Dissolution: Solvent penetrates capillary pores, dissolving soluble solid species.
  2. Intra-Particle Diffusion: Solute diffuses through the tortuous pore network to the external particle boundary: \[ J_A = -D_{eff} \frac{dC_A}{dr} \] where $D_{eff} = \frac{\varepsilon_{pore}}{\tau} D_{AB}$ is the effective pore diffusivity.
  3. External Boundary Layer Mass Transfer: Solute transfers across the external hydrodynamic film into bulk solvent: \[ N_A = k_c (C_{A,surf} - C_{A,bulk}) \]

3. Crosscurrent Leaching Model & Solvation Decay

In crosscurrent leaching with fresh solvent additions, the mass balance across stage $n$ with solvent ratio $(S/F)$ dictates exponential solute depletion:

\[ x_R = x_0 \exp\left[-k \left(\frac{S}{F}\right)\right] \]

Taking natural logarithms yields the linear model:

\[ \ln(x_R) = -k \left(\frac{S}{F}\right) + c \]

where $k$ is the leaching solvation rate constant and $c = \ln(x_0)$. Plotting $\ln(x_R)$ versus $(S/F)$ yields a straight line with slope $-k$, from which the required solvent consumption for a target residual purity is evaluated.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the difference between leaching and liquid–liquid extraction?

In leaching, the solute is dissolved or dispersed within an insoluble solid porous matrix, requiring intra-particle solid diffusion; in liquid-liquid extraction, all components are initially in the liquid phase.

Why is particle size reduction (milling or flaking) critical prior to leaching?

Milling increases specific surface area and ruptures cellular walls, dramatically shortening the intra-particle diffusion path length and accelerating extraction rates.

What is the underflow and overflow in industrial leaching circuits?

Overflow (miscella) is the clear pregnant solvent containing dissolved solute; underflow is the wet solid slurry containing insoluble inert solids and entrained solvent liquor.

How does counter-current leaching compare to crosscurrent leaching in solvent consumption?

Counter-current leaching uses solvent in series against solids moving in reverse, achieving high solute recovery with minimal fresh solvent; crosscurrent uses fresh solvent in each stage, consuming much larger solvent volumes.

What is residual solute retention in leached solid cakes?

Capillary forces retain a fraction of solute-laden solution in the porous spaces of the cake (solution holdup); thorough cake washing is required to displace this entrained solute.

Why is hexane universally used for commercial vegetable oil leaching?

Hexane has high selectivity for triglycerides, low boiling point (69 °C) for easy distillation recovery, low latent heat of vaporization, and does not dissolve water-soluble gums.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Hexane is highly volatile and flammable; operate all equipment under nitrogen purge in an explosion-proof hood.
  • Ensure reflux condensers are chilled to < 5.0 °C to prevent solvent vapor release.
  • Do not overheat the dry meal; thermal degradation degrades oil quality.
  • Allow complete solvent evaporation before final gravimetric weighing of extracted oil.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (1.0 to 6.0 kg/kg).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
