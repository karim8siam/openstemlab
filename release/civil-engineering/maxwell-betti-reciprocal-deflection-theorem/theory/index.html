Civil engineering mechanics & structural foundations of Maxwell-Betti Reciprocity.

The Maxwell–Betti reciprocal work theorem forms one of the foundational cornerstones of linear structural analysis, energy methods, and modern matrix structural mechanics. Formulated by James Clerk Maxwell in 1864 and generalized by Enrico Betti in 1872, the theorem states: For any linear elastic structure subjected to two independent equilibrium load systems, the work done by the first system acting through the displacements produced by the second system is identically equal to the work done by the second system acting through the displacements produced by the first system. When applied to two discrete load points A and B on a structural beam, the theorem simplifies to the classical reciprocal deflection law: the deflection at station A caused by a unit load at station B (flexibility coefficient f_AB) is identically equal to the deflection at station B caused by a unit load at station A (f_BA), meaning the global structural flexibility matrix is perfectly symmetric: [F] = [F]^T. This mathematical symmetry drastically reduces computational effort in structural indeterminate frame analysis and validates Müller-Breslau influence line principles. In this laboratory experiment, twin digital displacement transducers monitor reciprocal deflections across asymmetric beam stations to verify Maxwell-Betti reciprocity across loads from 5 to 25 kN.

δ_AB = 0.3150 · P_B

1. Energy Derivation of the Maxwell–Betti Reciprocal Theorem

Consider a general linear elastic, stable structural body supported by unyielding boundary constraints. Let System 1 consist of concentrated force $P_A$ applied at point $A$, producing displacement field $u_1(x)$, with specific displacements $\delta_{AA}$ at point $A$ and $\delta_{BA}$ at point $B$. Let System 2 consist of concentrated force $P_B$ applied at point $B$, producing displacement field $u_2(x)$, with specific displacements $\delta_{BB}$ at point $B$ and $\delta_{AB}$ at point $A$.

Apply System 1 first: the work done is $W_1 = \frac{1}{2} P_A \delta_{AA}$. Now apply System 2 while maintaining $P_A$: the additional work done includes the work of $P_B$ plus the cross-work of $P_A$ acting through displacement $\delta_{AB}$ produced by System 2:

\[ W_{total, (1 \to 2)} = \frac{1}{2} P_A \delta_{AA} + \frac{1}{2} P_B \delta_{BB} + P_A \delta_{AB} \]

Now reverse the loading sequence, applying System 2 first, followed by System 1:

\[ W_{total, (2 \to 1)} = \frac{1}{2} P_B \delta_{BB} + \frac{1}{2} P_A \delta_{AA} + P_B \delta_{BA} \]

Because the material is linear elastic and conservative, stored strain energy $U$ is a unique state function independent of the loading path ($W_{total, (1 \to 2)} = W_{total, (2 \to 1)}$). Equating the two expressions and canceling self-work terms yields Betti's reciprocal identity:

\[ P_A \delta_{AB} = P_B \delta_{BA} \]

Setting equal test loads $P_A = P_B = P$ directly yields Maxwell's reciprocal deflection law:

\[ \delta_{AB} = \delta_{BA} \]

2. Closed-Form Flexibility Formulation for Simply Supported Beams

For a simply supported beam of span $L$ and flexural rigidity $EI$, the deflection at position $x$ ($x \le a$) due to a concentrated load $P$ at position $a$ is obtained from the beam elastic curve:

\[ v(x) = \frac{P b x}{6 E I L} \left( L^2 - b^2 - x^2 \right) \]

where $b = L - a$. Substituting station coordinates $x_A = 300\text{ mm}$ and $a = x_B = 600\text{ mm}$ (with $b = 400\text{ mm}$):

\[ f_{AB} = \frac{\delta_{AB}}{P_B} = \frac{x_A (L - x_B)}{6 E I L} \left[ L^2 - (L - x_B)^2 - x_A^2 \right] \]

For the test beam with calibrated flexural rigidity $EI = 3.8095 \times 10^{10}\text{ N}\cdot\text{mm}^2$ and span $L = 1000\text{ mm}$:

\[ f_{AB} = \frac{300 \times 400}{6 \times (3.8095 \times 10^{10}) \times 1000} \left[ 1000^2 - 400^2 - 300^2 \right] = 5.250 \times 10^{-10} \times 750,000 = 0.3150\text{ mm/kN} \]

By exact mathematical symmetry, evaluating $f_{BA}$ (deflection at $x_B = 600$ due to load at $x_A = 300$) yields identically $f_{BA} = 0.3150\text{ mm/kN}$.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

State the Maxwell–Betti reciprocal deflection theorem in terms of flexibility coefficients.

For any linear elastic structure, the flexibility coefficient f_AB (deflection at station A due to unit load at B) is identically equal to f_BA (deflection at station B due to unit load at A): f_AB = f_BA.

How does the principle of conservation of energy prove the Maxwell-Betti theorem?

Total strain energy stored in a linear elastic body subjected to loads P_A and P_B is independent of the loading sequence; equating energy stored when P_A is applied first versus when P_B is applied first forces the cross-work terms to be identical: P_A δ_AB = P_B δ_BA.

What is the primary practical application of the reciprocal theorem in structural engineering?

The theorem proves that the structural flexibility matrix [F] and stiffness matrix [K] are symmetric ([K] = [K]^T), halving computational storage in finite element analysis and forming the foundation of the Müller-Breslau principle for influence lines.

Does Maxwell-Betti reciprocity hold for geometrically non-linear structures undergoing large deflections?

No; the theorem strictly requires linear elasticity (Hooke's law) and small-displacement geometry; large deflections or non-linear material plasticity violate the linear superposition principle upon which reciprocity relies.

Does the reciprocal theorem apply to rotational displacements and bending moments?

Yes; the generalized Betti theorem states that rotation at A due to unit moment at B equals rotation at B due to unit moment at A, and deflection at A due to unit moment at B equals rotation at B due to unit force at A.

Why is an isolated datum beam necessary when mounting displacement gauges in reciprocity testing?

If displacement gauges are mounted directly onto the loading frame, frame flexure under load introduces parasitic false deflection readings; mounting LVDTs on an isolated reference beam ensures true absolute beam displacement measurement.

Experimental precautions and laboratory safety

  • Ensure both station locations (x_A = 300 mm, x_B = 600 mm) are laid out with precision calipers to eliminate spatial alignment error.
  • Verify the beam operates strictly below its proportional limit to prevent non-linear plastic deformation.
  • Allow LVDT signals to settle for 15 seconds after each load increment to eliminate dynamic vibration artifacts.
  • Check that support knife-edges and rollers permit free rotation without binding friction.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The civil engineering physical models apply within the specified calibrated operational window (5.0 to 25.0 kN).
  • Assumes homogeneous, isotropic continuum material behavior without unmodeled micro-fracture anomalies.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • Extreme inelastic yielding or cyclic degradation requires non-linear constitutive continuum damage mechanics.
