4. Theory and graph choice

In 1931, Swedish physiologists Robin Fåhræus and Torsten Lindqvist discovered one of the most remarkable phenomena in microvascular hemodynamics: when whole blood flows through narrow cylindrical tubes with internal lumen diameters smaller than ~300 μm down to ~10 μm, the apparent relative viscosity of blood η_rel (normalized to plasma viscosity) decreases progressively as tube diameter narrows. This counter-intuitive reduction in flow resistance—the Fåhræus-Lindqvist effect—plays a profound physiological role, drastically reducing the cardiac pumping work required to perfuse the extensive microvascular networks of human organs. The physical mechanism underlying this phenomenon stems from red blood cell lateral migration toward the central high-velocity axis of the tube. This axial migration leaves an erythrocyte-depleted, cell-free marginal plasma layer along the vessel wall (thickness δ ≈ 2 to 4 μm). Because the low-viscosity plasma layer lubricates the high-shear region adjacent to the endothelium while the dense cellular core travels at higher average velocity, the overall tube hematocrit H_T becomes significantly lower than the feed reservoir hematocrit H_F (the Fåhræus effect). Over the microvascular diameter range from 20 μm to 95 μm, apparent relative viscosity η_rel scales linearly with capillary lumen diameter: η_rel = k_d · D + η_0, where k_d is the Fåhræus-Lindqvist dimensional slope (μm⁻¹). In this laboratory investigation, precision micro-capillary silica tubes perfused with anti-coagulated human blood evaluate pressure-flow scaling to quantify the Fåhræus-Lindqvist effect.

η_rel = k_d · D + η_0 = 0.0125 · D + 1.2500

The governing physiological relationship was linearized into the standard regression coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Capillary lumen diameter D (μm), and the dependent coordinate y represents Apparent relative blood viscosity η_rel (ratio). The gradient m directly determines Fåhræus-Lindqvist Slope k_d, while the intercept b accounts for baseline null offsets, pre-stress tares, or reference thresholds. Linear transformation allows robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear solver instability or convergence traps. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays sensor sensitivities, elastic moduli, hemodynamic resistances, or acoustic attenuation coefficients directly as linear slopes. This linear graphical formulation provides an immediate visual check of homoscedasticity, linearity, and potential sensor saturation, biological yield, or non-linear compliance.

5. Method

  1. Prepare fresh anti-coagulated human blood (Hct = 45.0%); centrifuge an aliquot to isolate plasma and measure plasma viscosity η_p on cone-plate viscometer (1.20 mPa·s).
  2. Mount 20 μm silica micro-capillary into the perfusion manifold, pre-wet with plasma to prevent bubble entrapment, and seal pressure ports.
  3. Apply pneumatic pressure drop ΔP to achieve high wall shear rate (γ̇_w > 500 s⁻¹), ensuring fully dispersed non-rouleaux flow.
  4. Determine steady volumetric flow rate Q via timed micro-gravimetric collection on ultra-microbalance over 180 seconds.
  5. Calculate apparent blood viscosity from Poiseuille's formula: η_app = [π · D⁴ · ΔP] / [128 · L · Q], and compute relative viscosity η_rel = η_app / η_p.
  6. Repeat standardized perfusion across tube diameters D = 35, 50, 65, 80, and 95 μm, recording apparent relative viscosity η_rel at each diameter.
  7. Plot apparent relative viscosity η_rel against capillary diameter D and extract slope k_d and baseline intercept η_0 via linear regression.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Ensure micro-capillary lumen is pristine; particulate debris in a 20 μm capillary causes catastrophic clogging.
  • Maintain wall shear rate above 500 s⁻¹ to avoid non-Newtonian rouleaux aggregation from corrupting the geometric diameter scaling.
  • Verify zero fluid evaporation during micro-gravimetric catch weighing using an oil-sealed evaporation trap.
  • Maintain constant temperature at 37.0 °C to stabilize baseline plasma viscosity.
