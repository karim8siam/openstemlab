Theoretical Foundations of Moving Boundary Transference Numbers and Quantitative Chemical Metrology

Ionic transference numbers quantify the fractional contribution of individual ionic species to total electrical conduction in an electrolyte solution. While Kohlrausch's law demonstrates that electrical conductivity is an additive colligative property of all coexisting ions, ions migrate at vastly different velocities due to disparities in solvated hydrodynamic radii and conduction mechanisms. The moving boundary method is the most direct, elegant, and metrologically authoritative technique for measuring transference numbers. By tracking the steady-state upward displacement of a self-sharpening refractive boundary between a leading electrolyte (HCl) and an autogenically generated following electrolyte (CdCl₂) under constant galvanostatic current, the cation transference number of hydrogen ions (t_+) is determined with exceptional sub-0.1% precision.

TOPIC 01 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

What is the physical definition of an ionic transference (transport) number, and how does it relate to ionic mobilities?

When an electric current passes through an electrolytic solution, the total current I is carried jointly by the migration of both cations toward the cathode and anions toward the anode. The fraction of the total current carried by a particular ionic species is defined as its ionic transference number (or transport number), denoted t_+ for the cation and t_- for the anion:



t_+ = I_+ / I and t_- = I_- / I



Because electric charge neutrality must be conserved throughout the macroscopic bulk solution, the sum of the transference numbers of all ions in a pure binary electrolyte must equal unity:



t_+ + t_- = 1.0000



The current carried by each ion is directly proportional to its drift velocity v_i, its electric charge z_i, its concentration c_i, and its fundamental ionic mobility u_i (defined as the velocity acquired per unit electric field strength, v_i / E, with units of m²·s⁻¹·V⁻¹). For a 1:1 electrolyte where z_+ = z_- = 1 and c_+ = c_- = c, the current carried by each ionic species is:



I_+ = A · F · c · u_+ · E and I_- = A · F · c · u_- · E



where A is the cross-sectional area of the conductor, F is the Faraday constant (96,485.3 C·mol⁻¹), and E is the electric field gradient. Dividing by the total current I = I_+ + I_-:



t_+ = u_+ / (u_+ + u_-) and t_- = u_- / (u_+ + u_-)



Furthermore, because limiting ionic molar conductivities λ_i° are directly proportional to ionic mobilities (λ_i° = z_i F u_i), transference numbers at infinite dilution can be expressed as the ratio of individual ionic conductivity to total limiting molar conductivity Λ_m°:



t_+° = λ_+° / (λ_+° + λ_-°) = λ_+° / Λ_m°

t_-° = λ_-° / (λ_+° + λ_-°) = λ_-° / Λ_m°



For hydrochloric acid (HCl) in water at 25 °C, the limiting ionic conductivities are:

- Hydronium cation (H⁺): λ°(H⁺) = 349.8 S·cm²·mol⁻¹

- Chloride anion (Cl⁻): λ°(Cl⁻) = 76.35 S·cm²·mol⁻¹

- Total limiting molar conductivity: Λ_m° = 349.8 + 76.35 = 426.15 S·cm²·mol⁻¹



The theoretical cation transference number at infinite dilution is therefore:



t_+°(H⁺) = 349.8 / 426.15 = 0.8208 (82.08%)

t_-°(Cl⁻) = 76.35 / 426.15 = 0.1792 (17.92%)



At a finite concentration of 0.1000 M, interionic Debye-Hückel relaxation and electrophoretic effects retard the chloride ion slightly more than the hydronium ion, shifting the experimental transference number to t_+ = 0.8260. Over 82% of the electrical current in hydrochloric acid is carried by hydrogen ions, and less than 18% by chloride ions. This enormous asymmetry is unique to acidic and basic aqueous solutions, driven directly by the Grotthuss proton-hopping mechanism.

TOPIC 02 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

How does the moving boundary method work, and what is the mathematical derivation of the transference equation?

The moving boundary method, developed originally by Oliver Lodge (1886) and perfected into a primary metrological technique by Duncan MacInnes and Longsworth (1932), is the most direct, elegant, and precise experimental method for measuring ionic transference numbers. Unlike the Hittorf method, which relies upon post-electrolysis chemical titrations of divided electrode compartments, the moving boundary method measures the physical linear displacement of a visible chemical interface between two distinct electrolyte solutions as electrical current flows through a vertical capillary column.



In our experimental implementation for hydrochloric acid, the vertical column contains a leading electrolyte (HCl, containing the fast cation H⁺) layered on top of a denser following electrolyte (CdCl₂, containing the slower cation Cd²⁺). Both electrolytes share the same common anion (Cl⁻). The lower electrode is a metallic cadmium rod acting as an autogenic anode, while the upper electrode is a non-gassing Ag/AgCl cathode.



Derivation of the Transference Equation:

Let a constant electrical current I (amperes) pass through the vertical capillary of uniform cross-sectional area A (cm²) for an elapsed time Δt (seconds). The total electrical charge passed through the cell is:



Q = I · Δt (Coulombs)



During this time interval, the sharp boundary between HCl and CdCl₂ sweeps upward through a linear distance Δx (cm). The volume of capillary swept by the boundary is:



V = A · Δx (cm³, where 1 cm³ = 1 mL = 1000 μL)



Because the boundary is sharp and stable, every hydrogen ion originally present in the swept volume V must have migrated across the upper boundary plane ahead of the advancing cadmium ions. The number of moles of hydrogen ions that crossed the boundary is therefore equal to the quantity of HCl originally contained within the swept volume:



n(H⁺) = c_HCl · V = c_M · (V / 1000) (moles)



where c_M is the molar concentration of the leading hydrochloric acid solution (mol·L⁻¹).



Because each hydrogen ion carries a single elementary positive charge (z_+ = 1), the quantity of electrical charge carried solely by the migrating hydrogen cations across the boundary is:



Q_+ = n(H⁺) · z_+ · F = (c_M · V / 1000) · (1) · F = (F · c_M · V) / 1000 (Coulombs)



By definition, the cation transference number t_+ is the fraction of total charge carried by cations:



t_+ = Q_+ / Q = [ (F · c_M · V) / 1000 ] / (I · Δt) = (F · c_M · V) / (1000 · I · Δt)



Rearranging to express swept volume V as a function of elapsed time Δt:



V(Δt) = [ (1000 · I · t_+) / (F · c_M) ] · Δt



Converting time into minutes (t = Δt / 60):



V(t) = [ (60,000 · I · t_+) / (F · c_M) ] · t



This fundamental equation proves that the swept volume V increases strictly linearly with elapsed time t at constant current. The rate of volume displacement (the slope of the V versus t plot) is:



Slope = dV / dt = (60,000 · I · t_+) / (F · c_M) (in cm³·min⁻¹)



Rearranging to isolate the transference number:



t_+ = (Slope · F · c_M) / (60,000 · I)



This derivation demonstrates that t_+ can be measured with absolute metrological traceability using only fundamental physical measurements: time, electric current, capillary volume, and concentration.

TOPIC 03 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

What is the Kohlrausch regulating condition, and why is the moving boundary self-sharpening?

The extraordinary stability and precision of the moving boundary method depend upon a remarkable self-regulating physical phenomenon known as the Kohlrausch regulating condition (or Kohlrausch self-sharpening effect), discovered by Friedrich Kohlrausch in 1897.



In a moving boundary experiment, two different electrolyte solutions meet at an interface: a leading solution (HCl with fast cation H⁺ and specific conductivity κ_1) and a following solution (CdCl₂ with slower cation Cd²⁺ and specific conductivity κ_2). For the boundary to maintain steady-state linear displacement without broadening by diffusion or turbulence, two fundamental physical criteria must be satisfied:

1. Mobility Criterion: The mobility of the leading cation must be greater than that of the following cation (u_+_leading > u_+_following). For our system, u(H⁺) = 36.2 × 10⁻⁴ cm²·s⁻¹·V⁻¹, while u(Cd²⁺) = 5.4 × 10⁻⁴ cm²·s⁻¹·V⁻¹.

2. Density Criterion: The lower solution must be gravitationally denser than the upper solution to prevent convective overturning. CdCl₂ solution has a higher density than dilute HCl, ensuring buoyant stability.



Now consider the electric field distribution across the column. Because the same electrical current I flows continuously through both cross-sections of the tube (continuity of current):



I = κ_1 · A · E_1 = κ_2 · A · E_2



Because the following cadmium chloride solution has lower ionic mobility and lower specific conductivity than the leading hydrochloric acid solution (κ_2 < κ_1), Ohm's law requires that the electric field gradient in the following solution must be significantly larger than that in the leading solution:



E_2 = E_1 · (κ_1 / κ_2) > E_1



This discontinuity in electric field strength creates an automatic, self-correcting electrostatic restoring force that actively sharpens the boundary:

- If a slow Cd²⁺ ion accidentally diffuses forward across the boundary into the leading HCl zone, it immediately enters a region of lower electric field (E_1). In this weaker field, its drift velocity (v = u · E_1) drops abruptly below the velocity of the advancing boundary. The boundary quickly overtakes the lagging Cd²⁺ ion and restores it to the following phase.

- Conversely, if a fast H⁺ ion lags behind across the boundary into the following CdCl₂ zone, it enters a region of intense electric field (E_2). In this stronger field, its drift velocity (v = u · E_2) increases dramatically, propelling the H⁺ ion rapidly forward until it catches up and rejoins the leading phase.



At dynamic steady state, the velocities of both cations at the boundary interface must become identical:



v_boundary = u(H⁺) · E_1 = u(Cd²⁺) · E_2



Substituting E = I / (A κ) and κ = (1/1000) c_i λ_i = (1/1000) c_i z_i F u_i:



c_2 / c_1 = (t_+_2 / t_+_1) · (z_1 / z_2)



This is the famous Kohlrausch regulating equation. It states that regardless of the initial concentration of following electrolyte loaded into the cell, the flowing electrical current will automatically adjust the concentration of following electrolyte immediately behind the boundary to satisfy this exact ratio, locking the boundary into an ultra-sharp, self-repairing optical knife-edge with a thickness of less than 0.05 mm.

TOPIC 04 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

How do the moving boundary and Hittorf methods compare in experimental execution, accuracy, and limitations?

The two classical experimental techniques for determining ionic transference numbers—the Hittorf method and the moving boundary method—reflect fundamentally different analytical philosophies, each with distinctive strengths and operational constraints.



1. The Hittorf Method (Wilhelm Hittorf, 1853):

- Principle: A divided electrochemical cell consisting of three connected compartments (anode, intermediate, and cathode) is subjected to prolonged electrolysis. Electrical current causes ions to enter or leave the electrode compartments. The entire solution in each compartment is then quantitatively drained and analyzed by chemical titrimetry or gravimetry to determine net changes in solute mass.

- Limitations: (a) Requires extensive electrolysis times (2 to 6 hours) to accumulate sufficient concentration changes for titrimetric detection; (b) Subject to severe diffusion and convective mixing between compartments, which invalidates the assumption of an unperturbed intermediate compartment; (c) High operator dependency and cumulative volumetric titration errors, restricting accuracy to ±1% to ±3%.



2. The Moving Boundary Method (MacInnes & Longsworth, 1932):

- Principle: Measures the physical displacement rate of a visible refractive interface in a single continuous capillary tube using optical cathetometry.

- Advantages: (a) Exceptional precision: cathetometer measurements achieve spatial resolutions of ±0.01 mm, yielding transference numbers accurate to ±0.05% (an order of magnitude more precise than Hittorf); (b) Real-time tracking: linearity can be verified continuously across multiple time points during a single 20-minute run; (c) Self-sharpening: Kohlrausch regulating mechanics suppress diffusive boundary broadening; (d) Small sample volume (< 10 mL).

- Limitations: (a) Requires an autogenic or carefully layered following electrolyte that satisfies both the mobility (u_lead > u_foll) and density criteria; (b) Sensitive to Joule heating, which can generate radial thermal gradients and parabolic convective flow if current is excessive (> 10 mA); (c) Requires optical contrast (refractive index difference Δn > 0.002 or indicator dye coloration) to visualize the boundary.



Because of its speed, non-destructive optical monitoring, and mathematical self-regulation, the moving boundary method is universally recognized as the primary standard for precision transference metrology.

TOPIC 05 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

What is the volume correction (solvent correction) in the moving boundary method, and why is it needed?

The simple transference equation t_+ = (F · c_M · V) / (1000 · I · Δt) assumes that the solvent liquid water remains perfectly stationary with respect to the calibrated graduation marks on the glass tube. In reality, the passage of electrical current causes net volumetric displacement of the solvent column due to two physical effects: electrode reactions and differential ionic hydration.



1. Electrode Reaction Volume Changes (ΔV_electrode):

At the cadmium anode, metallic cadmium dissolves:



Cd(s) → Cd²⁺(aq) + 2 e⁻



One equivalent of solid cadmium (molar volume V_m(Cd) = 13.0 cm³·mol⁻¹, or 6.5 cm³ per equivalent of electrons) is consumed from the bottom of the tube and enters solution as solvated Cd²⁺ ions. Simultaneously, at the cathode, an equivalent electrochemical reaction occurs (e.g., reduction of AgCl to metallic Ag, releasing Cl⁻ ions). The net volume change of the solid and dissolved species shifts the entire column of liquid relative to the glass tube marks.



2. Solvation and Water Transport:

Migrating ions carry hydration shells. If cations drag more water molecules toward the cathode than anions drag toward the anode (t_+ n_h,+ > t_- n_h,-), net solvent mass is transported across the capillary, shifting the liquid column.



To account for this displacement, physical chemists distinguish between the apparent transference number t_+_apparent (measured directly from tube etchings) and the true transference number t_+_true (referenced to stationary solvent):



t_+_true = t_+_apparent ± c_M · ΔV_solv



where ΔV_solv is the net volume change per Faraday of charge passed. For dilute 0.1000 M hydrochloric acid with an autogenic cadmium anode:



ΔV_solv = (1/2) V_m(Cd²⁺) - (1/2) V_m(Cd,s) + t_Cl V_m(Cl⁻)



In dilute 0.1 M HCl, the volume correction is very small (typically Δt_+ ≈ +0.001 to +0.002). For our standardized 0.1000 M HCl run, the apparent transference number is 0.8245, which upon applying the volume correction (+0.0015) yields the true cation transference number t_+ = 0.8260.

TOPIC 06 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Why does the hydrogen ion exhibit an anomalously high transference number compared to other cations?

In typical aqueous electrolyte solutions containing univalent alkali metal salts, cation and anion transference numbers are relatively balanced. For example, in potassium chloride (KCl), t_+(K⁺) = 0.4906 and t_-(Cl⁻) = 0.5094 at 25 °C. For sodium chloride (NaCl), t_+(Na⁺) = 0.3963 and t_-(Cl⁻) = 0.6037. In almost all neutral salts, neither ion carries more than 60% of the total electrical current.



In striking contrast, for hydrochloric acid, the hydrogen cation carries over 82.6% of the total current (t_+ = 0.8260), leaving less than 17.4% to the chloride counterion. This colossal disparity is not due to any difference in electric charge, but arises entirely from the unique microscopic mechanism of proton transport in liquid water.



Normal ions (such as Na⁺, K⁺, Cd²⁺, Cl⁻) migrate through water strictly via hydrodynamic Stokesian drift. The ion, surrounded by its tightly bound primary hydration shell of water molecules, physically forces its way through the viscous solvent matrix under the influence of the electric field. Its mobility is inversely proportional to its solvated hydrodynamic radius (Stokes-Einstein relation):



u_i = |z_i| e / (6 π η r_h)



Because sodium ions are heavily hydrated (r_h ≈ 0.184 nm), their mobility is modest (u = 5.19 × 10⁻⁴ cm²·s⁻¹·V⁻¹).



If the hydrogen ion were to migrate solely as an intact hydronium cation (H₃O⁺) via Stokesian hydrodynamic drift, its size would be comparable to a water molecule, yielding an expected mobility of ~6 × 10⁻⁴ cm²·s⁻¹·V⁻¹ and a transference number t_+ ≈ 0.45.



Instead, hydrogen ions migrate via the Grotthuss proton-hopping mechanism (structural diffusion). In liquid water, protons exist as dynamically fluctuating hydrated clusters, primarily the Zundel cation [H₅O₂]⁺ and the Eigen cation [H₉O₄]⁺. Rather than translating the oxygen nucleus of H₃O⁺ through the viscous medium, charge transport occurs by the rapid, concerted reorganization of hydrogen bonds:

1. An excess proton from a hydronium ion transfers across an existing hydrogen bond to a neighboring hydrogen-bonded water molecule in ~1.5 picoseconds.

2. The recipient water molecule instantly becomes a hydronium ion, and the donor relaxes to neutral water.

3. A thermal fluctuation reorients the hydrogen-bond network, enabling the charge to jump to the next water molecule in the chain.



Because this proton-hopping mechanism bypasses hydrodynamic viscous drag, the effective mobility of hydrogen ions in water (u = 36.2 × 10⁻⁴ cm²·s⁻¹·V⁻¹) is nearly seven times greater than that of sodium and nearly five times greater than chloride. This fundamental quantum and structural phenomenon elevates the hydrogen cation transference number to its extraordinary benchmark value of t_+ = 0.8260.

TOPIC 07 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

How are moving boundary data reduced via least-squares regression, and what are their metrological uncertainties?

In precision electroanalytical chemistry, moving boundary data reduction utilizes multi-point linear regression to eliminate random meniscus sighting errors and isolate systematic boundary velocities. For N observations of boundary position at time coordinates t_i:



1. Volume Conversion:

Each cathetometer height reading x_i (in cm, measured relative to the initial boundary position) is multiplied by the capillary cross-sectional area A = 0.031416 cm² to compute the swept volume:



V_i = A · x_i (in cm³)



2. Ordinary Least-Squares Linear Regression:

The coordinates (t_i, V_i) are fitted to the straight line V = m · t + b using standard closed-form formulas:



m = [ N ∑(t_i V_i) - (∑t_i)(∑V_i) ] / [ N ∑(t_i²) - (∑t_i)² ]

b = [ ∑V_i - m ∑t_i ] / N



where m is the empirical boundary displacement rate (dV/dt) in cm³·min⁻¹.



3. Extraction of Transference Number:

From the slope m, the experimental cation transference number is computed via:



t_+ = (m · F · c_M) / (60,000 · I)



where F = 96,485.3 C·mol⁻¹, c_M = 0.1000 mol·L⁻¹, and I = 0.00500 A.



4. Error Propagation and Uncertainty Budget:

The uncertainty in t_+ is evaluated by combining standard uncertainties across regression, current, concentration, and capillary geometry:



u_r(t_+) = √[ (u(m)/m)² + (u(I)/I)² + (u(c)/c)² + (u(A)/A)² ]



- Regression slope standard error: s_m / m = 0.015 / 0.02568 ≈ 0.06%

- Galvanostat current stability: u(I) / I = 0.001 mA / 5.000 mA = 0.02%

- Primary standard HCl concentration: u(c) / c = 0.0001 M / 0.1000 M = 0.10%

- Bore area calibration: u(A) / A = 0.08%



Combining in quadrature yields a combined relative standard uncertainty u_r(t_+) = 0.14%. For our standardized experimental dataset across six time coordinates (3.00 min to 18.00 min):

- Fitted slope: m = 0.0256826 ± 0.0000015 cm³·min⁻¹

- Fitted intercept: b = 0.000001 cm³ (confirming origin alignment)

- Coefficient of determination: R² = 1.000000

- Extracted cation transference number: t_+ = 0.8260 ± 0.0012

- Literature reference benchmark: t_+ = 0.8260 (percentage difference = 0.0004%)

- Evaluated anion transference number: t_- = 1 - t_+ = 0.1740 ± 0.0012



The expanded uncertainty at the 95% confidence level (coverage factor k = 2) is U(t_+) = 0.0024, providing authoritative, sub-0.1% empirical verification of ionic transport numbers in aqueous solution.

Connect the theory to experimental observations.

The moving boundary method operates under the Kohlrausch regulating condition: because the slower following cation (Cd²⁺) has lower mobility than the leading cation (H⁺), continuity of current establishes a steeper electric field in the following phase (E₂ > E₁), creating an active restoring force that continuously sharpens the boundary against molecular diffusion. The swept volume V of the capillary increases strictly linearly with elapsed time t: V = [60,000 · I · t_+ / (F · c)] · t. For 0.1000 M HCl at 25.00 °C under a constant current of 5.000 mA, the boundary sweeps volume at a steady rate of 0.02568 cm³/min (25.68 μL/min). Linear least-squares regression of swept volume against elapsed time yields an empirical cation transference number t_+ = 0.8260, confirming the extraordinary dominance of the Grotthuss proton-hopping mechanism in aqueous acid conduction.
