01Vectors, Scalars, and Concurrent Coplanar Force Systems

In Newtonian mechanics, physical quantities are divided into scalars, which possess magnitude only (such as mass, temperature, and time), and vectors, which possess both magnitude and a spatial direction, transforming according to the laws of coordinate rotation. Force is a vector quantity, formally defined as the time rate of change of linear momentum: F = dp/dt. When multiple forces act on a rigid body such that their lines of action all intersect at a single geometric point, they constitute a concurrent force system. When all lines of action additionally lie within a single geometric plane, the system is described as concurrent and coplanar. For such systems, the rotational moment arm about the common point of concurrence is zero for every acting force, meaning no external torques can be generated about that point (Σ τ_O = 0). Consequently, rotational equilibrium is satisfied automatically, and the necessary and sufficient condition for complete mechanical equilibrium reduces entirely to translational equilibrium: the vector sum of all concurrent forces must equal the null vector, Σ F = 0.

\mathbf{F} = F_x \hat{\mathbf{i}} + F_y \hat{\mathbf{j}}, \quad |\mathbf{F}| = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2}, \quad \theta = \operatorname{atan2}(F_y, F_x)
Why does a concurrent force system eliminate the possibility of rotational torque about the point of concurrence?

Torque is defined as the vector cross product τ = r × F. When all forces intersect at the point of concurrence O, the position vector r from O to the line of action of each force has length zero (or is collinear with the force), producing identically zero torque (τ = 0) about that point.

02The Parallelogram Law and Triangle Law of Vector Addition

The geometric foundation of vector addition is the Parallelogram Law of Forces, originally articulated by Simon Stevin in 1586 and formalized by Isaac Newton in the Principia. The law states that if two concurrent forces F_1 and F_2 acting on a particle are represented in magnitude and direction by two adjacent sides of a parallelogram drawn from a common vertex, their vector resultant R is represented in magnitude and direction by the diagonal of the parallelogram passing through that vertex. Equivalently, the Triangle Law of Vectors states that if two vectors are placed head-to-tail, the vector drawn from the tail of the first to the head of the second represents the resultant vector: R = F_1 + F_2. By applying the Law of Cosines to the vector triangle, the magnitude of the resultant for two forces separated by an included angle φ is given by R = √[F_1² + F_2² + 2 F_1 F_2 cos(φ)]. When the two forces have equal magnitude F_1 = F_2 = F and are placed at symmetric angles ±θ (such that φ = 2θ), this expression simplifies via the half-angle trigonometric identity 1 + cos(2θ) = 2 cos²(θ) to R = √[2 F² (1 + cos 2θ)] = √[4 F² cos²(θ)] = 2 F cos(θ).

R = \sqrt{F_1^2 + F_2^2 + 2 F_1 F_2 \cos\phi}, \quad \text{For } F_1 = F_2 = F, \; \phi = 2\theta: \quad R = 2 F \cos\theta
What is the physical interpretation of the included angle term 2 F_1 F_2 cos(φ) in the Law of Cosines for vectors?

The term represents the geometric interference (overlap) between the two force vectors. When φ = 0° (parallel forces), cos(φ) = 1 and R = F_1 + F_2 (maximum scalar sum). When φ = 180° (anti-parallel forces), cos(φ) = -1 and R = |F_1 - F_2| (minimum scalar difference). When φ = 90° (orthogonal forces), the term vanishes, reducing to the Pythagorean theorem R = √(F_1² + F_2²).

03Orthogonal Component Resolution and the Analytical Method

While graphical vector addition provides intuitive geometric visualization, analytical resolution into mutually perpendicular Cartesian components provides superior mathematical precision. In a two-dimensional Cartesian plane defined by unit basis vectors i and j, any force vector F_i oriented at angle θ_i to the positive x-axis can be resolved into orthogonal scalar components: F_{ix} = F_i cos(θ_i) and F_{iy} = F_i sin(θ_i). The components of the resultant vector R are obtained by scalar algebraic summation over all n acting forces: R_x = Σ F_{ix} = Σ F_i cos(θ_i) and R_y = Σ F_{iy} = Σ F_i sin(θ_i). The magnitude and direction of the resultant are then given by R = √[R_x² + R_y²] and θ_R = atan2(R_y, R_x). In our experimental configuration with two symmetric forces F_1 = M_0 g at +θ and F_2 = M_0 g at -θ, the sum along the y-axis is R_y = M_0 g sin(θ) + M_0 g sin(-θ) = M_0 g [sin(θ) - sin(θ)] = 0. The sum along the x-axis is R_x = M_0 g cos(θ) + M_0 g cos(-θ) = 2 M_0 g cos(θ). Thus, the resultant is purely horizontal, proving that orthogonal decomposition greatly simplifies multi-force equilibrium problems.

R_x = \sum_{i=1}^n F_i \cos\theta_i, \quad R_y = \sum_{i=1}^n F_i \sin\theta_i, \quad R = \sqrt{R_x^2 + R_y^2}, \quad \theta_R = \operatorname{atan2}(R_y, R_x)
Why is the analytical component method inherently more accurate than graphical polygon drafting in experimental mechanics?

Graphical methods depend on the visual precision of drafting instruments (rulers, protractors, pencil lead thickness) and are limited to fractional-millimeter accuracy (~1-2% error). In contrast, analytical component summation operates on exact trigonometric functions evaluated to double precision, limited only by the metrological resolution of the experimental instruments.

04The Equilibrant Vector and the Closed Force Polygon

When a system of forces acts on a body, their vector sum produces a net unbalanced resultant force R that would accelerate the body according to Newton's Second Law: R = m_total · a. To maintain static equilibrium (a = 0), an additional force must be introduced that completely neutralizes R. This balancing force is defined as the equilibrant, denoted by the vector E. By definition, the condition for equilibrium requires R + E = 0, which immediately implies that E = -R. The equilibrant vector possesses exactly the same magnitude as the resultant (|E| = |R|), acts along the same line of action, but points in the diametrically opposite direction: θ_E = θ_R + 180° (modulo 360°). Geometrically, when a system of concurrent forces is in static equilibrium, their vector arrows placed head-to-tail form a closed force polygon. For three concurrent forces (F_1, F_2, and E), the closed polygon is a triangle of forces. If the polygon fails to close, the gap between the starting tail and the finishing head represents the non-zero resultant force acting on the system.

\mathbf{E} = -\mathbf{R} = -(\mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2), \quad \sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2 + \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}, \quad \theta_E = \theta_R + 180^\circ
What happens to the closed force triangle if one of the three forces is suddenly removed?

If one force is removed, the remaining two forces no longer form a closed triangle. The vector sum of the remaining two forces equals the resultant R, which is exactly equal and opposite to the removed force, causing the system to accelerate along the direction of R.

05Symmetric Angle Configuration and Linear Least-Squares Transformation

The symmetric experimental design establishes a rigorous linear relationship that can be analyzed using ordinary least-squares (OLS) regression. The theoretical equilibrium condition states that the equilibrant mass m_E balances the vector resultant of the two symmetric load masses M_0: m_E · g = 2 M_0 · g · cos(θ). Dividing both sides by g eliminates the local gravitational acceleration completely: m_E = 2 M_0 · cos(θ). Defining the independent variable as the trigonometric projection x = cos(θ) (dimensionless) and the dependent variable as the equilibrant mass y = m_E (in kg), the governing equation matches the standard linear model: y = S · x + b. Here, the theoretical slope is S = 2 M_0 (in kg), and the theoretical intercept is b = 0. By gathering observations across multiple half-angles θ and performing OLS regression, the unknown component mass is determined as M_0 = S / 2. This linear transformation provides immense experimental advantages: it evaluates the validity of the cosine projection across a wide domain, tests for zero-offset intercept errors caused by pulley friction or cord weight, and calculates the standard error of the fitted slope.

m_E = (2 M_0) \cos\theta + b, \quad y = m_E, \quad x = \cos\theta, \quad S = 2 M_0, \quad M_0 = \frac{S}{2}
Why is the special symmetric setting of θ = 60° (total angle 2θ = 120°) considered a classic benchmark in physics?

At θ = 60°, cos(60°) = 0.500, which yields m_E = 2 M_0 (0.500) = M_0. In this configuration, all three forces have identical magnitudes (F_1 = F_2 = E = M_0 g) and are separated by mutually equal angles of 120°, forming an equilateral triangle of vectors in perfect three-fold rotational symmetry.

06Metrological Uncertainties and Pulley Friction Modeling

Measurements on a physical force table are subject to several well-defined experimental uncertainties. The primary sources of error include: (1) angular positioning resolution of the pulleys on the 360° graduated rim (typical least count ±0.25°); (2) mass tolerances of the slotted weights (typically ±0.1 g); and (3) static bearing friction within the pulley axles. According to the Capstan (belt friction) equation, tension across a pulley with friction satisfies T_tight = T_slack · e^(μ β), where μ is the coefficient of axle friction and β is the wrap angle. In a real pulley, this friction creates a narrow 'dead-band' of mass Δm around the true equilibrium value within which the ring remains stationary due to static friction resistance. Lightly tapping the table rim introduces high-frequency mechanical vibration that temporarily breaks asperity contact, allowing the ring to settle into its true frictionless equilibrium position. According to GUM guidelines, the combined relative uncertainty in M_0 satisfies (u_M / M_0)² = (u_S / S)², where u_S is the standard error of the regression slope.

T_{\text{tight}} = T_{\text{slack}} e^{\mu \beta}, \quad \Delta m_{\text{deadband}} \approx 2 \mu_{\text{axle}} \left(\frac{r_{\text{pin}}}{R_{\text{pulley}}}\right) m, \quad \frac{u_{M_0}}{M_0} = \frac{u_S}{S}
How does tapping the force table rim reduce measurement error caused by pulley friction?

Tapping the table transmits micro-vibrations to the pulley bearings. This momentarily reduces the static friction coefficient μ_s to the much smaller kinetic or agitated friction coefficient, releasing any trapped tension differentials and permitting the cords to equalize so the ring floats centered.

07Engineering Applications: Truss Analysis and Concurrent Equilibrium

The principle of concurrent coplanar force equilibrium is the foundational concept behind structural engineering analysis of pin-jointed trusses used in bridges, roof supports, and aerospace frames. In the classical Method of Joints, every individual joint (node) where structural members intersect is isolated as a free-body diagram. Because the members are designed as two-force members carrying only axial tension or compression, all forces acting on a joint intersect at a single concurrent point (the pin). Static equilibrium at each joint requires Σ F_x = 0 and Σ F_y = 0. By systematically solving these simultaneous linear equations joint by joint, structural engineers determine the exact internal axial loads throughout multi-span trusses such as the Warren, Pratt, and Howe bridges. Similarly, concurrent equilibrium governs the rigging and bridle tension calculations in cranes, guyed transmission towers, sailing ship rigging, and orthopedic traction systems in modern medicine.

\text{Method of Joints}: \quad \sum F_x = 0, \quad \sum F_y = 0 \quad (\text{at every truss pin node})
How does the force table experiment directly simulate the behavior of an idealized truss joint?

The central ring acts as the pin joint, the radial strings represent the structural members carrying purely axial tensile forces, and the suspended weights represent the internal member loads. The condition that the ring floats centered corresponds exactly to the requirement that the pin joint is in static equilibrium (Σ F = 0).
