Probabilistic foundations of Central Limit Theorem.

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) is arguably the most celebrated theorem in mathematical statistics, explaining why the normal (Gaussian) distribution appears ubiquitously across natural, physical, and social phenomena. The Lindeberg-Lévy CLT guarantees that the standardized sum of independent, identically distributed random variables with finite variance converges to a standard normal distribution, regardless of the parent distribution's shape. In this laboratory, we simulate sample averages from skewed and uniform non-Gaussian parents, evaluate the Berry-Esseen convergence bounds, and quantitatively confirm the universal N^(-1/2) standard error scaling law. In mathematical statistics, empirical data science, and modern computational engineering, understanding the asymptotic sampling properties and theoretical behavior of Central Limit Theorem and Lindeberg-Lévy Asymptotics provides crucial guarantees. Rigorous experimental validation connects foundational probability laws with observable empirical phenomena, ensuring that statistical inference remains valid under finite sample sizes and real-world data collection constraints.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the Lindeberg-Lévy Central Limit Theorem, and what are its assumptions?

The Lindeberg-Lévy CLT states that if X_1, X_2, ..., X_n are independent and identically distributed random variables with finite mean μ and finite non-zero variance σ^2 (0 < σ^2 < ∞), then the standardized sample mean Z_n = (X̄_n - μ) / (σ / √n) converges in distribution to a standard normal distribution N(0, 1) as n → ∞: lim_{n→∞} P(Z_n ≤ z) = Φ(z) = (1/√2π) ∫_{-∞}^z e^(-u^2/2) du. The theorem requires only finite second moments, making no assumptions about the symmetry, continuity, or shape of the parent distribution.

How is the Central Limit Theorem proved using characteristic functions?

Let Y_i = (X_i - μ)/σ have mean 0, variance 1, and characteristic function φ_Y(t) = E[e^(itY)]. By Taylor's theorem, φ_Y(t) = 1 - t^2/2 + o(t^2) as t → 0. The standardized sum is Z_n = (1/√n) ∑ Y_i. By independence, its characteristic function is φ_{Z_n}(t) = [φ_Y(t/√n)]^n = [1 - t^2/(2n) + o(t^2/n)]^n. Taking the limit as n → ∞: lim_{n→∞} φ_{Z_n}(t) = e^(-t^2/2). Since e^(-t^2/2) is the characteristic function of the standard normal distribution, Lévy's continuity theorem establishes that Z_n converges in distribution to N(0, 1).

What does the Berry-Esseen Theorem establish regarding the rate of convergence in the CLT?

The Berry-Esseen theorem (Andrew Berry 1941, Carl-Gustav Esseen 1942) quantifies the maximum uniform error between the standardized sample distribution F_n(z) and the Gaussian CDF Φ(z): sup_{z ∈ ℝ} |F_n(z) - Φ(z)| ≤ C · ρ / (σ^3 √n), where ρ = E[|X - μ|^3] is the third absolute central moment and C is a universal constant. Irina Shevtsova (2011) proved that C ≤ 0.4748. This proves that the convergence rate of the CLT is strictly O(1/√n), and that distributions with high skewness (large third moment ρ) require substantially larger sample sizes n to achieve normal approximation.

What is the Lindeberg Condition for independent but non-identically distributed random variables?

For independent random variables X_i with means μ_i, variances σ_i^2, and total variance s_n^2 = ∑_{i=1}^n σ_i^2, the Lindeberg condition requires that for every ε > 0: lim_{n→∞} (1 / s_n^2) ∑_{i=1}^n E[(X_i - μ_i)^2 · I(|X_i - μ_i| ≥ ε s_n)] = 0. This condition ensures that no single random variable dominates the total variance s_n^2. Jarl Waldemar Lindeberg (1922) proved that this condition is sufficient for the CLT; William Feller (1935) proved it is also necessary under mild conditions (Lindeberg-Feller CLT).

Why is the uniform parent distribution U[0, 1] an ideal demonstration of rapid CLT convergence?

For a continuous uniform parent U[0, 1], the distribution is perfectly symmetric about its mean μ = 0.5, meaning its third central moment is exactly zero: μ_3 = E[(X - μ)^3] = 0. Because the skewness term in the Edgeworth expansion vanishes, the leading O(1/√n) error term in the Berry-Esseen bound drops out, leaving the remaining error to scale as O(1/n). Consequently, the sum of just n = 12 uniform variates (the classical Irwin-Hall distribution) produces a distribution that is virtually indistinguishable from a standard normal distribution.

What happens when the parent distribution has an asymmetric, highly skewed shape (e.g. Exponential)?

When the parent distribution is skewed (e.g., Exponential(λ) with skewness γ_1 = 2), the third central moment μ_3 = 2/λ^3 is large and positive. The standardized distribution F_n(z) inherits this positive skewness for moderate n, resulting in an elongated right tail and a steeper left slope than the Gaussian bell curve. By the Edgeworth expansion, F_n(z) ≈ Φ(z) - (γ_1 / (6√n)) (z^2 - 1) ϕ(z). Achieving a 1% approximation accuracy for an exponential distribution requires n ≥ 30 to 50 samples, compared to n ≥ 5 for a symmetric uniform parent.

How does the Central Limit Theorem justify the widespread assumption of normality in measurement errors?

In experimental physics and engineering metrology, observed measurement errors typically arise from the superposition of numerous independent micro-scale physical disturbances (e.g., thermal fluctuations, acoustic vibrations, optical distortions, electrical noise). Under the Central Limit Theorem, the collective sum of these diverse independent error components asymptotically converges to a Gaussian distribution, providing the theoretical justification for Carl Friedrich Gauss's normal error model and least-squares analysis.

Experimental precautions and statistical checks

  • Standardize sample sums using the true population mean μ and standard deviation σ rather than sample estimates.
  • Use at least 5,000 Monte Carlo repetitions at each sample size to achieve stable empirical standard error estimates.
  • Inspect both the central peak and the extreme tails (|z| > 2.5) to assess whether normality has truly been achieved.
  • Perform replication across multiple independent pseudorandom seeds to verify that estimator variance and bias bounds are invariant to initial generator states.

Theoretical assumptions & limitations

  • The classical CLT applies only to sums of variables with finite second moments; it fails for heavy-tailed distributions like Cauchy or Pareto (α ≤ 2).
  • Strongly dependent or long-memory time series processes can exhibit non-Gaussian limit distributions or fractional Brownian motion limits.
