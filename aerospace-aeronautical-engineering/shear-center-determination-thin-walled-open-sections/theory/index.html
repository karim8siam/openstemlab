Aerodynamic and physical foundations of Shear Center Determination.

In aeronautical structural engineering, preventing unwanted torsional twisting under aerodynamic lift loads is paramount for structural integrity and aeroelastic stability. For thin-walled open cross-sections (such as C-channel spars, slit tubes, and J-stringers), the center of gravity (centroid) does not coincide with the point of resultant internal shear resistance. If a transverse shear load is applied through the centroid, the internal shear flows generated in the flanges and web exert a net torque, causing the beam to undergo violent torsional twist in addition to bending. The Shear Center (or Center of Flexure) is defined as the unique cross-sectional point through which a transverse shear load must pass to produce pure bending without any torsional twisting. According to thin-walled beam shear flow theory, internal shear flow q(s) = - (V_y / I_xx) * int (y * t) ds circulating through the flanges generates an internal moment about the web: M_internal = V_y * e_s, where the shear center offset is e_s = (b^2 * h^2 * t) / (4 * I_xx). Applying an external load at an offset e from the web produces net torque T = P * (e - e_s) and torsional twist angle theta = [P * L / (G * J)] * (e - e_s). In this experiment, a cantilevered C-channel is loaded at variable horizontal offsets from e = -25 to +25 mm. Plotting twist angle theta against load offset e reveals the exact zero-twist crossing point, locating the shear center offset e_s = -18.50 mm.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the Shear Center of a structural cross section?

The Shear Center (or center of flexure) is the point in the cross section through which the resultant shear force must act so that the beam undergoes pure transverse bending deflection without any torsional twisting. If the load is applied at any other point, the beam experiences coupled bending and torsion.

Why does the shear center of an open C-channel lie outside the physical cross section?

In a C-channel, vertical shear V_y is resisted entirely by the vertical web, while horizontal shear flows circulate in opposing directions across the top and bottom flanges. These horizontal flange forces form an internal couple (torque) of magnitude V_y * e_s. To balance this internal couple, the external load must act outside the web at distance e_s = (b^2 * h^2 * t) / (4 * I_xx) on the side opposite the flanges.

Where is the shear center located in cross sections with one or two axes of symmetry?

If a cross section has an axis of symmetry, the shear center must lie somewhere along that symmetry axis. If a cross section has two axes of symmetry (such as an I-beam, circular tube, or rectangular box), the shear center coincides precisely with the geometric centroid.

Why are thin-walled open sections much weaker in torsion than thin-walled closed tubes?

Open thin-walled sections have extremely low torsional stiffness J (Saint-Venant torsion constant J = (1/3) * sum(s_i * t_i^3)), because shear stresses must reverse direction across the microscopic wall thickness. Closed tubes support continuous circulating shear flow (Bredt-Batho theory), achieving torsional stiffness hundreds of times higher for the same structural weight.

What structural problems occur if aerodynamic lift on an aircraft wing does not act through the wing's shear center axis?

If aerodynamic lift acts ahead of the shear center (elastic axis), the resulting nose-up pitching moment twists the wing, increasing its effective angle of attack. This generates even more lift, causing a catastrophic runaway structural failure known as aeroelastic torsional divergence.

What is cross-sectional warping in thin-walled beams under torsion?

Under torsion, cross sections of non-circular beams do not remain planar; points displace axially along the z-axis (warping). If warping is restrained at a fixed root clamp, longitudinal normal stresses (warping stresses) and secondary shear stresses develop, significantly stiffening the root against twist.

Experimental precautions and wind tunnel safety

  • Ensure the transverse loading arm is sufficiently rigid to prevent localized flexure that could corrupt twist angle measurements.
  • Apply load strictly downwards to avoid introducing lateral components into the thin-walled section.
  • Verify that the cantilever root fixture clamps both web and flanges completely to enforce classical Saint-Venant warping restraint.
  • Do not exceed the elastic limit of the C-channel flanges during extreme positive offset testing.

Theoretical assumptions & flow limits

  • Thin-walled open section theory assumes wall thickness t is small compared to flange width and web height (t/b < 0.1).
  • Restrained warping at the cantilever root fixture slightly stiffens the beam against torsion near the root.
  • Assumes constant cross-sectional geometry along the beam span.
  • Localized shear deformation near the loading cross-arm introduces minor local distortion.
