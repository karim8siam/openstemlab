Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Plate Heat Exchanger.

In food processing, pharmaceuticals, biochemical manufacturing, and specialty chemical synthesis, gasketed plate-and-frame heat exchangers (PHEs) have largely superseded traditional shell-and-tube units for low-to-medium pressure duties. A plate heat exchanger consists of a pack of corrugated thin stainless steel or titanium plates compressed between a fixed head and movable follower frame. Elastomeric gaskets seal the plate perimeters and direct hot and cold fluids into alternate narrow inter-plate channels in true counter-current flow. The defining feature of PHEs is the chevron (herringbone) surface corrugation pressed into each plate. Adjacent plates are inverted so that corrugation ridges cross, forming a complex three-dimensional network of intersecting tortuous channels. These corrugations induce continuous swirling vortex shedding and boundary layer disruption at Reynolds numbers as low as 50 to 100, transitioning into intense turbulence well below the Re = 2,300 threshold of smooth tubes. Consequently, plate heat exchangers achieve exceptionally high overall heat transfer coefficients U (often 3 to 5 times greater than tubular exchangers), allowing extremely close temperature approaches (less than 1 °C) and compact footprints. In this experiment, an instrumented 10-plate chevron heat exchanger is operated across channel velocities from 0.10 to 0.60 m/s to quantify overall conductance U and verify empirical power-law velocity scaling U = S · v_p^0.65.

1. Architecture of Gasketed Plate Heat Exchangers

Plate-and-frame heat exchangers (PHEs) provide compact, high-efficiency thermal processing across food, bioprocess, and chemical industries. Thin, corrugated metal plates ($0.5$ to $0.8\text{ mm}$ thickness) are clamped together in a frame, with elastomeric perimeter gaskets sealing alternate channels to establish isolated hot and cold counter-current flow paths.

2. Chevron Corrugation Hydrodynamics & Heat Transfer Enhancement

The characteristic herringbone (chevron) pattern pressed into each plate induces three-dimensional swirling vortices that continuously renew the thermal boundary layer. In conventional smooth conduits, laminar-to-turbulent transition occurs at $Re \approx 2,300$. In chevron plates, transition occurs at $Re \approx 50$ to $100$. The Nusselt number in corrugated plate channels is modeled by the Martin / Heavner empirical power-law correlation:

\[ Nu = \frac{h D_h}{k} = C \cdot Re^m Pr^n \]

where $D_h = 2 b$ is the hydraulic diameter ($b$ is mean channel corrugation depth, typically $2$ to $4\text{ mm}$) and exponent $m$ typically ranges from $0.65$ to $0.70$ for $60^\circ$ chevron angles.

3. Overall Conductance Model

Because the plates are extremely thin ($0.6\text{ mm}$) and manufactured from high-grade 316 stainless steel ($k = 16\text{ W/(m}\cdot\text{K)}$), wall conductive resistance is minimal ($R_{wall} = t / k \approx 3.75 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^2\cdot\text{K/W}$). When operating in turbulent liquid-liquid service, convective film coefficients $h_h$ and $h_c$ govern performance. Relating overall heat transfer coefficient $U$ to process channel velocity $v_p$:

\[ U = S \cdot (v_p)^{0.65} \]

where $S$ is the plate exchanger conductance coefficient in $\text{W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)/(m/s)}^{0.65}$.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How do chevron corrugations promote turbulence at exceptionally low Reynolds numbers?

The crossing corrugations force fluid to split, swirl, and recombine continuously in three-dimensional helical vortices, destroying laminar boundary layers and inducing turbulent mixing at Reynolds numbers as low as Re = 50 to 100.

What is the trade-off between thermal enhancement and hydraulic pressure drop in PHEs?

The intense swirling vortex generation that elevates heat transfer coefficients by 300% to 500% also creates high form drag and friction, producing substantially higher pressure drops per unit length than smooth tubes.

What is the significance of the chevron angle β (typically 30° vs 60°)?

A 30° chevron (low-theta) plate produces lower pressure drop and moderate heat transfer; a 60° chevron (high-theta) plate generates intense turbulence and high heat transfer coefficients at the expense of higher pumping head.

Why can plate heat exchangers achieve very close temperature approaches (< 1 °C)?

The high overall heat transfer coefficient U, true counter-current flow without crossflow bypass, and high thermal length (NTU) allow cold fluid to approach hot fluid inlet temperature within fractions of a degree.

How are plate heat exchangers cleaned in food and pharmaceutical manufacturing?

They are cleaned in place (CIP) by circulating caustic detergent and acid sanitizing solutions at high velocity, or dismantled rapidly by loosening compression bolts for manual inspection and sanitization.

What are the primary operating limits of gasketed plate heat exchangers?

Elastomeric gaskets (nitrile, EPDM, Viton) limit operating temperatures to typically < 160 °C and pressures to < 25 bar; exceeding these limits requires welded or brazed plate designs.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Tighten compression bolts evenly in cross-pattern to the exact specified dimension to avoid crushing plate corrugations or blowing gaskets.
  • Verify zero air binding by venting both hot and cold channel top bleed ports before acquiring data.
  • Maintain cold utility flow rate strictly constant at 10.0 L/min across all process velocity test points.
  • Never exceed the maximum pressure rating of 10.0 bar for the gasketed plate pack.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (0.1 to 0.6 m/s).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
