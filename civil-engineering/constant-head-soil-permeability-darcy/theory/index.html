Civil engineering mechanics & structural foundations of Constant Head Permeability.

Hydraulic conductivity (permeability, k) is the primary geotechnical transport property governing groundwater seepage beneath earth dams, dewatering rates for deep foundation excavations, drainage through highway base courses, and consolidation settlement kinetics in saturated soil deposits. Formulated empirically by Henry Darcy in 1856 through public water fountain filtration experiments in Dijon, France, Darcy's law states that the apparent discharge velocity v of fluid through a porous medium is directly proportional to the hydraulic gradient i: v = k · i, where i = Δh / L (head loss per unit seepage length). The volumetric discharge rate Q through soil of cross-sectional area A is Q = k · i · A = k · (Δh / L) · A. Under ASTM D2434, the constant-head permeameter test is specifically designed for coarse-grained permeable soils (hydraulic conductivity k > 10^-4 cm/s, such as sands and gravels). A cylindrical soil specimen is held in a transparent permeameter cell connected to an adjustable constant-head reservoir with an overflow weir that maintains an invariant hydraulic head difference Δh. Manometer piezometer ports tapped along the specimen length monitor internal head loss across a known spacing L. In this laboratory, discharge flow rate Q is measured across head differentials from 10 to 60 cm to verify Darcy's linear seepage law and determine hydraulic conductivity k.

Q = 1.4500 · Δh

1. Darcy's Law & Navier–Stokes Porous Media Homogenization

Fluid seepage through granular soil represents low-Reynolds-number viscous flow through a tortuous three-dimensional network of interconnected pore channels. Homogenization of the creeping Navier–Stokes equations (Stokes flow) over a representative elementary volume (REV) leads directly to the macroscopic empirical transport relation discovered by Henry Darcy in 1856:

\[ v = - k \nabla h \]

where $v = Q / A$ is the superficial discharge (Darcian) velocity (volume flux per unit cross-sectional area), $k$ is the hydraulic conductivity (coefficient of permeability), and $\nabla h$ is the piezometric hydraulic head gradient. For one-dimensional steady seepage along coordinate $x$ through length $L$ under head difference $\Delta h$:

\[ i = \frac{\Delta h}{L}, \qquad v = k \cdot i = k \left( \frac{\Delta h}{L} \right) \]

2. Absolute (Intrinsic) Permeability Formulation

Hydraulic conductivity $k$ depends upon both the geometric matrix structure of the soil and the physical properties of the permeant fluid. It is expressed in terms of intrinsic permeability $K$ (in $\text{m}^2$ or Darcys):

\[ k = \frac{K \rho g}{\mu} \]

where $\rho$ is fluid density, $g$ is gravitational acceleration, and $\mu$ is dynamic viscosity. Because water viscosity $\mu(T)$ decreases rapidly with temperature ($-2.4\%\text{ per }^\circ\text{C}$), standard geotechnical practice normalizes experimental conductivity $k_T$ to $20.0^\circ\text{C}$:

\[ k_{20} = k_T \left( \frac{\mu_T}{\mu_{20}} \right) \]

3. Constant-Head Discharge Model

The volumetric discharge flow rate $Q$ through a permeameter of cross-sectional area $A$ and manometer tap spacing $L$ is:

\[ Q = k \cdot i \cdot A = k \left( \frac{\Delta h}{L} \right) A = \left[ \frac{k A}{L} \right] \Delta h = S_k \cdot \Delta h \]

For permeameter dimensions $D = 7.50\text{ cm}$ ($A = 44.1786\text{ cm}^2$) and tap spacing $L = 15.0\text{ cm}$:

\[ S_k = \frac{k A}{L} = \frac{0.49231 \times 44.1786}{15.0} = 1.4500\text{ cm}^2\text{/s} \]

Plotting experimental discharge $Q$ versus head difference $\Delta h$ yields a linear response whose slope directly provides hydraulic conductivity $k$.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

State Darcy's Law and identify the conditions under which it remains strictly valid.

Darcy's Law states Q = k · i · A; it is strictly valid for laminar fluid flow through saturated porous media where Reynolds number based on mean grain diameter is Re_p = (rho v D_50) / mu < 1 to 10; at higher velocities, inertial turbulence causes non-linear deviations.

Why is the constant-head test preferred for coarse sands while the falling-head test is used for clays?

Coarse sands have high permeability, discharging measurable volumes of water rapidly under constant head; fine-grained clays have extremely low permeability (k < 10^-6 cm/s), meaning flow under constant head is too slow to measure, requiring a falling-head standpipe.

Why is de-aired water strictly required in laboratory permeability testing?

Tap water contains dissolved air; under negative pressure gradients within soil voids, dissolved air comes out of solution as microscopic bubbles that lodge in pore throats, drastically reducing apparent permeability by up to 90%.

How is measured hydraulic conductivity k_T standardized to the reference 20 °C baseline?

Water viscosity decreases with temperature, increasing permeability; standardized conductivity is k_20 = k_T · (mu_T / mu_20), where mu is dynamic viscosity of water.

What is Hazen's empirical formula relating hydraulic conductivity to grain size in sands?

Allen Hazen established that k ≈ C · (D_10)^2 in cm/s, where D_10 is the effective grain size (in cm) passing 10% on the gradation curve, and C is Hazen's empirical coefficient (typically 100 to 150).

What is the physical distinction between discharge velocity v and seepage velocity v_s?

Discharge velocity v = Q / A assumes flow occurs through the entire cross-section; seepage velocity v_s = v / n represents actual interstitial velocity through interconnected pore channels, where n is soil porosity (n < 1, so v_s > v).

Experimental precautions and laboratory safety

  • Always saturate soil specimens from bottom to top under low hydraulic head to allow buoyant air bubbles to escape freely.
  • Ensure flow velocities remain strictly within the laminar Darcy regime; avoid excessively steep hydraulic gradients (i > 2.0).
  • Verify manometer piezometer lines are entirely free of air bubbles before recording differential head Δh.
  • Maintain constant room temperature to prevent water density and dynamic viscosity fluctuations during flow logging.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The civil engineering physical models apply within the specified calibrated operational window (10.0 to 60.0 cm).
  • Assumes homogeneous, isotropic continuum material behavior without unmodeled micro-fracture anomalies.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • Extreme inelastic yielding or cyclic degradation requires non-linear constitutive continuum damage mechanics.
