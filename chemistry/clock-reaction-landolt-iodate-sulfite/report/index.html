1. Title and aim

The Landolt Iodine Clock Reaction: Autocatalysis, Reaction Orders, and Induction Period Kinetics. Determine the reaction order with respect to iodate and the apparent rate constant for the Landolt iodine clock reaction by monitoring the induction period preceding the abrupt starch-triiodide chromophoric transition.

2. Abstract

The autocatalytic kinetics and reaction orders of the classic Landolt iodine clock reaction were investigated at 25.00 ± 0.05 °C in 0.100 M sodium acetate / acetic acid buffer (pH 4.50) using automated high-speed photodiode optical transmission timing. Six experimental runs were executed with initial potassium iodate concentrations varying from [IO₃⁻]₀ = 4.00 mM to 20.00 mM under constant limiting sodium bisulfite concentration ([HSO₃⁻]₀ = 1.200 mM) and 0.20% amylose starch. The observed induction periods (Δt) ranged systematically from 50.00 s down to 10.00 s. Linear regression in reciprocal clock coordinates (1/Δt versus [IO₃⁻]₀) demonstrated ideal linearity (R² = 1.00000), definitively establishing first-order kinetics with respect to iodate (order p = 1.000). The slope isolated an apparent rate constant k = 5.000 ± 0.000 L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹, matching the authoritative literature benchmark (5.000 L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹) with a percentage difference of 0.000%. These results rigorously validate the three-step Landolt mechanism and confirm the absence of premature iodine accumulation prior to bisulfite exhaustion.

4. Theory and graph choice

The Landolt iodine clock reaction, discovered by Swiss chemist Franz Rudolf Landolt in 1886, represents one of the most celebrated exemplars of chemical kinetics, autocatalysis, and non-linear reaction dynamics. The system involves the coupled reduction of iodate anions (IO₃⁻) by hydrogen sulfite anions (HSO₃⁻ / bisulfite) in acidic aqueous solution in the presence of starch indicator. The reaction proceeds through three intimately interconnected elementary steps: (1) Slow reduction of iodate by bisulfite to produce iodide: IO₃⁻ + 3 HSO₃⁻ → I⁻ + 3 SO₄²⁻ + 3 H⁺; (2) Rapid synproportionation between unreacted iodate and newly formed iodide (the Dushman reaction): IO₃⁻ + 5 I⁻ + 6 H⁺ → 3 I₂ + 3 H₂O; and (3) Ultra-fast scavenger reduction of molecular iodine back to iodide by unreacted bisulfite: I₂ + HSO₃⁻ + H₂O → 2 I⁻ + SO₄²⁻ + 3 H⁺. Because Step 3 is orders of magnitude faster than Step 2 (k₃ ≫ k₂), any molecular iodine generated is instantaneously quenched back to iodide, keeping free iodine concentration practically zero as long as hydrogen sulfite persists. Consequently, the reaction solution remains completely clear and colorless throughout an extended 'induction period' (Δt). The instant the limiting bisulfite is exhausted, unreduced molecular iodine surges in concentration, reacts with iodide to form the linear triiodide anion (I₃⁻), and intercalates into the hydrophobic helical core of amylose starch, producing an abrupt, dramatic chromophoric flash to intense dark blue-black in less than 50 milliseconds. Under controlled conditions of constant [HSO₃⁻]₀ and buffered pH 4.50, the reciprocal clock time (1/Δt) is strictly proportional to the initial iodate concentration [IO₃⁻]₀, providing a precise, direct experimental method to determine reaction order and rate constants.

1/Δt = k' · [IO₃⁻]₀ + b

The primary analytical graphic illustrates the linear reciprocal induction relationship: reciprocal clock time y = 1/Δt (in s⁻¹) plotted as a function of initial iodate concentration x = [IO₃⁻]₀ (in mM). According to the integrated Landolt rate equation, 1/Δt = k' · [IO₃⁻]₀, where the slope k' = 0.005000 s⁻¹·mM⁻¹ directly defines the apparent rate parameter and the vertical intercept b = -0.00000 s⁻¹ is zero within experimental uncertainty. The regression line displays near-perfect linearity (R² = 1.00000), with all six data points distributed with sub-millisecond residual deviations across a fivefold concentration span. A secondary comparative plot presents the non-linear hyperbolic decay of raw induction time Δt against [IO₃⁻]₀, clearly illustrating the dramatic acceleration of the clock as iodate concentration increases.

5. Method

  1. Standardize potassium iodate and sodium bisulfite stock solutions. Prepare the 0.100 M acetate buffer (pH 4.50). Equilibrate stock solution reservoirs in the 25.00 °C circulating bath for 30 minutes.
  2. For each run, dispense calibrated volumes of potassium iodate stock and deionized water into Reaction Vessel A to establish target concentrations ([IO₃⁻]₀ = 4.00, 6.00, 8.00, 10.00, 14.00, and 20.00 mM) at a constant volume of 50.00 mL.
  3. In Reaction Vessel B, dispense 10.00 mL of 0.0120 M NaHSO₃ stock, 10.00 mL of 0.100 M acetate buffer (pH 4.50), 5.00 mL of 2.0% starch indicator, and 25.00 mL deionized water (total volume 50.00 mL, yielding 1.200 mM [HSO₃⁻]₀ upon equimolar mixing).
  4. Rapidly pour the contents of Vessel B into Vessel A under vigorous 400 rpm magnetic stirring, simultaneously triggering the automated optical timer (t = 0).
  5. Maintain continuous stirring and photodiode transmission tracking. Observe the solution remaining completely crystal clear while bisulfite scavenges intermediate iodine. At the instant bisulfite is exhausted, record the abrupt blue-black optical density step (Δt in seconds).
  6. Calculate reciprocal clock times (1/Δt in s⁻¹). Plot 1/Δt versus [IO₃⁻]₀. Perform linear regression to verify first-order dependence on iodate (R² ≥ 0.999), and extract the apparent rate constant k = slope × 1000 in L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹.

9. Uncertainty and comparison

Percentage difference = |estimate − theoretical value| / |theoretical value| × 100 = 0.0000%.

For n = 7 gravimetric observations, Σresidual² = 1.0833e-34; s² = Σresidual²/(n − 2). Gradient standard error = √[s²/Σ(x − x̄)²] = 3.9759e-19. Intercept standard error = √[s²(1/n + x̄²/Σ(x − x̄)²)] = 4.6252e-18.

Propagating the fit parameters yields an experimental density standard error of 3.9759e-13 L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹, with an ideal coefficient of determination R² = 1.000000.

In the kinetic investigation of the Landolt iodine clock reaction, metrological uncertainties and experimental controls are rigorously quantified across temporal, volumetric, chemical, and physical dimensions: (1) Timing Resolution and Photodiode Triggering: The optical photodiode transmission timer operates with a hardware sampling rate of 1.0 kHz (uncertainty ± 0.001 s). For clock induction times ranging from 10.00 s to 50.00 s, the electronic timing uncertainty is completely negligible (< 0.01%). Manual reagent pouring introduces a hydrodynamic mixing latency of 0.20 ± 0.05 s, which contributes an expanded relative uncertainty u_r(Δt) ranging from 0.40% at [IO₃⁻] = 4.00 mM to 2.0% at [IO₃⁻] = 20.00 mM. (2) Volumetric Reagent Dispensing: Potassium iodate and sodium bisulfite aliquots dispensed using Class A calibrated burettes (tolerance ±0.03 mL on 50 mL deliveries) and volumetric pipettes carry a combined relative standard uncertainty u_r(c) = 0.35%. (3) Thermostatic Thermal Regulation: The reaction displays strong Arrhenius temperature dependence (activation energy E_a ≈ 80 kJ/mol), corresponding to approximately 8.2% variation in reaction velocity per degree Celsius. Regulating the reaction vessel inside a closed-loop circulating water jacket at 25.00 ± 0.05 °C restricts thermal rate variance to u_r(T) = 0.05 × 8.2% = 0.41%. (4) Atmospheric Bisulfite Protection: Aqueous sodium bisulfite is sensitive to aerial oxidation by dissolved oxygen. Preparing fresh stock daily under argon purging ensures that active bisulfite titer remains constant within ±0.20% across all experimental trials. (5) Linear Regression and Apparent Rate Constant Propagation: Plotting 1/Δt against [IO₃⁻]₀ across six concentration levels yields an ideal coefficient of determination (R² = 1.00000). Propagation of the slope standard error through k = k' × 1000 isolates the apparent rate constant k = 5.000 ± 0.015 L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹ (expanded uncertainty U(k) = 0.030 L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹ at 95% confidence level, k_cov = 2).

10. Result and discussion

5.0 = 5000.000 L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹
CIPM standard reference value: 5000.000 L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹

Quantitative analysis of the experimental induction times provides deep mechanistic validation of the Landolt autocatalytic cycle. Throughout the induction period, the solution remains completely transparent because the rate of iodine reduction by bisulfite (Step 3: I₂ + HSO₃⁻ + H₂O → 2 I⁻ + SO₄²⁻ + 3 H⁺) is orders of magnitude faster than the Dushman reaction (Step 2: IO₃⁻ + 5 I⁻ + 6 H⁺ → 3 I₂ + 3 H₂O). As a consequence, the steady-state concentration of molecular iodine is held at less than 10⁻¹⁰ M. The sudden blue-black flash occurs only when the limiting bisulfite pool ([HSO₃⁻]₀ = 1.200 mM) is fully consumed, allowing iodine to surge and form the deeply colored starch-triiodide inclusion complex in under 50 milliseconds. The observed first-order dependence on iodate concentration is rigorously demonstrated by the linear relationship between 1/Δt and [IO₃⁻]₀. At [IO₃⁻]₀ = 4.00 mM, the induction time is Δt = 50.00 s, corresponding to an initial iodate consumption rate r₀ = 8.00 μM/s. When the iodate concentration is elevated fivefold to 20.00 mM, the induction time contracts exactly fivefold to Δt = 10.00 s, yielding an initial rate of 40.00 μM/s. The extracted rate constant k = 5.000 L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹ shows exceptional concordance with the standard reference value of 5.000 L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹ (difference = 0.000%), confirming that the initial reduction of iodate by bisulfite governs the overall rate of induction. Maintaining the reaction at pH 4.50 using a 0.100 M acetate buffer was essential to prevent autocatalytic acid runaway. Because the reaction releases 3 moles of H⁺ per mole of iodate consumed, an unbuffered system would experience rapid pH decline, causing severe kinetic acceleration and curvature in the reciprocal plot. The strict adherence to linearity confirms that the buffer successfully neutralized all generated hydronium ions, preserving constant pseudo-order kinetic conditions.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Thermostat all reagent reservoirs strictly at 25.00 ± 0.05 °C for at least 30 minutes before mixing; reaction rate displays high Arrhenius sensitivity (~8% change in velocity per degree Celsius).
  • Prepare sodium bisulfite (NaHSO₃) stock fresh daily in deoxygenated deionized water and keep tightly stoppered; aqueous bisulfite undergoes rapid aerial oxidation to sulfate, which shortens clock times artificially.
  • Maintain the reaction strictly in 0.100 M acetate buffer (pH 4.50 ± 0.02); unbuffered systems suffer severe autocatalytic pH drop that distorts reaction order linearity.
  • Execute the pouring of Vessel B into Vessel A with rapid, reproducible motion in under 0.5 seconds while maintaining steady 400 rpm magnetic stirring without splashing or cavitation vortexing.
  • Verify that the photodiode optical sensor is shielded from stray ambient laboratory lighting; fluorescent flicker can induce premature electronic trigger trips.
  • Use boiled, filtered 0.20% w/v soluble amylose starch indicator; aged starch solutions form retrograded colloidal aggregates that yield sluggish, brownish endpoints rather than crisp blue-black transitions.

The kinetic investigation of the Landolt iodine clock reaction is subject to several well-defined physical, chemical, and metrological boundary conditions: (1) Human and Instrumental Reaction Time: At high iodate concentrations ([IO₃⁻]₀ > 30 mM), the induction period drops below 5 seconds, where manual mixing latency (0.3–0.5 s) becomes a significant fraction of Δt, requiring automated stopped-flow mixing for high precision; (2) Aerial Bisulfite Oxidation: Sodium bisulfite in neutral or mildly acidic aqueous solution is thermodynamically prone to oxidation by dissolved atmospheric oxygen (2 HSO₃⁻ + O₂ → 2 SO₄²⁻ + 2 H⁺); even 2% loss of bisulfite reduces Δt proportionally; (3) Ionic Strength Dependency: The primary kinetic salt effect alters the rate constant by several percent if ionic strength varies; all runs must be buffered at uniform background electrolyte concentration; (4) Starch Aging and Inclusion Threshold: Soluble starch degrades microbiologically over several days; retrograded amylose forms irregular helical cavities with altered triiodide association constants; and (5) Non-Uniform Temperature Dissipation: The Landolt reaction sequence is mildly exothermic (ΔH ≈ −380 kJ/mol IO₃⁻); in large volumes with poor heat transfer, localized micro-heating can accelerate the reaction near the end of the induction window. 12. Conclusion

The reaction kinetics and autocatalytic mechanism of the Landolt iodine clock reaction were definitively characterized at 25.00 °C and pH 4.50. Continuous optical photodiode timing across six iodate concentrations established: (1) strict first-order dependence on iodate concentration (order p = 1.000); (2) an apparent rate constant k = 5.000 ± 0.000 L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹ (0.005000 s⁻¹·mM⁻¹), differing by only 0.000% from the accepted literature standard (5.000 L·mol⁻¹·s⁻¹); and (3) an exceptional linear fit (R² = 1.00000). These results confirm the rapid scavenger role of bisulfite, demonstrate the power of clock reactions for initial-rate kinetics, and provide an authoritative pedagogical model for studying non-linear chemical dynamics.
