Civil engineering mechanics & structural foundations of Shear Frame Free Vibration.

In structural civil engineering, earthquake engineering, and wind engineering, all buildings, bridges, and infrastructure towers undergo dynamic oscillations when excited by transient seismic ground motions, hurricane gusts, or mechanical machinery. The fundamental dynamic parameter governing structural response is the fundamental natural circular frequency ω_n = √(k / m), where k is the lateral structural stiffness (N/m) and m is the effective floor mass (kg). For an idealized single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) shear frame—composed of rigid floor slabs supported by flexible vertical column cantilevers—the lateral stiffness provided by two parallel fixed-guided elastic columns of height L and flexural rigidity EI is k = 2 · (12EI / L³). When released from an initial lateral displacement x_0, the structure executes free damped harmonic oscillations: x(t) = x_0 · e^(-ζ·ω_n·t) · cos(ω_d·t - φ), where ζ is the dimensionless viscous damping ratio (typically 1% to 5% for welded steel and concrete structures) and ω_d = ω_n · √(1 - ζ²) is the damped natural frequency. The damping ratio is quantified experimentally via the Logarithmic Decrement method: δ = (1/n) · ln(x_i / x_{i+n}) ≈ 2πζ, where x_i and x_{i+n} are successive peak displacement amplitudes recorded by accelerometers. In this laboratory investigation, floor mass m is systematically varied from 2.0 to 12.0 kg to calibrate lateral frame stiffness slope k = 1850.0000 N/m, verify the inverse-mass frequency relation ω_n² = k/m, and determine structural damping ratio ζ.

Dynamic Equation of Motion for SDOF Shear Frame

An idealized single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) shear building frame consists of floor mass $m$, total lateral column stiffness $k$, and equivalent viscous damping coefficient $c$. Applying Newton's second law for free vibration:

\[ m \ddot{x} + c \dot{x} + k x = 0 \]

Dividing by $m$ and defining undamped natural circular frequency $\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}$ and damping ratio $\zeta = c / (2 m \omega_n)$:

\[ \ddot{x} + 2 \zeta \omega_n \dot{x} + \omega_n^2 x = 0 \]

For an underdamped structure ($\zeta < 1$), the solution for initial displacement $x(0) = x_0$ and $\dot{x}(0) = 0$ is:

\[ x(t) = x_0 e^{-\zeta \omega_n t} \left( \cos \omega_d t + \frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} \sin \omega_d t \right) \]

where damped natural frequency is $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$.

Inverse Mass Formulation and Linear Stiffness Extraction

Squaring the natural frequency relation yields:

\[ \omega_n^2 = k \cdot \left( \frac{1}{m} \right) \]

Linear regression of $\omega_n^2$ against inverse mass $1/m$ yields lateral stiffness $k = 1850.0000\text{ N/m}$.

Logarithmic Decrement Damping Formulation

The logarithmic decrement across $n$ cycles is:

\[ \delta = \frac{1}{n} \ln\left( \frac{x_i}{x_{i+n}} \right) = \frac{2\pi \zeta}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} \approx 2\pi \zeta \] \[ \zeta = \frac{\delta}{2\pi} \]

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

What is the physical assumption defining a 'shear frame' idealization?

A shear frame assumes that floor slabs and girders are infinitely rigid compared to columns (no joint rotation, θ = 0), and that axial column deformation is zero, meaning lateral floor displacement occurs strictly via pure double-curvature column shear flexure (k = 12EI/L³ per column).

How does increasing structural mass alter the natural period T_n of a building?

Natural period T_n = 2π / ω_n = 2π · √(m / k); therefore, increasing floor mass increases the natural vibration period proportionally to √m, causing the building to oscillate more slowly.

What is the logarithmic decrement method for measuring structural damping?

The logarithmic decrement δ is the natural logarithm of the ratio of any two successive peak amplitudes: δ = ln(x_i / x_{i+1}) = 2πζ / √(1 - ζ²); for light structural damping (ζ < 10%), it simplifies to δ ≈ 2πζ, yielding damping ratio ζ = δ / 2π.

What are the typical viscous damping ratios specified for civil engineering structures?

Design codes (ASCE 7, Eurocode 8) specify: ζ = 1% to 2% for welded steel structures, ζ = 3% to 5% for uncracked reinforced concrete, and ζ = 5% to 7% for cracked concrete during severe seismic plastic yielding.

What is the phenomenon of resonance in civil engineering dynamics?

Resonance occurs when the frequency of an external dynamic excitation (earthquake ground shaking, wind gusting, pedestrian marching) matches the structure's natural frequency (ω_exc ≈ ω_n), causing vibration amplitudes to multiply dramatically into catastrophic failure.

How do Tuned Mass Dampers (TMDs) suppress building vibrations in skyscrapers?

A TMD (such as the 660-tonne pendulum inside Taipei 101) is a secondary mass-spring-damper tuned precisely to the building's fundamental natural frequency; when the tower sways, the TMD swings out of phase, dissipating inertial energy through hydraulic dampers.

Experimental precautions and laboratory safety

  • Release the shear frame from initial displacement cleanly without imparting any manual pushing impulse or lateral twist.
  • Fasten surcharge masses rigidly to the center of the floor plate to prevent mass rattling that absorbs spurious energy.
  • Verify that accelerometer cables are lightweight and taped along the support frame to avoid adding parasitic cable damping.
  • Ensure initial displacement does not exceed the elastic yield limit of the spring steel columns (keep strain < 0.2%).

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The civil engineering physical models apply within the specified calibrated operational window (2.0 to 12.0 kg).
  • Assumes homogeneous, isotropic continuum material behavior without unmodeled micro-fracture anomalies.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • Extreme inelastic yielding or cyclic degradation requires non-linear constitutive continuum damage mechanics.
