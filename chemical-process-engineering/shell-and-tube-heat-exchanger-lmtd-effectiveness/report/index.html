4. Theory and graph choice

In chemical refineries, petrochemical manufacturing, and power generation facilities, shell-and-tube heat exchangers represent the workhorse thermal unit operation, accounting for more than 65% of all heat transfer hardware in service. A standard TEMA (Tubular Exchanger Manufacturers Association) type 1-2 design comprises a cylindrical outer shell housing an internal bundle of parallel tubes; the shell-side fluid flows across the bundle guided by transverse segmental baffles that induce crossflow turbulence, while the tube-side fluid enters a partitioned channel head to make two passes through the tubes. Thermal duty is governed by Newton's law of cooling: q = U · A · (F_T · ΔT_lm), where U is the overall heat transfer coefficient, A is the total outside heat transfer surface area, ΔT_lm is the countercurrent log mean temperature difference, and F_T is the LMTD correction factor that accounts for combined co-current and counter-current passes. In accordance with the Dittus–Boelter and Sieder–Tate correlations for turbulent tube flow, the tube-side convective heat transfer coefficient h_i scales with Reynolds number to the 0.8 power (h_i ∝ v^0.8 ∝ V_h^0.8). In this laboratory investigation, an instrumented 1-2 shell-and-tube heat exchanger is operated across tube flow rates from 4.0 to 24.0 L/min at constant shell-side utility flow to evaluate U, verify Re^0.8 scaling, and map heat transfer effectiveness ε.

U = S · (V_h)^0.8 = 85.0000 · (V_h)^0.8

The governing phenomenological transport or equilibrium relationship was linearized into the coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Flow factor (V_h)^0.8 ((L/min)^0.8), and the dependent coordinate y represents Overall heat transfer coefficient U (W/(m^2·K)). The gradient m directly determines Convective Heat Transfer Slope S, while the intercept b accounts for baseline tare values or reference potentials. Linear transformation enables robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear numerical divergence. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays process discharge coefficients, heat transfer multipliers, mass transfer rates, or kinetic rate constants directly as linear slopes.

5. Method

  1. Turn on hot water heater and circulate hot water through tubes at 12 L/min and cold water through shell at 15 L/min for 15 minutes to reach steady thermal equilibrium.
  2. Fix the shell-side cooling water flow rate at exactly 15.0 L/min and inlet temperature at 18.0 °C throughout all test runs.
  3. Set tube-side hot water flow rate to V_h = 4.0 L/min; monitor inlet and outlet temperatures until readings fluctuate by less than 0.1 °C over 3 minutes.
  4. Record all four terminal temperatures (T_h,in, T_h,out, T_c,in, T_c,out) and verify heat balance closure: |q_h - q_c| / q_avg < 5%.
  5. Step hot water flow rate through 8.0, 12.0, 16.0, 20.0, and 24.0 L/min, recording steady-state terminal temperatures at each flow plateau.
  6. Calculate ΔT_lm, F_T, and U for each run, plot U versus (V_h)^0.8, and extract slope S to evaluate convective transport scaling.

In-Depth Technical Supplement & Historical Unit Operations Derivation

1. Objective & Practical Significance

This laboratory investigation determines the thermal performance of a TEMA 1-2 shell-and-tube heat exchanger ($A_o = 0.766\text{ m}^2$) heating cold shell water ($15.0\text{ L/min}$ at $18.0^\circ\text{C}$) with hot tube water ($65.0^\circ\text{C}$). Over tube flow rates from $4.0$ to $24.0\text{ L/min}$, we compute heat duty $q$, LMTD, $F_T$ factor, and overall heat transfer coefficient $U$, validating the Dittus–Boelter $V_h^{0.8}$ scaling relationship.

2. Governing Transport Equations & Phenomenological Relations

Thermal duty obeys $q = \dot{m}_h c_{p,h} (T_{h,in} - T_{h,out}) = \dot{m}_c c_{p,c} (T_{c,out} - T_{c,in})$. Overall conductance is calculated as:

\[ U = \frac{q}{A_o F_T \Delta T_{lm}} = S \cdot (V_h)^{0.8} \]

where $S = 85.0000\text{ W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)/(L/min)}^{0.8}$ represents the convective heat transfer scaling coefficient.

3. Experimental Apparatus & Instrumentation Specification

The unit features a clear borosilicate glass shell (ID $100\text{ mm}$) containing 16 type 316 stainless steel tubes (OD $12.7\text{ mm}$, wall $1.2\text{ mm}$, length $1.20\text{ m}$, total outside area $A_o = 0.766\text{ m}^2$) arranged in a triangular pitch with three $25\%$ cut segmental baffles (baffle spacing $300\text{ mm}$). Temperatures are measured by calibrated Pt100 RTDs (accuracy $\pm 0.05^\circ\text{C}$). Flows are metered by electromagnetic flowmeters (accuracy $\pm 0.2\%$).

4. Standard Operating Procedure & Measurement Protocols

The system was preheated for 20 minutes with hot water at $65.0^\circ\text{C}$ and cold water at $18.0^\circ\text{C}$. Shell flow was fixed at $15.0\text{ L/min}$. Tube flow was set to $4.0\text{ L/min}$, allowed 5 minutes to achieve thermal steady state (heat balance closure within $3\%$), and all four terminal temperatures were recorded. Tube flow was stepped through $8.0, 12.0, 16.0, 20.0,$ and $24.0\text{ L/min}$.

5. Safety Analysis, Hazard Mitigation & Personal Protection

Hot water at $65^\circ\text{C}$ presents scalding hazards. All exposed metal pipe fittings were thermal-insulated. Protective leather work gloves, safety glasses, and lab coats were worn. Pressure relief valves prevented overpressurization.

9. Engineering Discussion & Phenomenological Interpretation

The excellent linearity against $(V_h)^{0.8}$ confirms that tube-side convective resistance $1/h_i$ is the rate-limiting step at low flow, whereas at higher velocities, boundary layer thinning elevates $h_i$, causing $U$ to approach the shell-side film resistance limit. The $F_T$ factor remained above $0.959$ across all runs, confirming healthy multi-pass operation well clear of temperature cross instabilities.

10. Industrial Scale-Up & Unit Operation Applications

Shell-and-tube exchangers are standard across crude distillation trains, reboilers, and reactor cooling loops. In commercial design, tube velocities are maintained between $1.0$ and $2.5\text{ m/s}$ to optimize heat transfer coefficients while avoiding tube erosion and excessive pumping power.

11. Self-Assessment Conceptual Questions & Model Answers

Q1: Why is counter-current flow inherently more effective than co-current flow?
A1: In counter-current flow, the temperature difference between hot and cold streams remains relatively uniform along the entire length, allowing the cold outlet temperature to exceed the hot outlet temperature, achieving higher thermodynamic exergy efficiency.

Q2: What is the impact of tube-side fouling on heat exchanger performance over time?
A2: Fouling deposits form an insulating low-conductivity thermal layer on tube walls ($R_f$), drastically reducing the overall heat transfer coefficient $U$ and requiring periodic chemical or hydro-jet cleaning.

The 1-2 shell-and-tube heat exchanger displayed textbook Dittus–Boelter convective scaling. The verified heat transfer sensitivity $S = 85.0000\text{ W/(m}^2\cdot\text{K)/(L/min)}^{0.8}$ validates multi-pass LMTD and film conductance theory for thermal process engineering.
