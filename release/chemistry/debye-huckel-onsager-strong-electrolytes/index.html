Physical chemistry assumptions and metrological limits

['The Debye-Hückel-Onsager limiting law is strictly valid only at infinite dilution and low concentrations (c < 0.015 M for 1:1 salts); at higher concentrations, finite ion size and ion association cause deviations.', 'Dissolved atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO₂) forms carbonic acid which can elevate solvent background conductivity if water is not degassed or protected under nitrogen.', 'For multi-valent electrolytes (e.g., 2:2 salts like MgSO₄), strong electrostatic ion pairing requires extended Fuoss-Onsager association models rather than the simple linear square-root law.'] The Debye-Hückel-Onsager limiting law is expressed as Λ_m = Λ_m° - (A_rel · Λ_m° + B_el) · √c = Λ_m° - S · √c, where Λ_m° is the limiting molar conductivity at infinite dilution and S is the theoretical Onsager slope. For a 1:1 univalent electrolyte in water at 25.00 °C, the theoretical coefficients derived from first principles are A_rel = 0.2289 (mol/L)⁻¹/² and B_el = 60.20 S·cm²·mol⁻¹·(mol/L)⁻¹/². For potassium chloride (Λ_m° = 149.85 S·cm²·mol⁻¹), the theoretical slope is S = 94.50 S·cm²·mol⁻¹·(mol/L)⁻¹/². By plotting molar conductivity Λ_m against √c, linear least-squares regression yields an empirical intercept confirming Λ_m° = 149.9 S·cm²·mol⁻¹ and an empirical slope confirming S = 94.5 S·cm²·mol⁻¹·M⁻¹/² with R² > 0.99999, providing authoritative experimental confirmation of electrolyte conduction theory.

FOLLOW THE METHOD

A reason behind every step.

Determine the limiting molar conductivity (Λ_m°) of potassium chloride (KCl) and test the validity of the Debye-Hückel-Onsager limiting law across a series of dilute strong electrolyte solutions. Suggested independent volumetric settings: 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0, 12.0 mM.

01

Solvent Background Conductivity Determination

Rinse the platinized platinum cell four times with freshly drawn ultrapure water. Fill the thermostatic vessel with 80.0 mL of ultrapure water equilibrated at 25.00 ± 0.02 °C. Record the solvent background conductivity κ_w (typically 0.04 to 0.06 μS·cm⁻¹), which must be subtracted from all subsequent electrolyte measurements.

02

Cell Constant Verification with Primary KCl Standard

Prepare a 0.0100 M KCl calibration standard from desiccated primary-standard salt. Equilibrate at 25.00 °C in the thermostatic bath and measure conductivity κ_std = 1408.7 μS·cm⁻¹ to confirm the cell constant K_cell = 1.000 ± 0.004 cm⁻¹.

03

Serial Dilution of Strong Electrolyte Series

Using calibrated Class A volumetric flasks, prepare six precision test solutions of KCl spanning the dilute Debye-Hückel regime: 0.50 mM, 1.00 mM, 2.00 mM, 4.00 mM, 8.00 mM, and 12.00 mM (concentrations c from 0.0005 M to 0.0120 M).

04

Thermostatic Equilibration of Analyte Samples

Transfer the lowest concentration test solution (0.50 mM KCl) into the double-walled conduction cell. Allow 10 minutes of closed-loop thermal equilibration at 25.00 ± 0.02 °C under gentle magnetic stirring, pausing stirring prior to logging.

05

Conductance Acquisition & Frequency Independence Check

Measure specific electrolytic conductivity κ on the 1.0 kHz bridge. Verify frequency independence by switching briefly to 2.0 kHz to confirm that capacitive electrode reactance is negligible (< 0.02%). Repeat sequentially for all six concentrations from lowest to highest.

06

Molar Conductivity Computation & Onsager Linearization

Subtract solvent background conductivity (κ_corr = κ - κ_w). Compute molar conductivity Λ_m = 1000 × κ_corr / c_M (S·cm²·mol⁻¹). Calculate the square root of molarity √c_M, construct the Kohlrausch plot (Λ_m vs √c), and perform least-squares linear regression to extract limiting molar conductivity Λ_m° and the empirical Onsager slope S.
