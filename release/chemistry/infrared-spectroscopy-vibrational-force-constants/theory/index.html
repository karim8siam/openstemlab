Theoretical Foundations of FTIR Spectroscopy & Diatomic Force Constants and Quantitative Chemical Metrology

Vibrational-rotational infrared spectroscopy provides an extraordinary experimental window into the quantum mechanical architecture of chemical bonds, allowing direct determination of molecular force constants, equilibrium bond lengths, moments of inertia, and potential energy surface curvatures with sub-picometer precision. When a heteronuclear diatomic molecule absorbs infrared radiation, transitions occur simultaneously between vibrational levels (v) and rotational levels (J). For hydrogen chloride (HCl), the fundamental absorption band centered near 2886 cm⁻¹ consists of two symmetric manifolds of sharp, regularly spaced peaks: the R-branch (ΔJ = +1) on the high-frequency side and the P-branch (ΔJ = −1) on the low-frequency side, separated by a distinct central gap at the forbidden band origin (ΔJ = 0). Each individual line appears as a doublet resolved into H³⁵Cl and H³⁷Cl isotopologues in accordance with the 3:1 natural isotopic abundance of chlorine. In this investigation, high-resolution FTIR spectroscopy (0.5 cm⁻¹) of gas-phase HCl in a 10 cm NaCl-window cell is performed. By evaluating the mean transition wavenumbers ν̃_mean(m) across six rotational line pairs, ordinary least-squares regression extracts the fundamental vibrational band origin (ν̃₀ = 2885.90 cm⁻¹) and vibration-rotation coupling constant (α_e = 0.3072 cm⁻¹), isolating the harmonic bond force constant (k = 481.0 N/m) and equilibrium bond distance (r_e = 1.275 Å) with exquisite precision.

TOPIC 01 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

What are the quantum mechanical selection rules for vibration-rotation transitions in a heteronuclear diatomic molecule?

For a vibrating and rotating heteronuclear diatomic molecule modeled as an anharmonic oscillator and non-rigid rotor, the electric dipole moment must change during vibrational displacement (dμ/dr ≠ 0) for infrared activity. In the electric dipole approximation, the vibrational selection rule for the harmonic oscillator is Δv = ±1 (the fundamental band). For real anharmonic molecules, weak overtones (Δv = ±2, ±3) also appear. Concurrently, conservation of angular momentum imposes the rotational selection rule ΔJ = ±1 for diatomic molecules with zero electronic orbital angular momentum in the electronic ground state (¹Σ⁺, such as HCl). The transition with ΔJ = J' − J'' = +1 (where the molecule gains one quantum of rotational angular momentum) constitutes the R-branch (high-frequency side), while ΔJ = −1 (where the molecule loses rotational angular momentum) forms the P-branch (low-frequency side). A Q-branch (ΔJ = 0) is strictly forbidden for ¹Σ diatomic molecules because photon emission/absorption involves intrinsic spin-1 angular momentum that must be compensated by a change in molecular rotational state.

TOPIC 02 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Why is there a conspicuous central gap (missing line) in the infrared spectrum of HCl at 2885.90 cm⁻¹?

The central gap at 2885.90 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the hypothetical pure vibrational transition (v = 0, J = 0 ⟶ v = 1, J = 0), which would have ΔJ = 0 (the Q-branch origin). Because HCl in its ground electronic state (¹Σ⁺) possesses no electronic orbital angular momentum about the internuclear axis, a pure vibrational transition with zero change in rotational state (ΔJ = 0) cannot satisfy conservation of total angular momentum when absorbing an electric dipole photon (spin quantum number S = 1). Consequently, the transition probability is identically zero, leaving an empty frequency gap of width Δν̃ ≈ 4B₀ ≈ 41.8 cm⁻¹ between the first R-branch line R(0) at 2906.17 cm⁻¹ and the first P-branch line P(1) at 2865.02 cm⁻¹.

TOPIC 03 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Why does each rotational line in the FTIR spectrum of natural HCl appear as a doublet with an intensity ratio of approximately 3:1?

Natural chlorine consists of two stable isotopes: ³⁵Cl (75.76% natural abundance) and ³⁷Cl (24.24% natural abundance), yielding an isotopic ratio of approximately 3.12:1. Because isotopic substitution changes the nuclear mass without altering the electronic potential energy surface (Born-Oppenheimer approximation), the reduced mass of H³⁷Cl (μ = 1.63155 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) is slightly greater than that of H³⁵Cl (μ = 1.62665 × 10⁻²⁷ kg). Because the vibrational frequency scales inversely with the square root of reduced mass (ν̃₀ ∝ μ⁻¹/²), the band origin of H³⁷Cl is shifted toward lower wavenumbers by Δν̃₀ ≈ 2.1 cm⁻¹ (ν̃₀ = 2883.8 cm⁻¹ for H³⁷Cl vs 2885.9 cm⁻¹ for H³⁵Cl). Similarly, the rotational constant B = h / (8π²cμr²) is slightly smaller for H³⁷Cl. Under high-resolution FTIR spectroscopy (≤ 0.5 cm⁻¹), every single R-branch and P-branch line is cleanly resolved into a doublet: a taller peak for H³⁵Cl and a closely adjacent shorter peak for H³⁷Cl whose intensity ratio mirrors the 3:1 natural isotopic abundance.

TOPIC 04 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

How does vibration-rotation coupling (α_e) cause line spacing to compress in the R-branch and expand in the P-branch?

In a real diatomic molecule, the internuclear bond is not a rigid rod but an anharmonic oscillator. Because the potential energy curve is asymmetric (Morse potential), as the vibrational quantum number increases from v = 0 to v = 1, the average internuclear bond distance increases (r₁ > r₀) due to anharmonic repulsion at short distances. Because the rotational constant is inversely proportional to the moment of inertia (B_v = h / (8π²cμ⟨r²⟩_v)), the effective rotational constant in the excited vibrational state is smaller than in the ground state: B₁ < B₀. The difference is parameterized by the vibration-rotation coupling constant: B_v = B_e − α_e (v + 1/2), where α_e = B₀ − B₁ > 0. For transition lines parameterized by running index m: ν̃(m) = ν̃₀ + (B₁ + B₀)m + (B₁ − B₀)m² = ν̃₀ + (2B_e − 2α_e)m − α_e m². In the R-branch (m > 0), the negative quadratic term −α_e m² opposes m, causing successive line spacings Δν̃ = 2B₀ − 2α_e(m + 1) to contract progressively toward higher frequencies. In the P-branch (m < 0), the quadratic term reinforces the spacing, causing successive lines to spread progressively further apart.

TOPIC 05 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Derive the linear relationship for the mean wavenumber ν̃_mean(m) = [ν̃_R(m−1) + ν̃_P(m)] / 2.

The rotational lines are indexed by integer m: for the R-branch, transitions originate at J'' = m − 1 and terminate at J' = m, so ν̃_R(m−1) = ν̃₀ + (B₁ + B₀)m + (B₁ − B₀)m². For the P-branch, transitions originate at J'' = m and terminate at J' = m − 1, corresponding to replacing m with −m: ν̃_P(m) = ν̃₀ − (B₁ + B₀)m + (B₁ − B₀)m². Adding the two corresponding transition wavenumbers: ν̃_R(m−1) + ν̃_P(m) = [ν̃₀ + (B₁ + B₀)m + (B₁ − B₀)m²] + [ν̃₀ − (B₁ + B₀)m + (B₁ − B₀)m²] = 2ν̃₀ + 2(B₁ − B₀)m². Dividing by 2 yields the mean wavenumber: ν̃_mean(m) = [ν̃_R(m−1) + ν̃_P(m)] / 2 = ν̃₀ + (B₁ − B₀)m². Because B₁ − B₀ = −α_e, this simplifies identically to: ν̃_mean(m) = ν̃₀ − α_e · m². Thus, plotting ν̃_mean(m) versus m² yields an exact straight line with slope equal to −α_e and Y-intercept equal to the fundamental vibrational band origin ν̃₀.

TOPIC 06 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

How is the harmonic force constant k calculated from the band origin ν̃₀ and reduced mass μ?

In classical and quantum mechanics, the fundamental vibrational frequency of a harmonic oscillator is given by ω₀ = √(k/μ) in radians per second, or ν₀ = (1 / 2π) √(k/μ) in Hertz. Converting frequency to spectroscopic wavenumber in units of cm⁻¹ via ν̃₀ = ν₀ / c (where c is the speed of light in cm/s): ν̃₀ = (1 / 2πc) √(k/μ). Squaring both sides: ν̃₀² = k / (4π²c²μ). Rearranging explicitly for the force constant: k = 4π²c² ν̃₀² μ. For H³⁵Cl, using ν̃₀ = 2885.90 cm⁻¹, c = 2.99792458 × 10¹⁰ cm/s, and μ = 1.626652 × 10⁻²⁷ kg: k = 4π² × (2.997925 × 10¹⁰)² × (2885.90)² × (1.626652 × 10⁻²⁷) = 480.68 N/m ≈ 481.0 N/m. This force constant measures the curvature (second derivative) of the electronic potential energy surface at the equilibrium internuclear distance: k = (d²V/dr²)_{r=r_e}.

TOPIC 07 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

How does the equilibrium bond length r_e relate to the rotational constant B_e, and how is it determined experimentally?

The rotational constant B_e represents the rigid-rotor rotational energy parameter: B_e = h / (8π² c I_e), where I_e = μ r_e² is the equilibrium moment of inertia about the center of mass. Solving for the equilibrium internuclear bond distance r_e: r_e = √[ h / (8π² c μ B_e) ]. In terms of the experimental constants B₀ and B₁ obtained from combination differences or line positions: B_e = B₀ + 0.5 α_e = (3 B₀ − B₁) / 2. For H³⁵Cl, B_e = 10.5934 cm⁻¹ and μ = 1.62665 × 10⁻²⁷ kg yield: r_e = √[ 6.62607 × 10⁻³⁴ / (8π² × 2.997925 × 10¹⁰ × 1.62665 × 10⁻²⁷ × 10.5934) ] = 1.2746 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 1.275 Å. This establishes the equilibrium separation between the hydrogen nucleus and chlorine nucleus in the electronic ground state with sub-picometer precision.

Connect the theory to experimental observations.

The physical chemistry simulation model evaluates gas-phase H³⁵Cl at 25.0 °C (sample pressure 25.0 Torr, 100.0 mm path length). System parameters: reduced mass μ(H³⁵Cl) = 1.626652 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; fundamental band origin ν̃₀ = 2885.90 cm⁻¹; equilibrium rotational constant B_e = 10.5934 cm⁻¹; vibration-rotation coupling constant α_e = 0.3072 cm⁻¹; ground state rotational constant B₀ = 10.4398 cm⁻¹; excited state rotational constant B₁ = 10.1326 cm⁻¹. Across the six rotational pairs m = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], measured mean transition wavenumbers are ν̃_mean = [2885.593, 2884.671, 2883.135, 2880.985, 2878.220, 2874.841] cm⁻¹. Plotting ν̃_mean against m² = [1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36] yields a linear slope of −α_e = −0.30719 cm⁻¹ (α_e = 0.3072 cm⁻¹) and Y-intercept ν̃₀ = 2885.900 cm⁻¹ with R² = 1.0000000. Applying the harmonic relation k = 4π²c²ν̃₀²μ extracts an empirical force constant k = 480.68 N/m (difference = 0.0660% from the 481.0 N/m literature benchmark), confirming quantum mechanical harmonic and anharmonic oscillator models.
