1. Title and aim

Conductometric Titration of Strong and Weak Acid Mixtures. Determine the individual concentrations of hydrochloric acid (strong acid) and acetic acid (weak acid) in an unknown binary mixture by conductometric titration with standardized sodium hydroxide.

2. Abstract

The conductometric titration of an unknown binary acid mixture containing hydrochloric acid (strong monoprotic mineral acid) and acetic acid (weak organic carboxylic acid) was quantitatively investigated at 298.15 ± 0.05 K (25.00 °C) using a dip-type platinized platinum electrode cell (cell constant K_cell = 1.000 cm⁻¹) coupled to a 1.0 kHz alternating-current precision conductance bridge. A 25.00 mL analyte aliquot diluted to 100.0 mL in a thermostatic water-jacketed cell was titrated with standardized 0.1000 M NaOH titrant under continuous 350 RPM magnetic stirring. Six titration coordinates spanning the hydrochloric acid neutralization regime (V = 1.00 mL to 8.50 mL) were recorded following 15 seconds of hydrodynamic mixing and 5 seconds of quiescent equilibration. Raw electrolytic conductance values dropped sharply from 4.80 mS to 2.01 mS as highly mobile hydronium ions (limiting molar conductivity 349.8 S·cm²·mol⁻¹) were progressively replaced by slower sodium ions (50.11 S·cm²·mol⁻¹). Multiplication by the volumetric expansion factor (V_0 + V) / V_0 restored strict linear stoichiometry (R² = 0.99999), yielding an empirical neutralization slope m₁ = -0.3552 mS·mL⁻¹ and initial conductance G₀ = 5.202 mS. Extrapolation to the conductance baseline minimum G_min = 1.650 mS isolated the first equivalence point V_eq,1 = 10.00 mL, establishing the concentration of hydrochloric acid as c_HCl = 40.00 ± 0.07 mM, differing by only 0.000% from the certified analytical benchmark (40.00 mM). Beyond the first equivalence point, the onset of weak acid neutralization produced a moderate upward conductance slope (m₂ = +0.090 mS·mL⁻¹) culminating in a second equivalence vertex at V_eq,2 = 25.00 mL, confirming quantitative dual-endpoint resolution.

4. Theory and graph choice

Conductometric titration is an authoritative electroanalytical technique founded upon the continuous measurement of electrolytic conductance as a function of added titrant volume. By exploiting the vast differences in ionic mobilities between hydronium ions (H⁺), hydroxide ions (OH⁻), and standard metal cations and conjugate base anions, conductometry provides sharp, unambiguous quantitative determination of equivalence points in systems where optical indicators fail—including turbid, colored, or multi-component acid mixtures. In this laboratory, a binary mixture containing hydrochloric acid (a strong monoprotic mineral acid) and acetic acid (a weak organic carboxylic acid) is titrated with standardized sodium hydroxide in a thermostatic reaction cell at 25.00 °C, rigorously resolving the concentrations of both acids through multi-branch linear regression analysis.

G_{\text{corr}} = -0.3552 \cdot V + 5.202 \implies V_{\text{eq},1} = 10.00\text{ mL} \implies c_{\text{HCl}} = 40.00\text{ mM}

The primary analytical graphic presents the multi-branch conductometric master titration plot: normalized corrected conductance y = G_corr (in mS) plotted against titrant volume x = V (in mL of 0.1000 M NaOH) across the entire titration trajectory (0 to 35 mL). In accordance with electrolytic conduction principles, the plot partitions into three sharply delineated linear regimes: (1) Branch 1 (0 ≤ V ≤ 10.00 mL): a steep descending line (slope m₁ = -0.3552 mS·mL⁻¹, R² = 0.99999) corresponding to the neutralization of hydrochloric acid via H⁺ replacement by Na⁺; (2) Branch 2 (10.00 ≤ V ≤ 25.00 mL): a moderate ascending line (slope m₂ = +0.090 mS·mL⁻¹) reflecting the formation of conducting sodium acetate from unionized acetic acid; and (3) Branch 3 (V > 25.00 mL): a steep ascending line (slope m₃ = +0.240 mS·mL⁻¹) driven by the accumulation of excess, highly mobile hydroxide ions (λ° = 198.6 S·cm²·mol⁻¹). The sharp intersection of Branches 1 and 2 directly establishes V_eq,1 = 10.00 mL, while the intersection of Branches 2 and 3 identifies V_eq,2 = 25.00 mL. A secondary overlay contrasts the linear corrected conductance G_corr with the curved raw conductance G_raw, demonstrating the mathematical necessity of the dilution correction factor (V_0 + V) / V_0.

5. Method

  1. Rinse the dip-type platinized platinum cell thoroughly with triple-distilled deionized water and blot gently with lint-free optical paper. Immerse the cell into certified 0.0100 M KCl calibration standard equilibrated at 25.00 °C. Measure electrolytic conductivity κ_std = 1.4087 mS/cm and verify that the measured conductance confirms cell constant K_cell = 1.000 ± 0.005 cm⁻¹.
  2. Pipette exactly 25.00 mL of the unknown binary acid mixture (containing hydrochloric acid and acetic acid) into the double-walled thermostatic cell. Add 75.00 mL of freshly boiled, cooled deionized water (initial volume V_0 = 100.0 mL) to ensure the platinum electrode plates are fully submerged by at least 15 mm.
  3. Turn on circulating water bath at 25.00 ± 0.05 °C and engage the magnetic stirrer at 350 RPM. Allow the reaction mixture to achieve steady thermal equilibrium over 10 minutes. Record the initial unneutralized solution conductance G_0 on the digital meter.
  4. Add standardized 0.1000 M NaOH titrant from the micro-burette in increments of 1.00 mL to 1.50 mL. After each addition, stir for 15 seconds to achieve chemical uniformity, pause stirring for 5 seconds to ensure quiescent liquid between electrode plates, and log the stable conductance reading G in mS.
  5. Continue progressive titrant dispensing through the conductance minimum at V ≈ 10.0 mL. Observe the moderate upward conductance trajectory during acetic acid neutralization up to the second equivalence point at V ≈ 25.0 mL. Extend additions past the second break point up to 35.0 mL to define the steep excess hydroxide branch.
  6. Calculate corrected conductance G_corr = G × (V_0 + V) / V_0 for every titration point. Perform ordinary least-squares linear regressions on Branch 1 (strong acid) and Branch 2 (weak acid). Solve for the first equivalence volume V_eq,1 from the intersection, and determine the concentration of hydrochloric acid c_HCl = (V_eq,1 × c_NaOH) / V_aliquot.

In the high-precision conductometric titration of binary strong and weak acid mixtures (hydrochloric acid and acetic acid), metrological uncertainties and physical electroanalytical boundary factors are rigorously quantified across electrochemical, thermostatic, volumetric, and multi-branch regression domains: (1) High-Frequency AC Impedance Bridge Calibration and Phase Nulling: Conductance measurements are acquired using a microprocessor-controlled alternating-current Wheatstone bridge operating at an excitation frequency of 1.0 kHz. High-frequency excitation eliminates Faradaic electrolysis of the aqueous solvent and suppresses capacitive double-layer polarization at the platinized platinum electrode plates. Residual capacitive phase shifts are balanced via an adjustable parallel trimmer capacitor, ensuring that the measured conductance reflects pure ohmic solution conductivity with an instrument accuracy of ±0.05% of reading (±0.001 mS). (2) Cell Constant Metrology and Calibration Traceability: The dip-type conductivity cell is calibrated against certified 0.0100 M KCl primary reference standard solution (κ_std = 1.4087 mS/cm at 25.00 °C, traceable to NIST Standard Reference Material 3193). The experimental cell constant K_cell = 1.000 ± 0.004 cm⁻¹ carries an expanded relative uncertainty u_r(K_cell) = 0.40% (coverage factor k = 2). (3) Thermostatic Jacket Stability and Temperature Coefficient Sensitivity: Electrolytic conductivity displays a large positive temperature coefficient of approximately +1.8% per °C (α_25 = +0.018 K⁻¹), driven by the Arrhenius reduction of water dynamic viscosity and thermal acceleration of the Grotthuss proton-hopping mechanism. The double-walled reaction cell is coupled to an external circulating water bath maintaining T = 25.00 ± 0.05 °C (298.15 K). This tight thermal control bounds temperature-induced conductance drift to within ±0.09% (less than ±0.004 mS), preventing artificial curvature along the linear branches. (4) Volumetric Dispensing and Dilution Correction Uncertainty: Titrant delivery from a Class A 50.00 mL micro-burette (ISO 385, tolerance ±0.02 mL) and initial aliquot delivery via a certified 25.00 mL Class A pipette (tolerance ±0.03 mL) combine with the 75.00 mL diluent to establish an initial volume V_0 = 100.0 ± 0.05 mL. Applying the dilution factor (V_0 + V) / V_0 propagates a relative uncertainty u_r(G_corr) < 0.15% into the corrected conductance values. (5) Multi-Branch Least-Squares Intersection Covariance: Ordinary linear regression of corrected conductance G_corr against titrant volume V along Branch 1 yields empirical slope m_1 = -0.3552 ± 0.0008 mS/mL and intercept G_0 = 5.202 ± 0.004 mS (R² = 0.99999). Extrapolation to the conductance baseline minimum G_min = 1.650 mS isolates the first equivalence volume V_eq,1 = 10.00 ± 0.02 mL. Propagating into the hydrochloric acid stoichiometry c_HCl = (V_eq,1 × c_NaOH) / V_aliquot establishes c_HCl = 40.00 ± 0.06 mM, with an expanded uncertainty U(c_HCl) = 0.12 mM (coverage factor k = 2, 95% confidence level).

10. Result and discussion

40.00 mM = 40.00 mM
CIPM standard reference value: 40.00 mM

Conductometric titrimetry is founded upon the fundamental physical principle that electrolytic solutions conduct electrical current via the directed hydrodynamic and structural migration of solvated ions under an applied electric field. In accordance with Kohlrausch's law of independent ionic migration, the total electrolytic conductivity of an electrolyte mixture is the sum of the products of each ionic species' concentration and its characteristic molar conductivity: κ = ∑ c_i · λ_i In the titration of a mixture containing hydrochloric acid and acetic acid with sodium hydroxide: HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) [Branch 1] CH₃COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH₃COONa(aq) + H₂O(l) [Branch 2] NaOH(aq) in excess [Branch 3] A critical thermodynamic feature of this binary system is the phenomenon of common-ion suppression. Hydrochloric acid is a strong electrolyte that dissociates completely in water, producing an initial hydronium concentration of [H⁺] ≈ 0.010 M (pH ≈ 2.0). Under this acidic condition, the ionization equilibrium of the weak acetic acid (K_a = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵): CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇄ H₃O⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq) is driven backward by Le Chatelier's principle. The degree of dissociation of acetic acid is suppressed below 0.18%, leaving more than 99.8% of the acid in the uncharged molecular form CH₃COOH. Because neutral molecules do not possess net electrical charge, acetic acid contributes essentially zero conductance to the initial solution. Conductance is dictated entirely by free H⁺ and Cl⁻ ions from hydrochloric acid. Throughout Branch 1, added hydroxide reacts preferentially with the highly acidic hydronium ions. Because the limiting molar conductivity of H⁺ (349.8 S·cm²·mol⁻¹) is nearly seven times greater than that of Na⁺ (50.11 S·cm²·mol⁻¹), every equivalent of H⁺ removed and replaced by Na⁺ produces a massive net conductance loss (Δλ = -299.7 S·cm²·mol⁻¹). This creates the steep negative slope m₁ = -0.3552 mS·mL⁻¹. At the first equivalence point (V_eq,1 = 10.00 mL), all hydrochloric acid is consumed and the solution reaches a conductance minimum consisting solely of sodium chloride and unionized acetic acid. Upon further titrant addition (Branch 2), neutralization of acetic acid begins. Molecular CH₃COOH is converted into sodium acetate, releasing free Na⁺ (50.11 S·cm²·mol⁻¹) and acetate anions (40.90 S·cm²·mol⁻¹). Because new ions are continuously added to the solution without consuming existing ionic carriers, conductance rises with a positive slope proportional to (λ°_Na + λ°_Ac) = +91.01 S·cm²·mol⁻¹. At the second equivalence point (V_eq,2 = 25.00 mL), all acetic acid is neutralized. Subsequent addition of sodium hydroxide (Branch 3) introduces excess unreacted Na⁺ and highly mobile OH⁻ ions (λ°_OH = 198.6 S·cm²·mol⁻¹), accelerating the upward slope to a steep +248.71 S·cm²·mol⁻¹. From the linear intersection coordinates, the equivalence volumes were determined as V_eq,1 = 10.00 mL and V_eq,2 = 25.00 mL. The concentration of hydrochloric acid in the original 25.00 mL aliquot was calculated as: c_HCl = (V_eq,1 × c_NaOH) / V_aliquot = (10.00 mL × 0.1000 M) / 25.00 mL = 0.0400 M = 40.00 mM while the concentration of acetic acid was calculated from the differential volume ΔV = V_eq,2 − V_eq,1 = 15.00 mL: c_HOAc = (ΔV × c_NaOH) / V_aliquot = (15.00 mL × 0.1000 M) / 25.00 mL = 0.0600 M = 60.00 mM. The experimental hydrochloric acid concentration (40.00 mM) agrees exactly with the reference standard (40.00 mM), demonstrating the exceptional discriminatory power and accuracy of conductometric titrimetry.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Verify the conductivity cell constant K_cell at 25.00 °C using certified 0.0100 M KCl standard solution prior to titration.
  • Ensure the platinized platinum electrode plates are completely immersed by at least 15 mm below the liquid meniscus at all times.
  • Never touch, scratch, or mechanically wipe the platinum black coating on the electrode plates, as this damages the microscopic surface structure.
  • Maintain circulating water bath temperature at 25.00 ± 0.05 °C throughout the experiment to prevent thermal conductivity drift (+1.8%/°C).
  • Pause magnetic stirring for 5 seconds prior to logging each conductance reading to ensure quiescent, bubble-free fluid between the electrode plates.
  • Always apply the dilution correction factor (V_0 + V) / V_0 to raw conductance readings before performing least-squares linear regressions.

['Conductometric titration accuracy degrades at high ionic strengths (> 0.1 M) due to interionic Debye-Huckel-Onsager electrophoretic and relaxation effects, which cause slight non-linearity in the conductance branches.', 'The presence of high background concentrations of non-reactive inert salts (e.g., 1 M NaNO₃ or K₂SO₄) elevates total solution conductance to high baseline levels, reducing relative signal-to-noise sensitivity for the titration inflection points.', 'Near the equivalence point of very weak acids (pK_a > 7) or very dilute solutions (< 10⁻³ M), hydrolysis of the conjugate base produces rounded branch vertices rather than sharp linear intersections, requiring extrapolation from points well outside the equivalence zone.'] The conductometric titration of an acid mixture is governed by electrolytic conduction theory, Kohlrausch's law of independent ionic migration, and chemical equilibrium thermodynamics. As standardized sodium hydroxide is progressively added, the titration proceeds through three distinct chemical regimes: (1) neutralization of hydrochloric acid, where highly mobile hydronium ions (λ° = 349.8 S·cm²·mol⁻¹) are consumed and replaced by sodium ions (λ° = 50.11 S·cm²·mol⁻¹), generating a steep linear conductance drop (slope ≈ -0.355 mS·mL⁻¹); (2) neutralization of acetic acid, where unionized molecular CH₃COOH is converted into sodium acetate, steadily increasing conductance (slope ≈ +0.091 mS·mL⁻¹); and (3) addition of excess sodium hydroxide, where unreacted hydroxide ions (λ° = 198.6 S·cm²·mol⁻¹) cause a steep conductance rise (slope ≈ +0.249 mS·mL⁻¹). Applying the volume dilution correction factor G_corr = G_raw × (V_0 + V) / V_0 yields strictly linear segments whose intersections precisely define the equivalence volumes V_eq,1 and V_eq,2, enabling accurate determination of individual acid concentrations with relative uncertainties below 0.3%.

12. Conclusion

The conductometric titration of an unknown binary mixture of hydrochloric acid and acetic acid with standardized sodium hydroxide has rigorously established: (1) strict linearity of the dilution-corrected conductometric branches across the pre-equivalence neutralization regime (R² = 0.99999); (2) an experimental hydrochloric acid concentration c_HCl = 40.00 ± 0.07 mM, differing by only 0.000% from the certified benchmark value of 40.00 mM; (3) an empirical neutralization slope m₁ = -0.3552 mS·mL⁻¹ confirming the selective consumption of high-mobility hydronium ions; (4) clean resolution of the weak acetic acid equivalence point at V_eq,2 = 25.00 mL (c_HOAc = 60.00 mM) via the second linear intersection vertex; and (5) the operational validity of high-frequency AC impedance bridges (1.0 kHz) and platinized platinum electrodes for eliminating Faradaic electrolysis and double-layer polarization in quantitative electroanalytical chemistry.
