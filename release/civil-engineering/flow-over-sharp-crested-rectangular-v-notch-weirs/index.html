FOLLOW THE METHOD

A reason behind every step.

Determine the discharge coefficient C_d and head-discharge relationships for sharp-crested rectangular and 90° triangular V-notch weirs in an open laboratory flume according to ISO 1438 / ASTM D5242. Standardized parameter settings: 0.05, 0.09, 0.13, 0.17, 0.21, 0.25 m^(2.5).

01

Flume Leveling & Zero Datum Establishment

Set flume bed horizontal using digital inclinometer; fill flume until water crest touches the exact vertex of the V-notch; record hook gauge zero reading H_0.

02

Pump Activation & Upstream Baffle Stabilization

Start recirculation pump; adjust inlet control valve to establish initial steady flow; allow 5 minutes for stilling chamber surface waves to damp out.

03

Upstream Head Measurement via Point Gauge

Position hook gauge at a distance 4 to 5 times maximum head upstream of weir plate; lower point until tip touches the water surface without puncturing the meniscus; log reading H_total; compute head H = H_total - H_0.

04

Volumetric Flow Rate Measurement

Divert flume outflow into calibrated volumetric measuring tank; record time required to collect a known volume (e.g. 200 L) with digital stopwatch to determine discharge Q.

05

Systematic Multi-Head Flow Acquisition

Increment control valve across six systematic head levels (H^(5/2) = 0.05, 0.09, 0.13, 0.17, 0.21, and 0.25 m^2.5); measure steady-state head and discharge for each step.

06

Nappe Aeration Inspection & Rating Curve Plotting

Verify continuous air envelope beneath nappe; compute transformed coordinate H^(5/2); plot Q versus H^(5/2) and determine empirical discharge multiplier.

THEORY AND STRUCTURAL CONTEXT

Civil engineering background & physical principles.

In open channel hydraulic engineering, sharp-crested weirs are fundamental obstruction structures utilized for precise discharge measurement, flood routing, irrigation water allotment, and storm runoff control. When water spills over an aerated sharp-crested plate, it detaches cleanly to form a free-falling jet known as a nappe. For a 90° triangular V-notch weir, the integration of elementary horizontal strips across the triangular geometry yields the theoretical discharge relation Q_th = (8/15) · tan(θ/2) · √(2g) · H^(5/2), where θ is the notch angle (90°), g is gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s²), and H is upstream head above the vertex. Viscous boundary drag, surface tension, and contraction of streamlines reduce actual discharge relative to theoretical flow, necessitating a calibrated discharge coefficient C_d: Q = C_d · (8/15) · √(2g) · H^(5/2). For standard 90° brass notches, C_d typically ranges between 0.58 and 0.62, giving an empirical coefficient multiplier of approximately 1.38 m^(0.5)/s. Maintaining full aeration under the falling nappe is mandatory; insufficient venting creates an under-nappe partial vacuum that depresses pressure, sucks the jet downward, and induces catastrophic flow measurement errors up to 25%. In this laboratory flume experiment, water head H is accurately monitored with a vernier hook-and-point gauge at systematic discharge rates to establish the head-discharge rating curve and calibrate the weir discharge coefficient.
