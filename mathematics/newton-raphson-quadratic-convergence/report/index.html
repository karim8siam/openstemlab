4. Theory and graph choice

The Newton-Raphson method is widely regarded as the cornerstone of modern numerical root-finding and continuous optimization. Originally developed by Sir Isaac Newton in his 1669 treatise 'De analysi per aequationes numero terminorum infinitas' (applied to polynomial equations) and refined into its modern derivative-based algebraic form by Joseph Raphson in 1690 ('Analysis aequationum universalis'), the algorithm replaces a nonlinear function with its first-order Taylor series approximation (tangent line) at each iteration. Geometrically, the method evaluates the tangent line to the curve y = f(x) at the point (x_k, f(x_k)) and traces it down to its intersection with the horizontal X-axis, which becomes the next approximation x_{k+1} = x_k − f(x_k)/f'(x_k). The mathematical brilliance of the Newton-Raphson method lies in its local quadratic convergence (order α = 2): once an iterate enters the asymptotic neighborhood of a simple root, the error at each step is approximately proportional to the square of the preceding error (ε_{k+1} ≈ C · ε_k²). Consequently, the number of correct significant decimal digits doubles with every single iteration—advancing from 2 digits to 4, 8, 16, and 32 digits in four rapid steps. This superfast convergence makes Newton's method the algorithm of choice for high-speed hardware square-root circuits, non-linear finite element solvers, orbital trajectory calculations, and multidimensional optimization (where it generalizes to Newton's method with Hessian matrices). In this laboratory investigation, students evaluate the Newton-Raphson algorithm applied to the benchmark equation f(x) = x² − 5 = 0 (Heron's square root of 5). By computing successive iterates, tracking error reduction, linearizing the logarithmic recurrence ln(ε_{k+1}) = α · ln(ε_k) + ln(C), and performing ordinary least-squares regression, students prove that α = 2.000 with sub-percent accuracy and extract the theoretical asymptotic error constant C = 1/(2√5) ≈ 0.2236.

\ln(\varepsilon_{k+1}) = \alpha \cdot \ln(\varepsilon_k) + \ln(C) \implies \alpha = 2.000,\ C = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{5}} \approx 0.2236

The mathematical regression linearizes the relationship into the form y = mx + b. Here, the independent variable x represents Natural Log of Current Error ln(ε_k), and the dependent variable y represents Natural Log of Next Error ln(ε_{k+1}). The slope m directly extracts Order of Convergence α, while the intercept b accounts for residual baseline offsets.

5. Method

  1. Select the quadratic equation f(x) = x² − 5 = 0 whose positive root is x* = √5 ≈ 2.2360679775. Choose initial guess x₀ = 3.000. Calculate initial error ε₀ = |x₀ − x*| = |3.000 − 2.236068| = 0.763932.
  2. Evaluate f(x₀) = 3² − 5 = 4.000 and f'(x₀) = 2(3) = 6.000. Compute tangent intercept: x₁ = x₀ − f(x₀)/f'(x₀) = 3.0 − 4.0/6.0 = 2.333333. Compute error ε₁ = |2.333333 − 2.236068| = 0.097265.
  3. Evaluate f(x₁) = (7/3)² − 5 = 49/9 − 5 = 4/9 ≈ 0.444444 and f'(x₁) = 14/3 ≈ 4.666667. Compute x₂ = 2.333333 − (4/9)/(14/3) = 2.333333 − 2/21 = 47/21 ≈ 2.238095. Error: ε₂ = |2.238095 − 2.236068| = 0.002027.
  4. Apply recurrence: x₃ = 0.5 · (47/21 + 5·21/47) = 2207/987 ≈ 2.2360688956, with error ε₃ = 9.18 × 10⁻⁷; at k = 4, x₄ = 2.23606797749979, with error ε₄ = 1.88 × 10⁻¹³ (full 16 digits of machine precision achieved).
  5. Calculate the natural logarithm of consecutive errors: for (ε₀, ε₁), (ε₁, ε₂), (ε₂, ε₃), evaluate x = ln(ε_k) and y = ln(ε_{k+1}). For example: ln(ε₀) = −0.2693, ln(ε₁) = −2.3304; ln(ε₁) = −2.3304, ln(ε₂) = −6.2011; ln(ε₂) = −6.2011, ln(ε₃) = −13.9015.
  6. Perform ordinary least-squares regression of y = ln(ε_{k+1}) against x = ln(ε_k). Extract the regression slope α = 2.00000 and intercept ln(C) = −1.49787. Verify that α = 2.000 identically, proving quadratic convergence with asymptotic constant C = e^(−1.49787) ≈ 0.2236 = 1/(2√5).

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Ensure the initial estimate x₀ is chosen sufficiently close to the desired root; starting far away can cause divergence or convergence to an unexpected root.
  • Always check that f'(x_k) ≠ 0 prior to division to prevent floating-point division by zero (+Inf or NaN exceptions).
  • Monitor the denominator magnitude; if |f'(x_k)| is extremely small (near-zero slope), the step Δx = −f/f' can be excessively large, projecting the iterate outside the domain of interest.
  • Use hybrid safeguarding (such as the Brent-Dekker method): if a Newton step falls outside the known bracketing interval, reject it and fall back to a bisection step.
  • For multiple roots (f'(x*) = 0), apply Schroeder's accelerated modification x_{k+1} = x_k − m · f/f' to prevent convergence degradation from quadratic to linear.
  • Maintain full 64-bit IEEE 754 precision; quadratic convergence doubles digits rapidly, reaching machine epsilon (10⁻¹⁶) within 4 to 5 iterations.

Newton's method requires the analytical or computational derivative f'(x), which may be expensive, noisy, or impossible to evaluate in black-box models.
