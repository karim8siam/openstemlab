Biomedical and physiological foundations of FES Biphasic Charge Balance.

Functional Electrical Stimulation (FES) and neuroprosthetics restore motor function (such as foot-drop correction during hemiplegic gait or hand grasp in quadriplegia) by applying controlled electrical current pulses to intact peripheral motor nerves, activating muscle contractions in paralyzed limbs. When electrical current passes through metal electrodes into tissue electrolytes, charge injection occurs via two distinct mechanisms: reversible non-Faradaic electrical double-layer capacitive charging, and Faradaic reduction-oxidation (redox) chemical reactions. If stimulating pulses leave any residual uncompensated DC charge (charge imbalance), Faradaic electrolysis of water produces toxic hydrogen ions, hydroxyl radicals, chlorine gas, and irreversible metallic electrode dissolution, causing severe tissue necrosis and neurotoxicity. To ensure long-term biocompatibility, neural stimulators universally deliver charge-balanced biphasic rectangular current pulses: a primary cathodic pulse depolarizes the axon, immediately followed by an anodic charge-reversal pulse of equal magnitude and duration to reverse Faradaic reactions. For a constant-current rectangular cathodic pulse of amplitude I_stim and duration t_pw, the total injected electrical charge Q_p per phase is governed by Q_p = ∫ I_stim dt = I_stim · t_pw = t_pw · I_stim, where t_pw is the pulse width (expressed as 0.2500 μC/mA for a standard 250 μs duration). In this experiment, a clinical multichannel FES stimulator discharging into an electrochemical tissue phantom evaluates charge balance and injected charge Q_p across currents from 5 to 30 mA.

Q_p = t_pw · I_stim = 0.2500 · I_stim (where t_pw = 250 μs = 0.2500 ms)

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

Why is net zero DC charge balance essential in electrical stimulation of biological tissues?

Monopolar or charge-imbalanced currents cause irreversible Faradaic electrolysis at the metal-tissue interface: water oxidizes into oxygen and protons (acidifying tissue), water reduces into hydrogen and hydroxide ions (alkalizing tissue), and metal atoms dissolve into toxic cations, causing electrode breakdown and neuronal death.

What is the Shannon criterion for neural damage in electrical stimulation?

The Shannon equation log(D) = k - log(Q) defines the safe boundary for neural stimulation, where D is charge density per phase (μC/cm²), Q is charge per phase (μC), and k is an empirical safety limit (typically k = 1.75 to 1.85); exceeding this limit risks permanent axonal injury.

What is the difference between capacitive and Faradaic charge injection?

Capacitive charge injection involves reversible electrostatic alignment of ions in the aqueous double-layer without chemical electron transfer across the interface (completely safe); Faradaic charge injection involves actual electron transfer through redox chemical reactions, which can be reversible (e.g., platinum oxide reduction) or irreversible (water hydrolysis).

Why is an interphase gap (typically 50 to 100 μs) introduced between cathodic and anodic pulses?

The cathodic pulse depolarizes the axon; if the anodic reversing pulse follows immediately with zero delay, it can hyperpolarize the membrane prematurely, prematurely extinguishing the nascent action potential before it can propagate down the nerve.

Why is the cathodic phase generally used as the primary activating pulse in neurostimulation?

A cathodic (negative) electrode deposits negative charges extracellularly, creating a local outward transmembrane current that depolarizes the axolemma toward action potential threshold at much lower current amplitudes than an anodic pulse.

What is passive charge balancing versus active charge balancing?

Active charge balancing uses a controlled-current circuit to drive an equal and opposite anodic current pulse; passive charge balancing places a DC-blocking capacitor in series with the electrode and shorts the electrode to ground during the interpulse interval through a switch, discharging any residual accumulated charge.

Experimental precautions and patient safety

  • Always verify that net DC current is zero (< 1 μA); continuous DC leakage causes rapid tissue chemical burns.
  • Ensure electrode charge density does not exceed the water hydrolysis electrochemical potential window (-0.6 V to +0.8 V vs. Ag/AgCl).
  • Maintain firm, uniform electrode contact to avoid localized current crowding and superficial skin burns.
  • Calibrate the current-sensing resistor accurately to ensure precise phase charge integration.

Theoretical assumptions & biological limits

  • The simple Q = I · t relationship assumes a perfect rectangular current pulse; real inductive leads introduce finite rise and fall times.
  • Evaluates static RC phantom, whereas human skin exhibits dynamic, non-linear impedance that decreases with increasing current amplitude.
  • Tested on superficial transcutaneous parameters; deep brain stimulation (DBS) or cochlear implants operate at much lower currents (< 5 mA) and smaller electrode areas.
  • Does not model selective fiber recruitment (large-diameter sensory Aβ fibers vs. motor Aα fibers) in living nerves.
