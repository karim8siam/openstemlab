Flywheel moment of inertia and rotational dynamics theory

01Rotational Dynamics and Newton's Second Law for Angular Motion

Rotational dynamics describes the behavior of extended bodies rotating about fixed or moving axes under the influence of torques. In direct analogy to Newton's Second Law for translational motion (F_net = dp/dt), Newton's Second Law for rotation establishes that the net external torque acting on a rigid body equals the time rate of change of its angular momentum: τ_net = dL/dt. For a rigid body rotating about a fixed principal symmetry axis with moment of inertia I, the angular momentum vector is collinear with the angular velocity vector: L = I ω. Because the mass distribution remains rigid and invariant during motion (dI/dt = 0), differentiating the angular momentum product yields the fundamental rotational equation of motion: τ_net = I (dω/dt) = I · α, where α = d²θ/dt² is the angular acceleration expressed in radians per second squared. This governing equation demonstrates that torque τ is the rotational analogue of force, moment of inertia I is the rotational analogue of mass, and angular acceleration α is the rotational analogue of linear acceleration. In SI units, torque has units of Newton-meters (N m), moment of inertia has units of kilogram-meters squared (kg m²), and angular acceleration has units of radians per second squared (rad s⁻²).

\vec{\tau}_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec{L}}{dt} = I \frac{d\vec{\omega}}{dt} = I \vec{\alpha} \implies \alpha = \frac{\tau_{\text{net}}}{I}
What is the physical significance of torque in rotational mechanics compared to force in translational mechanics?

Force produces linear acceleration of the center of mass, whereas torque (the cross product of position vector and force, τ = r × F) produces angular acceleration about an axis of rotation.

02Moment of Inertia as the Rotational Analogue of Mass

In translational mechanics, a single scalar quantity—inertial mass m—quantifies a body's inertia regardless of its orientation or geometric shape. In rotational mechanics, however, the rotational inertia depends not only on the total mass M, but crucially on the spatial distribution of mass relative to the rotation axis. The moment of inertia of a discrete system of point particles is defined as I = ∑ m_i r_i², where r_i is the perpendicular distance from particle i to the axis of rotation. For a continuous rigid body with mass density ρ(r), the sum transitions to a volume integral: I = ∫ r_perp² dm = ∫ r_perp² ρ dV. Because the mass element is multiplied by the square of its distance from the axis (r_perp²), mass situated far from the axis contributes vastly more rotational inertia than mass near the center. For a solid uniform cylindrical disk of mass M and outer radius R rotating about its central axis, integration yields I_disk = 1/2 M R². If the same mass M is concentrated in a thin outer circular hoop of radius R, its moment of inertia doubles to I_hoop = M R². For a thick annular ring or flywheel with inner radius R₁ and outer radius R₂, the moment of inertia is I = 1/2 M (R₁² + R₂²). Precision flywheels are intentionally designed with heavy outer rims and lightweight spoked webs to maximize moment of inertia for a given total mass.

I = \sum m_i r_i^2 = \int r_{\perp}^2 dm, \quad I_{\text{disk}} = \frac{1}{2} M R^2, \quad I_{\text{ring}} = \frac{1}{2} M \left(R_1^2 + R_2^2\right)
Why does a hollow cylinder (hoop) have a greater moment of inertia than a solid cylinder of the same mass and outer radius?

Because in the hollow cylinder, all of the mass is situated at the maximum radius R from the axis, whereas in the solid cylinder, much of the mass is distributed closer to the center where r_perp < R.

03Kinematics of Uniformly Accelerated Rotation (α, ω, θ)

When a constant net torque τ acts on a flywheel of constant moment of inertia I, the resulting angular acceleration α = τ / I is strictly uniform. The kinematic equations describing this uniformly accelerated angular motion are exact mathematical duplicates of the linear kinematic equations of rectilinear motion, with linear displacement x replaced by angular position θ (in radians), linear velocity v replaced by angular velocity ω (in radians per second), and linear acceleration a replaced by angular acceleration α (in rad s⁻²). Integrating the constant acceleration equation d²θ/dt² = α once with respect to time yields the instantaneous angular velocity: ω(t) = ω₀ + α t. Integrating a second time yields the angular displacement: θ(t) = θ₀ + ω₀ t + 1/2 α t². When the flywheel is released from rest (ω₀ = 0) at θ₀ = 0, the equations simplify to ω = α t and θ = 1/2 α t². Eliminating time between the velocity and displacement relations yields the rotational Torricellian equation: ω² = 2 α θ. In our experimental apparatus, as the hanging mass falls through height h, the cord unwinds an arc length s = r · θ = h from the drum, coupling linear fall kinematics to rotational kinematics: a_linear = r · α, v_linear = r · ω.

\omega(t) = \omega_0 + \alpha t, \quad \theta(t) = \omega_0 t + \frac{1}{2} \alpha t^2, \quad \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2 \alpha \theta \quad (\text{where } a = r \alpha)
If a flywheel starts from rest and undergoes uniform angular acceleration α for time t, how is its final rotational kinetic energy related to its angular acceleration?

Because ω = α t, the rotational kinetic energy is K_rot = 1/2 I ω² = 1/2 I (α t)² = 1/2 I α² t², showing that kinetic energy grows quadratically with both acceleration and elapsed time.

04Linearization of the Torque-Acceleration Governing Equation

The dynamic equation governing the flywheel, α = (1 / I) · τ, represents an intrinsically linear relationship between angular acceleration α and applied torque τ. Plotting α on the vertical axis against τ on the horizontal axis yields the linear equation Y = S · X + b, where Y = α (rad s⁻²), X = τ (N m), theoretical slope S = 1 / I (kg⁻¹ m⁻²), and theoretical intercept b = 0. When an ordinary least-squares regression is performed across a series of experimental trials {(τ_i, α_i)}, the best-fit slope S minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals: S = [N ∑(τ_i α_i) - ∑(τ_i) ∑(α_i)] / [N ∑(τ_i²) - (∑ τ_i)²]. Inverting the fitted slope directly yields the experimental moment of inertia: I = 1 / S. The standard error in the slope SE(S) propagates into the moment of inertia uncertainty via SE(I) = SE(S) / S² = I² · SE(S). Furthermore, the fitted intercept b serves as an indispensable diagnostic tool: if the bearings exhibit uncompensated friction torque τ_friction, the equation becomes α = (1 / I) (τ - τ_friction) = (1 / I) τ - (τ_friction / I), producing a negative intercept b = -τ_friction / I. The magnitude of this negative intercept allows the friction torque to be quantified directly as τ_friction = |b| · I.

Y = S \cdot X + b \quad \text{where } Y = \alpha, \; X = \tau, \; S = \frac{1}{I}, \; b = 0 \implies I = \frac{1}{S}
If an experimental α versus τ plot yields a straight line with slope S = 20.0 kg⁻¹ m⁻² and a negative intercept b = -0.10 rad s⁻², what is the bearing friction torque?

The moment of inertia is I = 1 / S = 1 / 20.0 = 0.050 kg m². The bearing friction torque equals τ_friction = |b| · I = 0.10 × 0.050 = 0.005 N m.

05Bearing Friction Compensation and Energy Loss Determination

In any physical flywheel apparatus, mechanical friction opposes rotation. Friction originates primarily from rolling resistance within the ball bearings, viscous shear of the bearing lubricant, and aerodynamic air resistance (windage) acting on the rotating faces of the disk. In precision experiments, friction can be evaluated and compensated using the method of energy conservation during coast-down. When a weight of mass m falls through height h, the loss of gravitational potential energy is m g h. At the instant the weight reaches the floor, it has linear velocity v = r ω, possessing translational kinetic energy 1/2 m v², while the flywheel possesses rotational kinetic energy 1/2 I ω². If the cord remains attached, the wheel's inertia carries the weight back upward against gravity to a rebound height h₁. The loss of mechanical energy between the initial height h and the rebound height h₁ equals the total friction work done over the round trip: W_friction = m g (h - h₁) = f_friction · (h + h₁), where f_friction is the equivalent retarding force. Alternatively, by measuring the coast-down deceleration α_coast when no driving torque is applied, the friction torque is evaluated directly as τ_friction = I · |α_coast|.

W_{\text{friction}} = m g (h - h_1) = \tau_{\text{friction}} \cdot \theta_{\text{total}}, \quad \tau_{\text{net}} = \tau_{\text{applied}} - \tau_{\text{friction}}
Why does a flywheel continue rotating for many minutes after the accelerating weight has detached?

Because the flywheel has a large moment of inertia I and stores substantial rotational kinetic energy (1/2 I ω²), while high-precision ball bearings exert a very small frictional torque τ_friction, resulting in a tiny deceleration rate α = τ_friction / I.

06Effective Linear Inertia of the Falling Mass ($m r^2$ Correction)

A rigorous derivation of the accelerating torque must account for the fact that the falling mass m is itself accelerated linearly by gravity. Applying Newton's Second Law to the descending mass m yields m g - T_cord = m a. The torque exerted on the axle drum of radius r is τ = T_cord · r = (m g - m a) · r. Because the linear acceleration of the cord equals the tangential acceleration of the drum surface, a = r · α, the torque equation becomes τ = m g r - m r² α. Substituting this into the rotational equation of motion τ = I α gives m g r - m r² α = I α. Rearranging terms yields m g r = (I + m r²) α. This demonstrates that the hanging mass contributes an additional equivalent rotational inertia equal to m r². For a typical hanging mass m = 0.200 kg and drum radius r = 0.020 m (20.0 mm), this correction equals m r² = 0.200 × (0.020)² = 0.00008 kg m² (8.0 × 10⁻⁵ kg m²). Comparing this to the flywheel's moment of inertia I = 0.0500 kg m² shows that the m r² term represents an effect of only 0.16%, which is well within standard experimental error but provides an instructive advanced correction in university metrology.

m g r = \left(I + m r^2\right) \alpha \implies \alpha = \frac{m g r}{I + m r^2} \approx \frac{\tau_{\text{applied}}}{I} \quad (\text{since } m r^2 \ll I)
Under what experimental conditions would the m r² correction term become significant in a flywheel experiment?

The correction becomes significant if the drum radius r is large (e.g., r > 0.1 m) or if a very heavy accelerating mass m is used with a relatively lightweight flywheel.

07Industrial Flywheels: Kinetic Energy Storage and Rotational Power Smoothing

Flywheels have played a central role in mechanical power transmission since the dawn of the Industrial Revolution. In reciprocating internal combustion engines and steam engines, power is delivered in discrete, periodic pulses during the combustion or expansion strokes, while the remaining strokes (compression, intake, exhaust) consume energy. Without a flywheel, the engine's crankshaft would experience violent velocity fluctuations and stalling. A massive flywheel mounted on the crankshaft absorbs surplus kinetic energy during the power stroke (increasing its speed slightly: ΔK = 1/2 I (ω_max² - ω_min²)) and releases this stored energy during the idle strokes, smoothing the shaft rotation. In modern energy technology, advanced carbon-fiber flywheels operating in vacuum magnetic bearings serve as electromechanical batteries (Flywheel Energy Storage Systems, FESS), spinning at up to 60,000 RPM. These modern systems achieve energy efficiencies exceeding 90% and specific energies over 100 Wh/kg, providing grid frequency regulation, uninterruptible power supply (UPS) for data centers, and kinetic energy recovery systems (KERS) in high-performance electric vehicles.

E_{\text{stored}} = \frac{1}{2} I \omega^2, \quad \Delta E = \frac{1}{2} I \left(\omega_{\max}^2 - \omega_{\min}^2\right) = I \omega_{\text{mean}} \Delta\omega
Why are modern high-speed kinetic storage flywheels fabricated from carbon-fiber composites rather than high-strength steel?

Because the maximum kinetic energy stored per unit mass is limited by the material's specific tensile strength (tensile strength divided by density, σ / ρ). Carbon-fiber composites have vastly higher specific strength than steel, enabling them to withstand immense centrifugal hoop stresses at high angular velocities.
