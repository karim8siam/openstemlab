Combine Cleaning Shoe Aerodynamic Airflow & Grain-Chaff Separation. Measure the aerodynamic velocity distribution and chaff winnowing separation efficiency across a reciprocating combine cleaning shoe sieve under variable cleaning fan speeds.

4. Theory and graph choice

The cleaning shoe of a modern combine harvester performs the final critical separation of clean grain from chaff, short straw, weed seeds, and broken husks (Material Other than Grain, or MOG). The cleaning system operates through a combined aerodynamic and mechanical mechanism consisting of a centrifugal cross-flow cleaning fan, a reciprocating chaffer sieve, an adjustable lower cleaning sieve, and grain collection augers. As the threshed mixture drops from the grain pan onto the chaffer, the upward and rearward directed airflow fluidizes the mat. Because the terminal velocity of cereal grain (typically 8.0 to 10.5 m/s for wheat) is substantially higher than the terminal velocity of chaff and light straw (1.5 to 3.5 m/s), an intermediate air velocity (3.0 to 7.0 m/s) floats and suspends the chaff, blowing it out the rear of the machine, while the heavier grain kernels fall downward through the adjustable louvers onto the clean grain auger. If fan air velocity U is too low, chaff blankets the sieve louvers and grain cannot penetrate, causing sieve overload and heavy shoe loss. If air velocity exceeds the grain terminal threshold, clean grain is blown out the rear into the field. In this laboratory investigation, an instrumented full-scale cleaning shoe test rig with hot-wire anemometer arrays measures air velocity and chaff winnowing removal.

R_c = k_c · U = 14.5000 · U

The governing physical relationship was linearized into the standard regression coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent axis x represents Cleaning air velocity U (m/s), and the dependent axis y represents Chaff removal efficiency R_c (%). The gradient m directly determines Chaff Separation Gradient k_c, while the intercept b accounts for zero-load offsets, baseline tares, or reference thresholds. Linear transformation allows robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear solver instability or convergence failure. 5. Method

  1. Set the chaffer sieve louvers to 12 mm opening and lower cleaning sieve to 8 mm opening, aligning the fan windboard deflectors toward the front third of the chaffer.
  2. Engage the cleaning fan and traverse the hot-wire anemometer array across the sieve grid to establish average baseline face air velocity U = 3.0 m/s.
  3. Blend a standardized 15.0 kg test batch containing 10.5 kg clean wheat and 4.5 kg wheat chaff (70:30 grain-to-chaff ratio).
  4. Engage sieve reciprocating shake (300 cycles/min) and feed the mixture onto the pre-cleaner pan over 30 seconds.
  5. Weigh clean grain collected in the bottom hopper (m_clean), chaff captured in the rear cyclone (m_chaff_out), and residual chaff on the sieve to calculate chaff removal efficiency R_c = (m_chaff_out / m_chaff_total) × 100%.
  6. Increase fan speed across velocities of 3.8, 4.6, 5.4, 6.2, and 7.0 m/s, recording chaff removal R_c and monitoring grain blown-over loss.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Ensure anemometer probes are clean and protected from dust when operating with dry agricultural chaff.
  • Verify that reciprocating sieve drive eccentric bearings are properly lubricated to avoid excessive frame vibration.
  • Check that sieve louvers open uniformly across the full width without bent or binding slats.
  • Avoid operating fan at velocities > 7.5 m/s to prevent blowing sound grain out the rear.

Laboratory stand uses clean ambient air; field combines experience engine heat and ambient crosswinds that alter air density. Evaluated using a standardized mixture of 70% wheat grain and 30% wheat chaff (by weight) at chaffer sieve louver opening of 12 mm and stroke frequency of 300 cycles/min.
