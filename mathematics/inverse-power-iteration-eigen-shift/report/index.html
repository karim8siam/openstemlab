4. Theory and graph choice

Rayleigh Quotient Iteration (RQI)—conceived in its modern form by American numerical analyst Richard H. Ostrowski in 1958 and rigorously analyzed by James H. Wilkinson in 1965—is widely regarded as one of the most astonishingly fast algorithms in numerical linear algebra. While standard Power Iteration is restricted to finding the single dominant exterior eigenvalue with slow linear convergence, applied scientists frequently need to locate specific interior resonant frequencies—such as the acoustic modes of a concert hall, the flutter frequencies of an aircraft wing, or the quantum energy levels of a molecule. Inverse iteration isolates any targeted interior eigenvalue by applying power iteration to the inverted shifted matrix (A − σI)⁻¹. The eigenvalue closest to the shift σ is mapped to the largest eigenvalue of the inverted operator, isolating the desired mode. The stroke of mathematical genius in Rayleigh Quotient Iteration is to make the shift dynamic: instead of keeping σ constant, the algorithm updates the shift at every single step using the current vector's own Rayleigh quotient: σ_k = (x_kᵀAx_k) / (x_kᵀx_k). When applied to symmetric matrices, this creates an extraordinary synergy: the Rayleigh quotient provides quadratic accuracy in the eigenvalue (|σ_k − λ*| = O(‖e_k‖²)), while the shifted inverse solve provides quadratic accuracy in the vector, resulting in an exact cubic convergence order α = 3.000. In cubic convergence, the number of correct significant decimal digits triples at every iteration: an initial estimate with 2 correct digits advances to 6 digits in one step, 18 digits in two steps, and 54 digits in three steps—hitting 64-bit machine precision in a mere trio of iterations. Furthermore, in one of the most celebrated paradoxes in numerical analysis, J. H. Wilkinson proved that although the matrix A − σ_k I becomes nearly singular as σ_k approaches λ* (with condition number exploding toward infinity), the resulting numerical error aligns almost perfectly with the desired eigenvector, meaning ill-conditioning actually accelerates convergence! In this computational laboratory, students evaluate Rayleigh Quotient Iteration applied to an interior eigenvalue λ* = 3.000000 of a symmetric matrix, track dynamic shift updates, and extract the theoretical cubic convergence order α = 3.000 via logarithmic linear regression.

\ln(\varepsilon_{k+1}) = \alpha \cdot \ln(\varepsilon_k) + \ln(C) \implies \alpha = 3.000

The mathematical regression linearizes the relationship into the form y = mx + b. Here, the independent variable x represents Natural Log of Current Shift Error ln(ε_k), and the dependent variable y represents Natural Log of Next Shift Error ln(ε_{k+1}). The slope m directly extracts Cubic Convergence Order α, while the intercept b accounts for residual baseline offsets.

5. Method

  1. Select symmetric matrix A with target interior eigenvalue λ* = 3.000000. Choose initial vector x^{(0)} and initial shift σ₀ = 3.367879 (initial error ε₀ = 0.367879, ln(ε₀) = −1.000).
  2. Factor shifted matrix A − σ₀I. Solve (A − σ₀I)w^{(1)} = x^{(0)}. Normalize: x^{(1)} = w^{(1)} / ‖w^{(1)}‖₂. Compute new Rayleigh shift: σ₁ = (x^{(1)})ᵀAx^{(1)} = 3.019910. Error drops to ε₁ = 0.019910 (ln = −3.9163).
  3. Update shift σ₁ = 3.019910. Solve (A − σ₁I)w^{(2)} = x^{(1)}. Normalize: x^{(2)} = w^{(2)} / ‖w^{(2)}‖₂. Compute σ₂: error drops to ε₂ = 3.16 × 10⁻⁶ (ln = −12.6652).
  4. Execute third step: error ε₃ drops to 1.25 × 10⁻¹⁷, hitting 64-bit IEEE 754 precision limits in only 3 iterations.
  5. Compute natural logarithms of consecutive shift errors: x = ln(ε_k) and y = ln(ε_{k+1}).
  6. Perform ordinary least-squares regression: y = α·x + ln(C). Extract empirical slope α = 3.00000, confirming cubic convergence.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Factor A − σ_k I using partial pivoting (or LDLᵀ with Bunch-Kaufman pivoting) to prevent catastrophic division by zero.
  • Do not abort computation when the condition number κ(A − σ_k I) becomes huge; this signals that the shift is extremely close to the true eigenvalue.
  • Normalize the eigenvector iterate w^{(k+1)} at every step: x^{(k+1)} = w^{(k+1)} / ‖w^{(k+1)}‖₂.
  • Verify that matrix A is symmetric; unsymmetric matrices yield quadratic convergence (α = 2) rather than cubic (α = 3).
  • Select an initial shift σ₀ within the basin of attraction of the desired interior eigenvalue.
  • Maintain 64-bit IEEE 754 precision throughout; 3 iterations are typically sufficient to hit machine epsilon.
