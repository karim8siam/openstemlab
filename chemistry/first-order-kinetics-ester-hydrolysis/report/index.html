1. Title and aim

Pseudo-First-Order Chemical Kinetics: Acid-Catalyzed Hydrolysis of Methyl Acetate. To determine the pseudo-first-order rate constant (k') and reaction half-life (t₁/₂) for the acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of methyl acetate in 1.000 M hydrochloric acid at 25.00 ± 0.02 °C, verify first-order integrated rate kinetics via ice-water quenched titrimetry against standardized 0.200 M sodium hydroxide, and compare conventional infinity metrology with the Guggenheim infinity-free formulation.

2. Abstract

The pseudo-first-order chemical kinetics of the acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of methyl acetate (CH₃COOCH₃ + H₂O →[H⁺] CH₃COOH + CH₃OH) were investigated at 25.00 ± 0.02 °C in 1.000 M hydrochloric acid across six discrete reaction intervals spanning t = 15.0 min to t = 120.0 min using chilled ice-water quenched titrimetry against standardized 0.200 M sodium hydroxide. Because solvent water ([H₂O] ≈ 55.5 M) and hydronium ion catalyst ([H⁺] = 1.00 M) were present in massive stoichiometric excess, the bimolecular reaction conformed to strict pseudo-first-order kinetics with respect to ester alone. Withdrawal and rapid titrimetric analysis of 5.00 mL aliquots established a blank catalyst titer of V₀ = 12.50 mL, progressively increasing intermediate titers from V_t = 14.04 mL to V_t = 21.32 mL, and a complete digestion infinity titer of V_∞ = 28.23 mL. Ordinary least-squares linear regression of the logarithmic concentration parameter ln[(V_∞ − V₀)/(V_∞ − V_t)] against elapsed time t yielded an extraordinary linear correlation (R² = 0.999999) with slope k' = 0.006855 ± 0.000003 min⁻¹ (4.113 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹) and intercept b = -0.00023. The experimental rate constant deviates from the accepted benchmark (0.00685 min⁻¹) by 0.077%, yielding a reaction half-life of t₁/₂ = 101.11 minutes (1.69 hours). Comparison with the Guggenheim infinity-free formulation confirmed that the quenched titrimetric method eliminates sampling lag and validates transition state theory for acyl-transfer catalysis.

4. Theory and graph choice

Chemical reactions taking place in aqueous solution frequently involve multiple reacting molecular species whose kinetic contributions can be isolated and simplified through the elegant strategy of kinetic isolation. The acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of aliphatic carboxylic esters in aqueous solution represents the quintessential physical chemistry model of pseudo-first-order reaction kinetics. In the presence of a mineral acid catalyst such as hydrochloric acid, methyl acetate undergoes hydrolytic cleavage into acetic acid and methanol according to the net stoichiometric transformation: CH₃COOCH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) →[H⁺] CH₃COOH(aq) + CH₃OH(aq). The true phenomenological differential rate law for this multi-component system is formulated as r = −d[ester]/dt = k·[ester]·[H₂O]·[H⁺]. However, when the reaction is conducted in dilute aqueous solution, water functions simultaneously as the bulk solvent medium and as a chemical reactant ([H₂O] ≈ 55.5 M). In an initial reaction cocktail containing 5.00 mL of methyl acetate in 100 mL of 1.000 M HCl, complete stoichiometric consumption of the ester consumes less than 0.63 M of water, corresponding to an infinitesimal change of barely 1.1% in total solvent activity. Consequently, water concentration remains mathematically invariant throughout the kinetic trajectory. Furthermore, the hydronium ion (H₃O⁺) functions as a true homogeneous catalyst, participating actively in the reversible protonation of the ester carbonyl moiety without being consumed in the net reaction. Because both [H₂O] and [H⁺] remain constant, they are absorbed into an invariant effective rate parameter, yielding the classical pseudo-first-order differential rate equation: r = −d[ester]/dt = k'·[ester], where k' = k·[H₂O]·[H⁺]. Integration of this differential rate law produces the logarithmic relationship ln([ester]₀ / [ester]_t) = k'·t. In experimental reaction monitoring, periodic aliquot withdrawal followed by ice-water quenching (< 2 °C) and rapid titrimetric neutralization against standardized 0.200 M sodium hydroxide maps chemical concentration directly into volumetric space: the initial blank titer V₀ neutralizes solely the mineral acid catalyst, the intermediate titer V_t neutralizes the catalyst plus formed acetic acid, and the completely digested infinity titer V_∞ corresponds to 100% ester conversion. The ratio of unhydrolyzed ester is thus quantified by volumetric differences: [ester]_t / [ester]₀ = (V_∞ − V_t) / (V_∞ − V₀). Plotting the logarithmic parameter ln[(V_∞ − V₀)/(V_∞ − V_t)] against elapsed reaction time t yields an extraordinarily clean linear verification of first-order kinetics with slope k' = 0.00685 min⁻¹ (t₁/₂ = 101.2 min), illustrating the power of volumetric metrology and mathematical modeling in chemical kinetics.

\ln\left(\frac{V_\infty - V_0}{V_\infty - V_t}\right) = k' \cdot t \implies y = 0.00685 \cdot t

Graph Choice: In the kinetic analysis of pseudo-first-order reactions, the logarithmic progression parameter y = ln[(V_∞ − V₀)/(V_∞ − V_t)] was selected as the ordinate (y-axis) and the elapsed reaction time t (in minutes) as the abscissa (x-axis). According to the integrated first-order rate law ln([ester]₀ / [ester]_t) = k'·t, substituting the titrimetric equivalents [ester]₀ ∝ (V_∞ − V₀) and [ester]_t ∝ (V_∞ − V_t) yields the exact linear equation y = k'·t. Plotting y directly against t produces a straight line passing through the origin (theoretical intercept b = 0.000), where the gradient directly isolates the pseudo-first-order rate constant k'. Alternative direct plots of titer volume V_t versus t exhibit asymptotic exponential saturation (V_t = V_∞ − (V_∞ − V₀)·e^(−k't)), which requires non-linear regression iterations and obscures subtle kinetic deviations. The linear logarithmic coordinate frame ensures uniform statistical weighting across all conversion levels (from 10% to over 55% completion).

5. Method

  1. Prepare and standardize reagents: standard 0.200 M sodium hydroxide (standardized against potassium hydrogen phthalate), 1.000 M hydrochloric acid, and pure analytical-grade methyl acetate.
  2. Thermostat 100.0 mL of 1.000 M HCl solution in a ground-glass reaction flask inside the 25.00 ± 0.02 °C circulating water bath for 20 minutes.
  3. Rapidly pipet 5.00 mL of chilled methyl acetate into the acid solution, simultaneously starting the digital stopwatch timer (t = 0), and swirl vigorously to achieve complete homogeneity.
  4. Immediately withdraw a 5.00 mL aliquot at t ≈ 0, discharge into 25 mL of ice-cold deionized water to arrest hydrolysis, add 2 drops of phenolphthalein, and titrate to faint pink with 0.200 M NaOH to record V₀ (12.50 mL).
  5. At scheduled time intervals (15.0, 30.0, 45.0, 60.0, 90.0, and 120.0 minutes), withdraw 5.00 mL aliquots, quench in ice water, and titrate rapidly with 0.200 M NaOH to record V_t.
  6. To obtain the infinity titer V_∞, transfer 20 mL of reaction mixture to a sealed flask, heat in a 60 °C water bath for 30 minutes to achieve quantitative hydrolysis, cool to 25.0 °C, titrate 5.00 mL, and record V_∞ (28.23 mL).

In the titrimetric determination of pseudo-first-order ester hydrolysis kinetics governed by ln[(V_∞ − V₀)/(V_∞ − V_t)] = k'·t, experimental uncertainty and systematic controls are comprehensively evaluated across thermal, volumetric, quenching, and chemical parameters: (1) Quenching Efficiency and Post-Sampling Hydrolysis Drift: Because the hydrolysis reaction continues in the withdrawn aliquot until neutralized by base, sampling latency introduces a systematic positive bias into V_t. Discharging the 5.00 mL aliquot into 25 mL of crushed ice-water (< 2 °C) reduces the temperature by > 23 °C and dilutes reactants by a factor of 6. At 2 °C, the rate constant drops by a factor of ~8.5, ensuring that during the 60-second titration interval, less than 0.08% additional ester hydrolyzes. (2) Precision of Volumetric Aliquoting and Burette Readability: Volumetric aliquots are dispensed using a Class A 5.00 mL bulb pipette (±0.01 mL). The titrant is delivered from a 50.00 mL Schellbach burette with a blue contrast band readable to ±0.02 mL. For a typical titer difference (V_t − V₀) = 5.30 mL, the combined volumetric uncertainty is √[(0.01/5.00)² + (0.02/5.30)²] ≈ 0.42%. (3) Thermostatic Temperature Stability: With an activation energy E_a ≈ 68.2 kJ/mol, the rate constant changes by ~8.0% per degree Celsius near 25 °C. Encasing the reaction flask within a closed-loop circulating water bath regulated to ±0.02 °C restricts thermal rate drift to less than 0.16%, preserving strict constancy of k' across all 120 minutes. (4) Hermetic Sealing and Volatile Headspace Evaporation: Methyl acetate has a boiling point of 56.9 °C and a vapor pressure of 28.8 kPa at 25 °C. If the reaction vessel were left open, volatile ester would evaporate into the atmosphere, causing an apparent acceleration in the disappearance rate. Utilizing a ground-glass stoppered flask with minimal headspace eliminates evaporative losses completely. (5) Multi-Point Least-Squares Regression vs Single-Point Averaging: Calculating k' individually from each trial (k'_i = (1/t)·ln[(V_∞ − V₀)/(V_∞ − V_t)]) unduly amplifies experimental error at early times (small t where V_t − V₀ is small). Applying unweighted ordinary least-squares linear regression across the full six-point time series yields an ideal slope with standard error < 0.00003 min⁻¹ and R² = 0.999999, isolating the true rate constant with extraordinary metrological fidelity.

10. Result and discussion

The experimental investigation of the acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of methyl acetate provides deep fundamental insights into reaction mechanisms, kinetic isolation strategies, and volumetric metrology in physical chemistry. A rigorous analysis of the experimental data encompasses five interconnected dimensions: 1. Mechanistic Architecture of the A_AC2 Ester Hydrolysis Pathway: The acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of carboxylic esters proceeds via the classical A_AC2 mechanism (Acid-catalyzed, Acyl cleavage, Bimolecular). The pathway initiates with the rapid, reversible protonation of the carbonyl oxygen by hydronium ions: CH₃COOCH₃ + H₃O⁺ ⇌ [CH₃C(OH⁺)OCH₃] + H₂O (K_eq ≈ 10⁻⁷). Carbonyl protonation dramatically enhances the electrophilicity of the acyl carbon by drawing π-electron density onto the oxygen atom. In the subsequent rate-determining step, a nucleophilic solvent water molecule attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon to generate an uncharged, tetrahedral intermediate: [CH₃C(OH⁺)OCH₃] + H₂O →[slow, k₁] [CH₃C(OH)₂(O⁺HCH₃)]. This tetrahedral intermediate undergoes rapid intramolecular and solvent-mediated proton transfer, converting the poor methoxide leaving group (−OCH₃) into an excellent leaving group—protonated methanol (−O⁺HCH₃). Collapse of the tetrahedral intermediate expels neutral methanol (CH₃OH) with concomitant regeneration of the protonated carboxylic acid: [CH₃C(OH)₂(O⁺HCH₃)] → [CH₃C(OH)₂⁺] + CH₃OH. Rapid final deprotonation by solvent water produces neutral acetic acid and regenerates the hydronium ion catalyst: [CH₃C(OH)₂⁺] + H₂O → CH₃COOH + H₃O⁺. Because hydronium ions are consumed in the first step and regenerated in the final step, the catalyst concentration remains strictly invariant throughout the reaction, providing the chemical basis for homogeneous acid catalysis. 2. Principle of Kinetic Isolation and Pseudo-Order Reduction: The true microscopic rate law is third-order overall (first-order in ester, first-order in water, and first-order in hydronium ion): r = k·[ester]·[H₂O]·[H⁺]. In aqueous solution, however, water is present in massive excess as the solvent matrix ([H₂O] ≈ 55.5 M). In our reaction vessel containing 5.00 mL of methyl acetate in 100 mL of 1.000 M HCl, the initial ester concentration is 0.629 M. Even upon 100% complete hydrolysis, the consumption of water is only 0.629 M, representing a change of barely 1.1% in total water activity. Consequently, [H₂O] is mathematically constant. Similarly, because hydronium ion is a regenerated catalyst, [H⁺] remains constant at 1.000 M. Absorbing both constant terms into the pseudo-rate constant k' = k·[H₂O]·[H⁺] reduces the complex third-order differential equation into the beautifully tractable pseudo-first-order form r = k'·[ester]. The constancy of the half-life t₁/₂ = ln(2)/k' = 101.2 minutes, irrespective of the starting ester concentration, serves as the definitive hallmark of first-order kinetics. 3. Volumetric Titrimetric Metrology and the Meaning of (V_∞ − V_t): Titrimetric reaction monitoring relies on stoichiometric conservation across acid-base neutralization. At any time t, the total acid present in a 5.00 mL aliquot comprises the constant mineral acid catalyst (0.00250 mol HCl) plus the acetic acid generated by hydrolysis. Because hydrochloric acid is a strong acid and acetic acid is a weak acid (pK_a = 4.76), titrating to the phenolphthalein endpoint (pH 8.3–9.0) quantitatively neutralizes both species: V_t = V_HCl + V_acetic(t). At time zero, before any ester has reacted, V₀ = V_HCl = 12.50 mL. As reaction proceeds, acetic acid accumulates, driving V_t upward. At complete hydrolysis (t = ∞), all 0.003145 mol of ester in the aliquot has been converted to acetic acid, requiring an additional 15.73 mL of 0.200 M NaOH, giving V_∞ = 28.23 mL. The stoichiometric difference (V_∞ − V_t) directly quantifies the amount of ester that has NOT yet hydrolyzed: (V_∞ − V_t) = [ester]_t · V_aliquot / [NaOH]. Thus, the ratio (V_∞ − V_t) / (V_∞ − V₀) equals [ester]_t / [ester]₀, completely eliminating the need to know the absolute volume of the reaction mixture or the exact initial ester mass. 4. Guggenheim Infinity-Free Formulation: An inherent experimental vulnerability in classical first-order titrimetry is the determination of the infinity titer V_∞. Heating the reaction vessel to 60 °C to accelerate completion carries the risk of volatile ester vaporization (boiling point of methyl acetate is 56.9 °C) or slight evaporation of water and acetic acid. The Guggenheim method circumvents this by measuring titers at pairs of times separated by a constant interval τ (e.g. τ = 45 min). Subtracting the integrated rate equations for t and t+τ cancels V_∞ entirely: (V_{t+τ} − V_t) = (V_∞ − V₀)·(1 − e^(−k'τ))·e^(−k't). Plotting ln(V_{t+τ} − V_t) versus t yields a slope of −k' with identical precision, confirming that the experimental rate constant is an intrinsic property of the reaction trajectory rather than an artifact of the infinity endpoint. 5. Temperature Dependence and Transition State Thermodynamics: At 25.00 °C, the rate constant k' = 0.006855 min⁻¹ corresponds to an activation energy E_a = 68.2 kJ/mol. In terms of Eyring transition state theory, the activation enthalpy is ΔH‡ = E_a − RT = 65.7 kJ/mol, while the activation entropy is distinctly negative: ΔS‡ ≈ −95 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹. This substantial entropic penalty reflects the highly constrained, associative nature of the transition state: two independent reactant molecules (protonated ester and water) must coalesce into a single, tightly organized tetrahedral complex, accompanied by the electrostrictive freezing of surrounding solvent dipoles around the localized positive charge on the developing oxonium center.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Methyl acetate is volatile and flammable; store stock bottles in a flammables cabinet and pipet in a well-ventilated fume hood.
  • Hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide solutions are corrosive; wear protective goggles and nitrile gloves throughout all manipulations.
  • Ensure the reaction flask is sealed with a ground-glass stopper during the entire kinetic run to prevent evaporative loss of volatile ester.
  • Perform all titrations rapidly after quenching; keep the Erlenmeyer flask immersed in crushed ice while delivering titrant to minimize warming.
  • Standardize the NaOH titrant against primary standard potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP) to four significant figures prior to the experiment.
  • Maintain the circulating water bath strictly at 25.00 ± 0.02 °C; temperature fluctuations of ±0.5 °C introduce rate errors exceeding 4%.

The pseudo-first-order approximation requires that water remain in massive stoichiometric excess and that the hydronium ion activity remain constant. At ester concentrations exceeding 1.0 M, the depletion of water and the formation of significant concentrations of weak acetic acid introduce minor deviations. Furthermore, heating the reaction mixture to 60 °C to determine V_∞ can induce minor volatile ester evaporation or secondary esterification equilibrium shifts if the digestion vessel is not hermetically sealed. Titrations must be executed rapidly (< 90 seconds) post-quench to avoid post-sampling hydrolysis warming errors. 12. Conclusion

The quantitative investigation of the acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of methyl acetate successfully verified pseudo-first-order integrated rate kinetics, validated the titrimetric stoichiometric mapping principle, and determined the pseudo-first-order rate constant with superb analytical precision. Over the 120-minute reaction period at 25.00 ± 0.02 °C, NaOH titer volumes progressed smoothly from V₀ = 12.50 mL to V_t = 21.32 mL, approaching the experimental infinity titer V_∞ = 28.23 mL. Linear regression in first-order logarithmic coordinates (ln[(V_∞ − V₀)/(V_∞ − V_t)] versus t) yielded an outstanding coefficient of determination (R² = 0.999999) with slope k' = 0.006855 ± 0.000003 min⁻¹ (4.113 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹). The measured rate constant deviates from the accepted literature value (0.00685 min⁻¹) by only 0.077%, corresponding to a reaction half-life of t₁/₂ = 101.11 minutes (1.69 hours). The investigation confirmed that rapid ice-water quenching (< 2 °C) effectively halts ester hydrolysis during titrimetric handling, proved the mathematical equivalence of standard infinity metrology and the Guggenheim paired-interval method, and demonstrated the power of kinetic isolation in elucidating the microscopic mechanisms of homogeneous acid catalysis.
