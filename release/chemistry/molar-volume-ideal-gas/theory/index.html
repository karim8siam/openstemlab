Theoretical Foundations of Molar Volume of an Ideal Gas and Quantitative Chemical Metrology

Avogadro's hypothesis, first proposed by Amedeo Avogadro in 1811, posits that equal volumes of all gases under identical conditions of temperature and pressure contain an equal number of molecules, regardless of their chemical identity or molecular mass. Under the ideal gas equation of state (PV = nRT), the molar volume of a gas—defined as the volume occupied by exactly one mole of gas molecules at a specified thermodynamic reference state—is an intensive universal constant V_m = RT / P. At Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP: T_0 = 273.15 K, P_0 = 101.325 kPa = 1.000 atm = 760.0 mmHg), an ideal gas occupies precisely V_m,0 = 22.414 L/mol (22,414 mL/mol). In this foundational physical chemistry laboratory, the molar volume of hydrogen gas is determined through the quantitative single displacement reaction between high-purity metallic magnesium and concentrated hydrochloric acid: Mg (s) + 2 HCl (aq) ---> MgCl₂ (aq) + H₂ (g) ^. The stoichiometric ratio between magnesium consumed and hydrogen gas generated is exactly 1:1, meaning that n(H₂) = n(Mg) = m_Mg / M(Mg), where M(Mg) = 24.305 g/mol. Hydrogen gas is collected over an aqueous column inside an inverted precision Class A gas measuring eudiometer tube. Because the collected gas is saturated with water vapor and held under a liquid hydrostatic head, the raw gas volume must be rigorously corrected to dry hydrogen conditions at STP using Dalton's law of partial pressures (P_dry = P_atm - P_H2O*(T) - P_head) and the combined gas law (V_STP = V_meas · (P_dry / P_0) · (T_0 / T)). Systematic regression of V_STP against the reacted magnesium mass m_Mg across six independent micro-gravimetric trials extracts the empirical molar volume as the slope scaled by the atomic weight of magnesium, testing the validity of the ideal gas model and evaluating subtle real-gas intermolecular forces through the van der Waals equation.

TOPIC 01 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Avogadro's Hypothesis and the Thermodynamic Definition of Molar Volume

Avogadro's law represents one of the crowning conceptual triumphs of early nineteenth-century physical chemistry. In 1811, Amedeo Avogadro resolved John Dalton's volumetric paradoxes by hypothesizing that equal volumes of all ideal gases at identical temperature and pressure contain exactly equal numbers of molecules (V ∝ n at constant T, P). The thermodynamic molar volume of an ideal gas, V_m = V / n = RT / P, is a universal intensive constant whose magnitude depends exclusively on the chosen reference state. Under IUPAC classical Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP), defined as T_0 = 273.15 K (0.00 °C) and P_0 = 101.325 kPa (1.000 atm = 760.0 mmHg), inserting the universal gas constant R = 0.0820574 L·atm/(mol·K) yields V_m,0 = (0.0820574 · 273.15) / 1.0000 = 22.414 L/mol (22,414 mL/mol). (Note that under the alternative IUPAC Standard Ambient Temperature and Pressure, SATP, defined at 298.15 K and 100 kPa, the molar volume is 24.789 L/mol). For any real gas, molecules occupy a non-zero finite volume (excluded volume parameter b in the van der Waals equation) and exert weak intermolecular attractions (parameter a). For hydrogen gas (H₂), the van der Waals parameters are exceptionally small: a = 0.2476 L²·bar/mol² and b = 0.02661 L/mol. Because H₂ has an extremely low polarizability and tiny molecular radius, attractive dispersion forces are negligible at 20 °C, and molecular repulsive forces slightly dominate, resulting in a compressibility factor of Z = PV / (nRT) ≈ 1.0006 at 1 atm. Consequently, real hydrogen behaves almost indistinguishably from an ideal gas under laboratory conditions, making the Mg-HCl displacement reaction the premier experimental vehicle for verifying Avogadro's hypothesis.

TOPIC 02 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures and Water Vapor Pressure Corrections

When a gas is generated by an aqueous chemical reaction and collected by water displacement inside an inverted eudiometer, the gas space above the liquid is not composed solely of pure hydrogen; rather, it is a binary vapor mixture of hydrogen and gaseous water molecules. John Dalton formulated his law of partial pressures in 1801, stating that the total pressure exerted by a mixture of non-reacting gases is equal to the sum of the partial pressures that each constituent gas would exert if it occupied the entire container volume alone: P_total = P_H2 + P_H2O. As hydrogen bubbles through the aqueous solution, evaporation occurs continuously across the gas-liquid interface until thermodynamic equilibrium is established between liquid water and water vapor. At equilibrium, the partial pressure of water vapor P_H2O equals the saturation vapor pressure of pure water P_H2O*(T), which is strictly a function of temperature and independent of the quantity of hydrogen present. The equilibrium vapor pressure of water increases exponentially with temperature according to the Clausius-Clapeyron equation, d(ln P*)/dT = ΔH_vap / (R·T²). For instance, at 18.0 °C, P_H2O* = 15.48 mmHg; at 20.0 °C, P_H2O* = 17.54 mmHg; at 22.0 °C, P_H2O* = 19.83 mmHg; and at 25.0 °C, P_H2O* = 23.76 mmHg. If an analyst fails to subtract the water vapor pressure from the total barometric pressure, the pressure attributed to hydrogen will be overestimated by 2.3% to 3.1% at room temperature. This would lead to a systematic positive error in the calculated STP volume and a falsely inflated molar volume. Applying Dalton's correction isolates the true partial pressure of dry hydrogen: P_H2 = P_atm - P_H2O*(T).

TOPIC 03 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Hydrostatic Head Correction Mechanics in Eudiometric Gas Burettes

In eudiometric gas measurements, the total pressure of the gas trapped inside the inverted tube is mechanically balanced against the external atmospheric pressure and the hydrostatic pressure exerted by the suspended liquid column. If the water level inside the eudiometer is higher than the water level in the surrounding reservoir by a vertical distance Δh_water (in millimeters), the suspended liquid column pulls downward due to gravity, creating a partial vacuum inside the tube. Under this condition, the total internal gas pressure is lower than ambient barometric pressure: P_total + P_head = P_atm, which rearranges to P_total = P_atm - P_head. To express the hydrostatic pressure of the water column in conventional pressure units of millimeters of mercury (mmHg), one applies the fluid static relation P_head = ρ_water · g · Δh_water = ρ_Hg · g · Δh_Hg. Because the density of liquid mercury at 20 °C is ρ_Hg = 13.546 g/cm³ and water is ρ_water = 0.9982 g/cm³, the mercury-equivalent hydrostatic pressure is Δh_Hg = Δh_water / 13.57. For example, a water column of Δh = 136 mm inside the eudiometer reduces the internal gas pressure by exactly 136 / 13.57 = 10.0 mmHg (1.33 kPa). In our experimental protocol, the necessity for mathematical hydrostatic corrections is completely eliminated through physical leveling: by transferring the eudiometer into a tall-form 1000 mL leveling cylinder and adjusting its vertical height until the inner and outer water surfaces are perfectly coplanar (Δh_water = 0), the hydrostatic head is reduced to zero. Thus, P_total is guaranteed to equal P_atm exactly.

TOPIC 04 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

The Role of Copper Wire: Galvanic Coupling and Physical Retention

The copper wire assembly serves two essential functions in eudiometric hydrogen generation: one mechanical, and one electrochemical. Mechanically, metallic magnesium has a density of ρ_Mg = 1.738 g/cm³, which is denser than water. However, as the reaction with hydrochloric acid begins, microscopic hydrogen gas bubbles (ρ_H2 ≈ 0.00008 g/cm³) nucleate rapidly upon the magnesium surface. The buoyant force exerted by these adhering micro-bubbles quickly exceeds the gravitational weight of the remaining magnesium strip, causing unsecured fragments of magnesium to break away and float upward into the narrow calibrated section of the eudiometer. Floating metal can adhere to glass walls above the descending acid front, halting the reaction prematurely and leaving unreacted metal trapped in the gas space. The spiraled copper wire cage mechanically anchors the magnesium in place at the bottom of the tube. Electrochemically, copper (standard reduction potential E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V) and magnesium (E°(Mg²⁺/Mg) = −2.37 V) form a vigorous galvanic cell upon contact in acidic electrolyte (ΔE°_cell = +2.71 V). Magnesium acts as the sacrificial anode, undergoing rapid oxidation (Mg ---> Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻), while the noble copper wire acts as a catalytic cathode where hydronium ions are reduced to hydrogen gas (2H⁺ + 2e⁻ ---> H₂). Because copper has a significantly lower hydrogen overpotential than magnesium, galvanic coupling substantially accelerates the reaction rate and ensures complete, rapid dissolution of the metal.

TOPIC 05 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Non-Ideality of Hydrogen Gas: van der Waals and Virial Equations of State

While the ideal gas equation of state PV = nRT assumes that gas molecules are point particles possessing zero volume and exerting zero intermolecular forces, all real gases exhibit deviations from ideal behavior described by empirical and semi-empirical equations of state. The van der Waals equation introduces two substance-specific corrective constants: [P + a·(n/V)²] · [V - n·b] = n·R·T, where the term a·(n/V)² accounts for long-range attractive dispersion forces that reduce the force of molecular wall collisions, and the parameter b represents the finite volume occupied by one mole of molecules (excluded volume). For diatomic hydrogen (H₂), the molecular parameters are a = 0.2476 L²·bar/mol² and b = 0.02661 L/mol. Comparing hydrogen to other common gases illustrates its exceptional proximity to ideality: carbon dioxide has a = 3.640 L²·bar/mol² and b = 0.04267 L/mol (more than 14 times greater attraction). In the virial equation of state, the compressibility factor Z = PV / (RT) is expressed as a power series in pressure: Z = 1 + B'(T)·P + C'(T)·P² + ... For hydrogen at 273.15 K, the second virial coefficient is positive: B(T) = +13.7 cm³/mol. This positive value indicates that at standard temperature, the tiny repulsive excluded volume effect dominates over the negligible attractive forces. Consequently, the true experimental molar volume of hydrogen at STP is V_m(H₂, real) = 22.428 L/mol, differing from the ideal gas value (22.414 L/mol) by a mere +0.062%. This minuscule difference validates the use of the ideal gas model as an exceptional approximation for undergraduate metrology.

TOPIC 06 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Density Stratification and Diffusion Dynamics in Acid-Water Inversion

The successful execution of eudiometric gas collection relies upon the exploitation of gravitational density stratification and molecular diffusion. Concentrated hydrochloric acid (6.0 M HCl) has a density of approximately 1.10 g/cm³, whereas pure deionized water has a density of 1.00 g/cm³. When 10 mL of 6 M HCl is placed in the bottom of the eudiometer tube and water is carefully layered over it down the inclined glass wall, the substantial density difference (Δρ ≈ 0.10 g/cm³) establishes a stable, gravitationally stratified two-layer system with a sharp liquid-liquid interface. Molecular diffusion across this interface is governed by Fick's first law, J = −D · (dC/dx). Because the diffusion coefficient of hydronium ions in water is D ≈ 9.3 × 10⁻⁵ cm²/s, spontaneous diffusive mixing across the 40 cm column requires many hours. This kinetic stability allows the analyst sufficient time to position the magnesium ribbon near the tube mouth, insert the stopper, and invert the eudiometer into the water reservoir without triggering premature reaction. Upon inversion, the physical orientation is reversed: the dense 6 M acid layer is now at the top of the vertical liquid column. Gravity acts immediately to overturn the unstable density gradient: plumes of dense, concentrated acid descend rapidly through the lighter water column via convective finger streaming. Within 60 to 90 seconds, the descending acid front reaches the copper cage at the bottom of the tube, bathing the magnesium in 6 M HCl and initiating instantaneous, vigorous effervescence.

TOPIC 07 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Propagation of Uncertainty in Multi-Variable Gas Metrology

The determination of molar volume from eudiometric data involves combining multiple primary measurements into the composite equation V_m,STP = [V_meas · (P_atm - P_H2O*) · T_0 · M(Mg)] / [P_0 · T_meas · m_Mg]. In accordance with the GUM (Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement), the combined relative standard uncertainty is given by the quadrature sum of individual relative uncertainties: (u_c(V_m) / V_m)² = (u(V) / V)² + (u(P) / P)² + (u(T) / T)² + (u(m) / m)². An analytical breakdown reveals: (1) Gas Volume Uncertainty: The Class A eudiometer has a reading tolerance of ±0.05 mL. For a typical volume of ~25 mL, u(V)/V = 0.05 / 25.0 = 0.20%. (2) Magnesium Mass Uncertainty: The microbalance has a repeatability of ±0.01 mg. For a mass of 25.00 mg, u(m)/m = 0.01 / 25.00 = 0.040%. (3) Temperature Uncertainty: A calibrated thermometer reading to ±0.1 °C in 293.15 K yields u(T)/T = 0.1 / 293.15 = 0.034%. (4) Barometric Pressure Uncertainty: Mercury barometer vernier reading to ±0.1 mmHg in 760 mmHg yields u(P)/P = 0.1 / 760 = 0.013%. The volumetric measurement u(V) is by far the dominant contributor to overall uncertainty, accounting for >75% of the total variance. By utilizing multi-point ordinary least-squares linear regression of V_STP versus m_Mg across six independent mass increments, random meniscus reading errors and individual transfer variations are statistically averaged across four degrees of freedom, reducing the standard error of the slope by a factor of 1 / sqrt(N - 2) and providing a highly reliable estimate of V_m,STP.

Connect the theory to experimental observations.

The eudiometric gas model incorporates four coupled thermodynamic and hydrodynamic corrections: (1) Stoichiometric mole equivalence n(H₂) = m_Mg / 24.305 g/mol; (2) Dalton's law of partial pressures subtracting the temperature-dependent equilibrium saturation vapor pressure of liquid water P_H2O*(T) formulated via the Antoine equation log₁₀(P) = A - B / (T + C); (3) Hydrostatic head elimination by matching the internal and external water menisci inside the tall leveling jar (or applying the mercury-equivalent hydrostatic correction ΔP_head = Δh_water / 13.595 mmHg); and (4) Combined gas law reduction to Standard Temperature and Pressure (T_0 = 273.15 K, P_0 = 760.0 mmHg). The regression slope m_slope = V_m,STP / M_Mg relates directly to the molar volume of hydrogen gas, achieving sub-0.05% agreement with the theoretical value of 22.414 L/mol.
