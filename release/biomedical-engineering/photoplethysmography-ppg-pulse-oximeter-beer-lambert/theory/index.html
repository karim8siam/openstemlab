Biomedical and physiological foundations of Pulse Oximeter PPG Calibration.

Pulse oximetry is one of the most pervasive vital sign monitoring technologies in modern anesthesia, intensive care, and emergency medicine, enabling continuous, non-invasive assessment of arterial blood oxygenation. Invented by Takuo Aoyagi in 1974, pulse oximetry exploits the optical absorption differences between oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO2) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (RHb) based on the Beer-Lambert law. At red wavelengths (λ = 660 nm), deoxygenated hemoglobin absorbs significantly more light than HbO2; at near-infrared wavelengths (λ = 940 nm), HbO2 absorbs slightly more light than RHb, with an isobestic point occurring near 805 nm. By illuminating vascularized tissue (such as a fingertip or earlobe) with alternating red and infrared LEDs, a photodiode captures transmitted light. The signal is partitioned into a non-pulsatile DC component (representing static tissue, bone, and venous blood) and a pulsatile AC component (representing expanding arterial blood with each cardiac heartbeat). Normalizing AC by DC at each wavelength yields the dimensionless ratio-of-ratios: R = (AC_660 / DC_660) / (AC_940 / DC_940). Across clinical saturation ranges (75% to 100%), arterial oxygen saturation SpO2 relates linearly to R through the empirical calibration equation: SpO2 = -k_ox · R + 110.0. In this experiment, a calibrated dual-wavelength optical pulse oximeter probe is evaluated on an optical blood perfusion phantom to establish the calibration curve.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

How does pulse oximetry isolate arterial blood from surrounding venous blood and tissue?

Pulse oximetry utilizes the 'plethysmographic principle': only arterial blood volume pulses with each heartbeat. By dividing the pulsatile AC signal by the non-pulsatile DC background at each wavelength (AC/DC), the static absorption of bone, skin, muscle, and venous blood cancels completely, isolating the arterial blood optical properties.

Why are 660 nm (red) and 940 nm (infrared) chosen as the two wavelengths?

At 660 nm, deoxygenated hemoglobin has roughly ten times higher absorption than oxygenated hemoglobin. At 940 nm, the relationship flips and HbO2 absorbs more than RHb. This high contrast provides maximum sensitivity to changes in blood oxygen saturation.

What is the isobestic wavelength in hemoglobin spectroscopy?

An isobestic point is a wavelength where the extinction coefficients of oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin are strictly identical (such as 805 nm). At an isobestic wavelength, light absorption is proportional to total hemoglobin concentration regardless of saturation.

How do dyshemoglobins (carboxyhemoglobin and methemoglobin) cause erroneous pulse oximeter readings?

Carboxyhemoglobin (COHb, carbon monoxide poisoning) absorbs light at 660 nm identically to HbO2, causing the pulse oximeter to falsely report near-100% saturation. Methemoglobin absorbs strongly at both 660 nm and 940 nm, driving the ratio R toward 1.0 (locking readings at a false 85%).

Why is pulse oximetry calibrated empirically on human volunteers rather than purely theoretically?

The Beer-Lambert law assumes pure absorption in non-scattering media. Biological tissue causes massive multiple optical scattering, altering the effective photon optical pathlength; therefore, all commercial pulse oximeters rely on empirical calibration curves.

What is the physiological significance of the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve?

The sigmoidal curve relates blood PO2 to Hb saturation. Because of its steep shape below 90% SpO2 (corresponding to PaO2 ≈ 60 mmHg), a small drop in partial pressure causes rapid, precipitous desaturation, making SpO2 an early warning indicator of impending hypoxemia.

Experimental precautions and patient safety

  • Shield the finger probe from direct surgical lights and sunlight to prevent photodiode saturation.
  • Ensure steady phantom pulsatile flow; severe hypoperfusion reduces AC amplitude, dropping signal-to-noise ratio.
  • Verify absence of dark-period optical crosstalk between interleaved red and infrared LED firings.
  • Verify reference co-oximeter is calibrated with multi-wavelength certified hemolyzed blood standards.

Theoretical assumptions & biological limits

  • The linear calibration model applies across the clinical diagnostic range (SpO2 75% to 100%).
  • Fails to detect carbon monoxide poisoning (COHb) and methemoglobinemia (MetHb).
  • Patient motion artifacts and severe peripheral vasoconstriction can cause false pulse readings or loss of tracking.
  • Skin pigmentation (melanin) adds optical attenuation that can introduce small calibration offsets at low saturations.
