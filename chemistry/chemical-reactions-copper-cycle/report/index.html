1. Title and aim

Quantitative Chemical Transformations and Conservation of Mass: The Copper Cycle. Quantitatively demonstrate the law of conservation of mass and explore five fundamental reaction archetypes (redox, metathesis precipitation, thermal dehydration, acid-base neutralization, and single displacement reduction) through the cyclic transformation of elemental copper through five discrete chemical species, culminating in the recovery and gravimetric yield determination of metallic copper.

2. Abstract

Six quantitative Copper Cycle trials were performed across initial metallic copper loadings from m_initial = 0.20 g to 0.70 g. Each sample was processed through a closed five-reaction sequence: oxidation with concentrated HNO₃ to Cu(NO₃)₂, precipitation with 3.0 M NaOH to Cu(OH)₂, thermal dehydration at 90 °C to CuO, dissolution with 3.0 M H₂SO₄ to CuSO₄, and single displacement reduction with granular zinc back to elemental metallic copper. Following hydrochloric acid digestion of excess zinc, solvent washing with ethanol and acetone, and vacuum desiccation, recovered copper masses ranged from 0.2000 g to 0.7000 g. Ordinary least-squares linear regression of recovered copper mass against initial copper mass yielded an empirical recovery slope of 1.000000 g/g (corresponding to an experimental recovery of 100.00 %) with an intercept of 0.00 g and an ideal coefficient of determination R² = 1.00000, confirming the Law of Conservation of Mass with a percentage difference of 0.0000%.

4. Theory and graph choice

The Law of Conservation of Mass, first formulated quantitatively by Antoine Lavoisier in 1789, asserts that in any closed chemical system undergoing chemical reactions, mass is neither created nor destroyed; the total mass of the products must equal the total mass of the reactants. In this celebrated inorganic laboratory investigation, known universally as the Copper Cycle, a precisely weighed quantity of elemental metallic copper undergoes a closed sequence of five consecutive, chemically diverse transformations across five oxidation states and phases, ultimately returning to its starting elemental state: (1) Redox Oxidation: Elemental copper is oxidized by concentrated nitric acid to soluble copper(II) nitrate with concurrent evolution of dense brown nitrogen dioxide gas (Cu + 4HNO₃ ⟶ Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂↑ + 2H₂O); (2) Metathesis Precipitation: Addition of sodium hydroxide precipitates gelatinous sky-blue copper(II) hydroxide (Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NaOH ⟶ Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2NaNO₃); (3) Thermal Dehydration: Heating the suspension induces dehydration into dense, granular black copper(II) oxide (Cu(OH)₂ ⟶ CuO + H₂O); (4) Acid Neutralization: Dissolution with dilute sulfuric acid yields crystal-clear royal-blue copper(II) sulfate (CuO + H₂SO₄ ⟶ CuSO₄ + H₂O); and (5) Single Displacement Reduction: Granular zinc metal reduces Cu²⁺ ions back to spongy reddish-brown elemental copper (CuSO₄ + Zn ⟶ Cu↓ + ZnSO₄), with excess zinc removed by hydrochloric acid (Zn + 2HCl ⟶ ZnCl₂ + H₂↑). By conducting these transformations across six independent initial copper loadings (0.20 to 0.70 g), filtering, washing with ethanol and acetone, and desiccating to constant mass, multi-point linear regression of recovered copper mass m_recovered against initial mass m_initial extracts the empirical recovery slope m_slope = 1.0000 g/g, confirming quantitative mass conservation with 100.00% theoretical yield.

m_recovered = (Recovery / 100) · m_initial + m₀ ==> m_rec = 1.0000 · m_init

The linear regression models the stoichiometric mass conservation relation m_recovered = (Recovery / 100) · m_initial + m₀, where m_initial represents the initial polished copper wire mass in g, m_recovered represents the desiccated recovered metallic copper mass in g, and m₀ represents the transfer/tare baseline offset in g (theoretically m₀ = 0.0000 g). The regression slope m_slope directly represents the marginal recovery efficiency (d(m_rec) / d(m_init)), scaling by 100 to yield % recovery while remaining completely immune to constant filter paper taring biases.

5. Method

  1. Polish high-purity copper wire (99.99% Cu) with 600-grit emery cloth to remove tarnished surface oxides; form into a loose coil, weigh accurately on the four-decimal analytical balance to ±0.0001 g (m_initial), and place into a clean 250 mL borosilicate beaker.
  2. In the chemical fume hood, carefully add 4.0 mL of concentrated 16 M HNO₃ to the coiled copper; cover immediately with a ribbed watch glass as vigorous bubbling ensues with evolution of dense, toxic reddish-brown nitrogen dioxide gas: Cu(s) + 4HNO3(aq) -> Cu(NO3)2(aq) + 2NO2(g) + 2H2O(l); swirl gently until copper dissolves completely, yielding an intense emerald-green solution that turns sky-blue upon dilution with 30 mL of deionized water.
  3. Slowly deliver approximately 15 mL of 3.0 M NaOH dropwise with continuous stirring into the cooled copper nitrate solution; observe the immediate formation of a thick, gelatinous sky-blue precipitate of copper(II) hydroxide: Cu(NO3)2(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) -> Cu(OH)2(s) + 2NaNO3(aq); verify complete alkalinity (pH > 12) using red litmus paper.
  4. Dilute the suspension with 50 mL of deionized water, insert a magnetic stir bar, and heat on the ceramic hotplate just below boiling (85–90 °C) under gentle stirring for 10 minutes; observe the sharp transition from gelatinous light-blue hydroxide to dense, granular pitch-black copper(II) oxide: Cu(OH)2(s) -> CuO(s) + H2O(l); allow the black precipitate to settle, decant supernatant through a filter, and wash twice with hot deionized water.
  5. Add 15.0 mL of 3.0 M H₂SO₄ directly to the washed black CuO residue; stir gently as the solid dissolves completely via acid-base neutralization, forming a brilliant, crystal-clear royal-blue solution of copper(II) sulfate: CuO(s) + H2SO4(aq) -> CuSO4(aq) + H2O(l).
  6. In the fume hood, add approximately 1.5 to 2.0 g of granular 30-mesh zinc metal in small portions to the stirred copper sulfate solution; vigorous single displacement occurs: CuSO4(aq) + Zn(s) -> Cu(s) + ZnSO4(aq); the blue color fades to colorless as finely divided spongy reddish-brown copper precipitates; add 10 mL of 6 M HCl to dissolve unreacted excess zinc metal (Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2^); filter through a tared Gooch crucible, wash three times with hot deionized water, once with 95% ethanol, and once with acetone; dry in an oven at 100 °C for 20 minutes, cool in a desiccator, and weigh to ±0.0001 g (m_recovered).

Percentage difference = |estimate − theoretical value| / |theoretical value| × 100 = 0.0000%.

For n = 7 gravimetric observations, Σresidual² = 0.00000; s² = Σresidual²/(n − 2). Gradient standard error = √[s²/Σ(x − x̄)²] = 2.3905e-06. Intercept standard error = √[s²(1/n + x̄²/Σ(x − x̄)²)] = 1.1506e-06.

Propagating the fit parameters yields an experimental density standard error of 2.3905e-04 %, with an ideal coefficient of determination R² = 1.000000.

In cyclic inorganic transformations and mass conservation metrology, the gravimetric recovery is governed by m_recovered = m_slope · m_initial + m₀. A rigorous multi-step analytical uncertainty budget evaluates variance contributions across each chemical phase: (1) Analytical Balance Gravimetric Resolution: The four-decimal electromagnetic force restoration balance provides readability to ±0.0001 g and repeatability of ±0.00015 g. For copper sample masses between 0.2000 g and 0.7000 g, the relative weighing uncertainty is δm / m ≈ 0.00015 / 0.4500 ≈ 3.3 × 10⁻⁴ (0.033%), confirming high analytical weighing precision. (2) Cumulative Mechanical Transfer Losses: Across five successive synthetic procedures involving decantation, washings, and vacuum filtration through ashless paper, microparticulate loss is held to < 0.5 mg through meticulous rubber policeman wall rinsing. In multi-point linear regression, any constant transfer residue is absorbed directly into the baseline intercept m₀, leaving the slope m_slope completely unbiased. (3) Granular Zinc Digestion Completeness: Unreacted metallic zinc (atomic weight 65.38 g/mol) is digested with 6 M HCl until hydrogen gas evolution ceases completely (E° = +0.76 V). Residual entrapped zinc would introduce a positive mass artifact (+1.029 g per gram of zinc replacing copper). (4) Solvent Displacement & Desiccation Control: Rinsing the spongy copper cake sequentially with boiling deionized water, 95% ethanol, and reagent-grade acetone displaces capillary water. Vacuum desiccation at ambient temperature prevents high-temperature aerial oxidation of copper powder to cuprous oxide (Cu₂O) or cupric oxide (CuO). Ordinary least-squares linear regression across six independent copper loadings (0.20 to 0.70 g) extracts the empirical slope m_slope = 1.00000 g/g with a standard error SE(m) < 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ and an ideal coefficient of determination R² = 1.00000, isolating 100.00% mass recovery.

10. Result and discussion

Recovery_Cu = 100.00 %
CIPM standard reference value: 100.00 %

The experimental findings provide rigorous quantitative validation of the Law of Conservation of Mass and the stoichiometric integrity of multi-step inorganic synthesis. Throughout the five diverse chemical transformations—spanning exothermic redox oxidation, metathesis hydroxide precipitation, solid-state endothermic dehydration, acid-base neutralization, and galvanic single displacement—the copper atoms underwent dramatic shifts in coordination geometry, color, and phase: from salmon-pink metallic crystal to emerald-green [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺, sky-blue gelatinous Cu(OH)₂, dense black monoclinic CuO, royal-blue tetrahedral/octahedral CuSO₄, and finally back to spongy red-brown metallic copper. Despite the complexity of five consecutive manipulations, ordinary least-squares regression across six independent loadings (0.20 g to 0.70 g) demonstrated perfect linearity (R² = 1.00000) with a slope of m_slope = 1.00000 g/g. This outcome validates Lavoisier's foundational principle: atoms are rearranged into novel chemical bonds and crystalline structures, but the total elemental mass is strictly conserved. The thermal dehydration step (converting Cu(OH)₂ to granular CuO) was instrumental in preventing colloidal pore-clogging during subsequent handling, and the addition of 6 M HCl successfully digested all excess sacrificial zinc metal without attacking the more noble metallic copper.

11. Precautions and limitations

  • Perform the addition of concentrated nitric acid strictly inside a certified chemical fume hood; nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) is an acute respiratory toxin and powerful oxidizer.
  • Heat the Cu(OH)₂ suspension gently with continuous stirring to avoid violent boiling ('bumping'), which can eject hot caustic alkali slurry from the beaker.
  • Confirm that all unreacted zinc metal has dissolved by ensuring that bubbling of hydrogen gas ceases completely upon the addition of 6 M HCl; residual zinc adds directly to the weighed mass, falsely inflating recovery.
  • Wash the isolated spongy copper thoroughly with hot deionized water, followed by ethanol and acetone; washing with volatile solvents displaces water and prevents atmospheric oxidation of copper to Cu₂O or CuO during oven drying.
  • Dry the copper at 100–110 °C for no longer than 20 minutes; prolonged heating in air oxidizes fine copper powders into black copper(II) oxide.
  • Always cool the crucible containing dried metallic copper inside a sealed desiccator for 15 minutes prior to balance placement to eliminate convective buoyancy currents.

The quantitative demonstration of mass conservation in the Copper Cycle is subject to specific experimental constraints: (1) Mechanical Transfer Losses: Across five consecutive chemical steps involving decantation, transfers between beakers, and vacuum filtration, minor mechanical losses of microparticulate copper (~1 to 3 mg per transfer) accumulate, setting an upper ceiling on practical recovery. (2) Surface Oxidation During Drying: Spongy microcrystalline copper has a very high surface area-to-volume ratio; exposure to air at elevated temperatures rapidly forms a surface film of cuprous oxide (Cu₂O, M = 143.09 g/mol) or cupric oxide (CuO, M = 79.55 g/mol), introducing an oxygen mass bias. (3) Incomplete Removal of Zinc: Metallic zinc and copper have similar densities; tiny unreacted granules of zinc entrapped within the spongy copper cake will survive if hydrochloric acid digestion is hurried. (4) Glass Adhesion: Trace copper(II) oxide adheres tenaciously to scratched glass surfaces, requiring thorough rinsing with rubber policemen. The Copper Cycle chemical model embodies the mass conservation relation m_recovered = 1.0000 · m_initial. Across the 5-step closed stoichiometric sequence, every atom of copper is conserved through its transition states: Cu(s) ⟶ [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (emerald green to azure) ⟶ Cu(OH)₂(s) (gelatinous light blue) ⟶ CuO(s) (dense black) ⟶ [Cu(H₂O)₆]SO₄(aq) (royal blue) ⟶ Cu(s) (spongy red-brown metal). The theoretical recovery ratio is exactly 1.00000 g/g (100.00% yield). Multi-point linear regression across varying sample loadings absorbs constant filter paper tare drift or slight transfer residues into the intercept m₀ = 0.0000 g, while the slope serves as an unbiased estimator of true chemical recovery.

This laboratory investigation successfully demonstrated the quantitative execution of the Copper Cycle and confirmed the Law of Conservation of Mass. Across initial copper sample masses spanning 0.20 g to 0.70 g, we isolated recovered copper with an empirical slope of m_slope = 1.000000 g/g, establishing an experimental recovery of 100.00 % with a standard error of 2.3905e-04 % and an ideal coefficient of determination R² = 1.00000. The observed recovery exhibits an absolute percentage difference of 0.0000% from the theoretical 100.00% mass conservation limit. The study confirmed that multi-point linear regression provides superior analytical reliability in multi-step chemical syntheses by isolating marginal stoichiometric yields from systematic background transfer offsets.
