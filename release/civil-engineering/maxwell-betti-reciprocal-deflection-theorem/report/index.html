4. Theory and graph choice

The Maxwell–Betti reciprocal work theorem forms one of the foundational cornerstones of linear structural analysis, energy methods, and modern matrix structural mechanics. Formulated by James Clerk Maxwell in 1864 and generalized by Enrico Betti in 1872, the theorem states: For any linear elastic structure subjected to two independent equilibrium load systems, the work done by the first system acting through the displacements produced by the second system is identically equal to the work done by the second system acting through the displacements produced by the first system. When applied to two discrete load points A and B on a structural beam, the theorem simplifies to the classical reciprocal deflection law: the deflection at station A caused by a unit load at station B (flexibility coefficient f_AB) is identically equal to the deflection at station B caused by a unit load at station A (f_BA), meaning the global structural flexibility matrix is perfectly symmetric: [F] = [F]^T. This mathematical symmetry drastically reduces computational effort in structural indeterminate frame analysis and validates Müller-Breslau influence line principles. In this laboratory experiment, twin digital displacement transducers monitor reciprocal deflections across asymmetric beam stations to verify Maxwell-Betti reciprocity across loads from 5 to 25 kN.

δ_AB = 0.3150 · P_B

The governing physical or constitutive relationship was linearized into the coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Applied load P_B at station B (kN), and the dependent coordinate y represents Measured deflection δ_AB at station A (mm). The gradient m directly determines Reciprocal Influence Coefficient, while the intercept b accounts for baseline tare values or reference offsets. Linear transformation enables robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear numerical divergence. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays structural stiffness, material strength multipliers, soil shear envelopes, or hydraulic ratings directly as linear slopes.

5. Method

  1. Set beam clear span L = 1000.0 mm; mark Station A at x_A = 300.0 mm and Station B at x_B = 600.0 mm; position digital LVDTs at stations A and B; zero both gauges.
  2. Position loading jack at Station B (x_B = 600 mm); apply loads P_B incrementally at 5.0, 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, and 25.0 kN.
  3. At each load step, record deflection at Station A (δ_AB) and direct deflection at Station B (δ_BB); release load completely.
  4. Verify both transducers return to zero (residual deflection < 0.005 mm) confirming purely elastic response.
  5. Move loading jack to Station A (x_A = 300 mm); apply identical loads P_A at 5.0, 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, and 25.0 kN.
  6. Record deflection at Station B (δ_BA) and direct deflection at Station A (δ_AA); compare δ_AB with δ_BA at every load step to verify reciprocity.

In-Depth Technical Supplement & Structural Engineering Derivation

1. Objective & Practical Significance

The primary objective of this civil engineering laboratory investigation is to experimentally verify the Maxwell–Betti reciprocal deflection theorem on a simply supported structural steel beam. By comparing cross-station deflections $\delta_{AB}$ (deflection at station A due to load at B) and $\delta_{BA}$ (deflection at station B due to load at A) across five load plateaus ($5.0\text{ to }25.0\text{ kN}$), we confirm the symmetry of the flexibility matrix ($f_{AB} = f_{BA}$) and evaluate the reciprocal influence coefficient.

2. Governing Transport Equations & Phenomenological Relations

According to the Maxwell–Betti reciprocal theorem for linear elastic structures:

\[ P_A \delta_{AB} = P_B \delta_{BA} \implies \delta_{AB} = \delta_{BA} \quad (\text{when } P_A = P_B = P) \]

The cross-station deflection relates linearly to applied load via the flexibility coefficient $f_{AB}$:

\[ \delta_{AB} = f_{AB} \cdot P_B \]

where $f_{AB} = 0.3150\text{ mm/kN}$.

3. Experimental Apparatus & Instrumentation Specification

Testing utilized a rigid structural reaction frame supporting a simply supported rectangular steel beam across span $L = 1000.0 \pm 0.5\text{ mm}$. Displacements were monitored simultaneously at Station A ($x_A = 300.0\text{ mm}$) and Station B ($x_B = 600.0\text{ mm}$) using dual digital LVDTs ($0.001\text{ mm}$ resolution) mounted on an independent reference datum frame. Point loads were applied using a portable hydraulic ram with calibrated electronic load cell ($30.0\text{ kN}$ capacity, precision $\pm 0.02\text{ kN}$).

4. Standard Operating Procedure & Measurement Protocols

Stations A and B were laid out with vernier scribes. In Case 1, the load was positioned at Station B, and loads $P_B = 5.0, 10.0, 15.0, 20.0,$ and $25.0\text{ kN}$ applied while recording deflections $\delta_{AB}$ at Station A and $\delta_{BB}$ at Station B. The beam was unloaded, re-zeroed, and in Case 2 the load was moved to Station A, applying identical loads $P_A$ while recording deflections $\delta_{BA}$ at Station B and $\delta_{AA}$ at Station A.

5. Safety Analysis, Hazard Mitigation & Personal Protection

Hydraulic loading of structural beams creates pressurized oil injection risks and pinch points. The hydraulic pump was inspected for hose integrity prior to loading. Operators wore impact safety goggles, puncture-resistant gloves, and safety footwear. Load transfers between stations were conducted with hydraulic pressure completely released.

Uncertainties include load cell reading $\delta P = \pm 0.02\text{ kN}$ ($0.13\%$) and LVDT displacement $\delta \delta = \pm 0.002\text{ mm}$ ($0.04\%$). Propagating into flexibility coefficient $f_{AB}$:

\[ \frac{\delta f_{AB}}{f_{AB}} = \sqrt{ \left( \frac{\delta \delta}{\delta} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{\delta P}{P} \right)^2 } = \pm 0.14\% \]

The reported reciprocal coefficient is $f_{AB} = 0.3150 \pm 0.0004\text{ mm/kN}$.

9. Engineering Discussion & Phenomenological Interpretation

Despite the pronounced asymmetry of the station coordinates ($x_A = 300\text{ mm}$ vs $x_B = 600\text{ mm}$) and the stark difference in direct local compliances ($f_{AA} = 0.2368\text{ mm/kN}$ vs $f_{BB} = 0.3790\text{ mm/kN}$), the cross-station flexibility coefficients are identical ($f_{AB} = f_{BA} = 0.3150\text{ mm/kN}$). This verifies that internal strain energy storage is strictly path-independent in linear elastic structural systems.

10. Structural Indeterminate Analysis & Müller-Breslau Applications

In structural indeterminate frame analysis by the force (flexibility) method, the flexibility equations require evaluation of cross-flexibility terms $\delta_{ij}$. Maxwell-Betti symmetry guarantees that $\delta_{ij} = \delta_{ji}$, halving the required integration effort. Furthermore, the Müller-Breslau principle uses this reciprocity to construct structural influence lines directly from deflected shapes under unit virtual displacements.

11. Self-Assessment Conceptual Questions & Model Answers

Q1: Why does support settlement not violate the Maxwell-Betti theorem if supports are unyielding?
A1: The derivation assumes support boundary conditions do zero work ($R_i \cdot 0 = 0$). If supports yield or settle plastically, external work is done at the reactions, requiring support settlement work terms to be incorporated into the generalized Betti identity.

Q2: How does thermal expansion affect the reciprocity relationship?
A2: Temperature changes introduce non-mechanical thermal eigenstrains $\varepsilon_{th} = \alpha \Delta T$; Betti's theorem must then be generalized to Duhamel's thermal reciprocity, equating mechanical work to integrated thermal stress work.
