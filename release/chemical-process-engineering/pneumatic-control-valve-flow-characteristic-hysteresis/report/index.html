4. Theory and graph choice

The pneumatic control valve is the primary final control element (FCE) in chemical and process plants, modulating fluid flow rates to regulate pressures, temperatures, levels, and compositions across pipeline networks. A typical globe control valve consists of a pneumatic actuator (flexible elastomer diaphragm and opposing helical return springs) coupled via a stem to an internal plug modulating orifice area within the valve body. In an air-to-open (fail-closed, FC) valve, instrument air pressure (20 to 100 kPa / 3 to 15 psig) applied to the diaphragm overcomes spring pre-compression and packing friction to lift the plug off the seat ring. The inherent flow characteristic describes the relationship between fluid flow rate Q (or valve flow coefficient C_v) and fractional stem lift x under constant pressure drop ΔP_v. For a valve with linear trim, flow capacity is directly proportional to fractional stem lift: Q(x) = S · x, where S is the flow capacity slope ((m^3/h)/%). At full 100% lift, maximum rated flow capacity is Q_max = S · 100% = 85.0 m^3/h. The flow coefficient is governed by standard IEC 60534 / ISA-75 equations: C_v = Q · sqrt(SG / ΔP_v). In real mechanical valves, mechanical friction between the reciprocating stem and compressed PTFE/graphite chevron packing creates hysteresis and deadband: the stem position and flow rate at a given control signal differ between ascending (opening) and descending (closing) strokes. Digital valve positioners equipped with high-gain pneumatic relays and Hall-effect stem position feedback minimize hysteresis and linearize dynamic response. In this laboratory experiment, an instrumented 2-inch pneumatic diaphragm globe control valve operating under constant differential pressure (ΔP_v = 1.00 bar) is tested across lift setpoints from 10.0% to 60.0% to record ascending and descending flow rates, quantify stem hysteresis, and determine the linear flow capacity slope S = 0.8500 (m^3/h)/%.

Q = S · x = 0.8500 · x

The governing phenomenological transport or equilibrium relationship was linearized into the coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Valve stem lift signal x (%), and the dependent coordinate y represents Volumetric water flow rate Q (m^3/h). The gradient m directly determines Linear Valve Flow Capacity Slope S, while the intercept b accounts for baseline tare values or reference potentials. Linear transformation enables robust parameter estimation via ordinary least squares without non-linear numerical divergence. Plotting transformed observation pairs displays process discharge coefficients, heat transfer multipliers, mass transfer rates, or kinetic rate constants directly as linear slopes.

5. Method

  1. Set supply air to 2.5 bar; run automated positioner travel calibration to align 4.00 mA with 0.0% lift and 20.00 mA with 100.0% lift.
  2. Start recirculation pump; adjust upstream bypass regulator to establish constant differential pressure ΔP_v = 1.00 bar across the valve.
  3. Step positioner signal upward through x = 10.0%, 20.0%, 30.0%, 40.0%, 50.0%, and 60.0%; hold 30 s at each plateau and record flow rate Q_asc.
  4. Step positioner signal downward through x = 60.0%, 50.0%, 40.0%, 30.0%, 20.0%, and 10.0%; record descending flow rate Q_desc.
  5. Compute hysteresis difference ΔQ_hys = |Q_asc - Q_desc| at each lift setpoint; calculate maximum hysteresis as % of rated span.
  6. Compute mean flow rates Q_mean; plot Q versus lift signal x and fit linear regression to extract capacity slope S.

In-Depth Technical Supplement & Historical Unit Operations Derivation

1. Objective & Practical Significance

This laboratory investigation determines the inherent flow characteristic, linear flow capacity slope ($S$), rated maximum capacity ($Q_{\text{max}}$), and mechanical stem packing hysteresis of a 2-inch pneumatic diaphragm globe control valve operating under constant differential pressure ($\Delta P_v = 1.00\text{ bar}$) on water. Across stem lift control signals $x = 10.0\%$ to $60.0\%$ during ascending and descending strokes, we evaluate flow rates using an electromagnetic flowmeter, verify the linear model $Q = S \cdot x$, determine slope $S = 0.8500\text{ (m}^3\text{/h)/\%}$, and quantify stem packing hysteresis.

2. Governing Transport Equations & Phenomenological Relations

Flow capacity under constant differential pressure obeys the linear inherent trim relation:

\[ Q = S \cdot x = 0.8500 \cdot x \]

where $S = 0.8500\text{ (m}^3\text{/h)/\%}$. Rated full-stroke capacity ($x = 100\%$) is $Q_{\text{max}} = 85.00\text{ m}^3\text{/h}$. Valve flow coefficient is: $C_v = \frac{Q}{N_1} \sqrt{\frac{\text{SG}}{\Delta P_v}}$. Maximum hysteresis is $H_{\text{max}} = \max |Q_{\text{asc}} - Q_{\text{desc}}|$.

3. Experimental Apparatus & Instrumentation Specification

The test rig comprises a 2-inch ANSI Class 300 globe valve with linear contoured plug, a pneumatic diaphragm actuator ($A_d = 450\text{ cm}^2$), an electro-pneumatic positioner with non-contact Hall-effect stem sensor ($\pm 0.01\text{ mm}$), an in-line electromagnetic flowmeter (Krohne Optiflux, $\pm 0.15\%$), and a Rosemount 3051CD differential pressure transmitter ($\pm 0.005\text{ bar}$) regulating $\Delta P_v = 1.000\text{ bar}$.

4. Standard Operating Procedure & Measurement Protocols

Instrument air was set to $2.5\text{ bar}$. The positioner was calibrated ($4.0\text{ mA} = 0.0\%$, $20.0\text{ mA} = 100.0\%$). Water was recirculated, and $\Delta P_v$ was locked at $1.000\text{ bar}$. The valve was stroked upward through $x = 10.0, 20.0, 30.0, 40.0, 50.0,$ and $60.0\%$, holding $30\text{ s}$ per point to log ascending flow $Q_{\text{asc}}$. The valve was then stroked downward through the same setpoints to log descending flow $Q_{\text{desc}}$. Mean flows and hysteresis spans were evaluated.

Pneumatic actuators exert dangerous pinch forces; hand contact with open stem couplings was strictly prohibited. High-pressure water lines were equipped with relief valves set to $6.0\text{ bar}$. Safety glasses, steel-toed footwear, and ear protection were worn.

11. Self-Assessment Conceptual Questions & Model Answers

Q1: When is an equal percentage valve preferred over a linear valve?
A1: When system pressure drop across the valve decreases significantly as flow increases (valve authority $P_R < 0.3$), or in heat exchangers where heat transfer rate is non-linear with flow; an equal percentage trim compensates for line pressure drop to produce an overall linear installed characteristic.

Q2: What is valve authority and how does it affect control stability?
A2: Valve authority $P_R = \Delta P_{v,\text{max}} / \Delta P_{\text{total}}$ is the ratio of valve pressure drop to total system pressure drop; low valve authority ($P_R < 0.25$) distorts the valve curve and reduces control sensitivity, while high authority ($P_R > 0.5$) ensures tight linear controllability.
