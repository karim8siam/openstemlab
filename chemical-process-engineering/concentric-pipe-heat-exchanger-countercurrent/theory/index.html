Chemical engineering transport & equilibrium foundations of Double-Pipe Heat Exchanger.

The concentric double-pipe heat exchanger represents the simplest, most fundamental tubular heat transfer device, consisting of one pipe positioned coaxially inside a larger pipe. Fluid 1 flows through the inner tube, while Fluid 2 flows through the annular space between the two pipes. Because of its straightforward geometry, it provides an ideal platform for directly comparing co-current (parallel) and counter-current flow arrangements. In co-current flow, both hot and cold fluids enter at the same end and travel in the same direction; the maximum attainable exit temperature of the cold stream is thermodynamically limited by the hot fluid exit temperature. In counter-current flow, the fluids enter at opposite ends and flow in reverse directions, maintaining a favorable, nearly uniform temperature difference throughout the exchanger length, enabling the cold outlet temperature to surpass the hot outlet temperature. Thermal duty q transferred between streams is governed by the enthalpy balance: q = m_dot_c · c_p,c · (T_c,out - T_c,in) = U · A · ΔT_lm. In this laboratory experiment, a 2.0-meter double-pipe heat exchanger is operated with hot water in the inner tube and cooling water in the outer annulus across flow rates from 3.0 to 18.0 L/min to quantify the linear thermal duty sensitivity S and compare co-current vs counter-current heat recovery.

1. Concentric Double-Pipe Heat Exchanger Mechanics

The concentric double-pipe heat exchanger represents the canonical geometry for analyzing convective heat transfer in chemical processing. Consisting of two nested coaxial pipes, the design permits pure co-current or pure counter-current flow with zero crossflow mixing or baffle bypass.

2. First Law Enthalpy Balance & Heat Transfer Rate

Assuming adiabatic outer boundaries (negligible heat loss to ambient air), steady state, and constant fluid specific heat capacities $c_{p,h}$ and $c_{p,c}$, the thermal duty $q$ transferred from the inner hot stream to the annular cold stream is:

\[ q = \dot{m}_h c_{p,h} (T_{h,in} - T_{h,out}) = \dot{m}_c c_{p,c} (T_{c,out} - T_{c,in}) \]

where $\dot{m} = \rho V$ is mass flow rate. The overall heat transfer rate is governed by Newton's cooling law:

\[ q = U A \Delta T_{lm} \]

where $A = \pi D_o L$ is the outer surface area of the inner pipe and $\Delta T_{lm}$ is the log mean temperature difference.

3. Comparison of Co-Current vs Counter-Current Regimes

The terminal temperature differences for the two configurations are defined as:

  • Counter-Current Flow: $\Delta T_1 = T_{h,in} - T_{c,out}$ and $\Delta T_2 = T_{h,out} - T_{c,in}$. The temperature profiles slope in opposite directions, preventing local pinch bottlenecks and maximizing $\Delta T_{lm}$.
  • Co-Current Flow: $\Delta T_1 = T_{h,in} - T_{c,in}$ and $\Delta T_2 = T_{h,out} - T_{c,out}$. The driving force decreases rapidly along the tube, and cold fluid temperature cannot exceed hot outlet temperature.

4. Annular Flow Heat Transfer & Sensitivity Model

In the outer annular space, the hydraulic diameter is $D_h = D_2 - D_1$. Increasing cooling water flow rate $V_c$ elevates annular fluid velocity, thinning the laminar thermal boundary layer on the outer surface of the copper pipe. Over the operational flow envelope, counter-current thermal duty $q$ scales linearly with cooling water flow rate $V_c$:

\[ q = S \cdot V_c \]

where $S$ is the thermal duty flow sensitivity slope in $\text{kW/(L/min)}$.

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

Why is the counter-current arrangement thermodynamically superior to co-current flow?

Counter-current flow maintains a nearly uniform temperature gradient along the entire heat exchanger, maximizing the log mean temperature difference (LMTD) and allowing the cold stream to be heated above the hot stream outlet temperature.

What is the theoretical maximum thermal efficiency in a co-current heat exchanger with equal heat capacity rates?

In co-current flow with equal heat capacity rates (C_h = C_c), the maximum attainable effectiveness is 50%, because the two streams reach an identical equilibrium temperature at infinite length.

How is the hydraulic diameter D_h calculated for the annular space of a double-pipe exchanger?

For an annulus between outer pipe inner diameter D_2 and inner pipe outer diameter D_1, wetted perimeter is π(D_2 + D_1) and area is π(D_2^2 - D_1^2)/4; hydraulic diameter is D_h = 4 A / P_w = D_2 - D_1.

What is fouling resistance and how does it develop in double-pipe heat exchangers?

Fouling is the accumulation of mineral scale, biological slime, or corrosion products on tube walls, introducing an additional conductive resistance that degrades overall heat transfer over time.

How does inner tube material thermal conductivity impact overall heat transfer coefficient U?

High-conductivity metals (e.g., copper k ≈ 390 W/(m·K)) minimize wall conductive resistance to negligible levels (< 1% of total), ensuring convective boundary layers govern thermal resistance.

When are double-pipe heat exchangers preferred over shell-and-tube units in industry?

They are preferred for small duty applications (area < 20 m^2), high-pressure process streams (easier to contain in small-diameter pipes), and when true counter-current performance without F_T penalties is essential.

Experimental precautions and process safety

  • Ensure both inner tube and annulus are completely vented of air bubbles before taking temperature readings.
  • Maintain hot water inlet temperature strictly constant at 60.0 °C throughout all cooling water flow changes.
  • Allow at least 3 minutes of thermal stabilization after each flow adjustment before recording data.
  • Verify zero external leakage at the stuffing box end seals where the inner tube penetrates the outer jacket.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The transport and thermodynamic models apply within the specified calibrated operating window (3.0 to 18.0 L/min).
  • Assumes ideal continuum flow and homogeneous phase mixing without unmodeled parasitic wall losses.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • High-temperature or extreme concentration excursions require non-ideal equation-of-state corrections.
