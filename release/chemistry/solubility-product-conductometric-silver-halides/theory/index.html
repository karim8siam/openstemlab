Theoretical Foundations of Conductometric Solubility Product K_sp and Quantitative Chemical Metrology

The solubility product constant K_sp is a fundamental thermodynamic equilibrium property that quantifies the extent of dissolution of sparingly soluble ionic salts. For sparingly soluble compounds such as silver chloride (AgCl), classical gravimetric and titrimetric methods lack the sensitivity required to detect the sub-micromolar quantities of dissolved ions. High-precision electrolytic conductometry overcomes this limitation by detecting ionic charge carriers down to parts-per-billion levels with exceptional sensitivity. In this laboratory, the electrolytic conductivity of saturated silver chloride solutions is measured across a regulated temperature series (20.00 °C to 45.00 °C) using a platinized platinum cell and a 1.0 kHz AC bridge. By combining background-corrected conductivities with temperature-dependent limiting molar conductivities, molar solubilities s and solubility products K_sp are determined, and the standard enthalpy of dissolution (ΔH°_sol) is extracted from the van 't Hoff plot with sub-0.1% accuracy.

TOPIC 01 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

What is the physical principle of determining solubility products via conductometry, and why is the infinite-dilution approximation exact for sparingly soluble salts?

The quantitative determination of the thermodynamic solubility product constant K_sp for sparingly soluble salts—such as silver halides (AgCl, AgBr, AgI), barium sulfate (BaSO₄), and lead sulfate (PbSO₄)—presents a severe analytical challenge for classical gravimetric and titrimetric methods. Because the dissolved solute concentrations in saturated solutions of these salts are minuscule (typically 10⁻⁵ M to 10⁻⁸ M), the mass of dissolved material in standard laboratory volumes is below the detection threshold of conventional analytical balances.



Electrolytic conductometry provides an exceptionally sensitive, non-destructive physical method capable of measuring sub-micromolar ionic concentrations with precision better than ±0.1%. When an insoluble binary salt M_ν+ X_ν- is placed in contact with pure water, heterogeneous dissolution proceeds until dynamic chemical equilibrium is established:



AgCl(s) ⇄ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)



The thermodynamic equilibrium constant is the solubility product K_sp:



K_sp = a(Ag⁺) · a(Cl⁻) = [Ag⁺] · [Cl⁻] · γ_Ag · γ_Cl



where a_i represents the chemical activity, [i] is molar concentration (mol·L⁻¹), and γ_i is the ionic activity coefficient. If the molar solubility of silver chloride is denoted by s (mol·L⁻¹), the stoichiometric concentrations at equilibrium are:



[Ag⁺] = s and [Cl⁻] = s



Because silver chloride is extraordinarily insoluble, its saturated concentration at 25 °C is only s ≈ 1.33 × 10⁻⁵ M. The corresponding ionic strength of the saturated solution is:



I = (1/2) [ (1.33 × 10⁻⁵)(+1)² + (1.33 × 10⁻⁵)(-1)² ] = 1.33 × 10⁻⁵ M



According to the Debye-Hückel limiting law, the mean ionic activity coefficient γ_± in aqueous solution at 25 °C is:



log₁₀(γ_±) = -0.509 · |z_+ z_-| · √I = -0.509 · (1) · √(1.33 × 10⁻⁵) = -0.509 × (0.00365) = -0.00186

γ_± = 10^(-0.00186) ≈ 0.9957



The activity coefficient deviates from unity by less than 0.43%! Consequently, the solution behaves as an essentially ideal thermodynamic system at infinite dilution. Interionic electrostatic forces (Debye-Hückel relaxation and electrophoretic drag) are negligible, and activity can be equated directly to concentration:



K_sp = s²



Furthermore, because the solution is at virtually infinite dilution, the actual molar conductivity Λ_m of the dissolved ions is strictly equal to the limiting molar conductivity at infinite dilution, Λ_m°:



Λ_m = Λ_m° = λ°(Ag⁺) + λ°(Cl⁻)



Under Kohlrausch's law of independent ionic migration, the limiting molar conductivity is calculated from certified ionic conductivities (at 25 °C: λ°(Ag⁺) = 61.90 S·cm²·mol⁻¹, λ°(Cl⁻) = 76.35 S·cm²·mol⁻¹, yielding Λ_m° = 138.25 S·cm²·mol⁻¹). The specific electrolytic conductivity of the dissolved salt κ_AgCl (in S·cm⁻¹) is related to molar solubility s by:



κ_AgCl = (s · Λ_m°) / 1000



Solving directly for molar solubility:



s = (1000 · κ_AgCl) / Λ_m° = (1000 · [κ_sat - κ_w]) / Λ_m°



and the solubility product is:



K_sp = s² = [ (1000 · [κ_sat - κ_w]) / Λ_m° ]²



This simple, elegant formula allows direct extraction of K_sp from a single electrical conductance measurement without requiring chemical perturbation or optical labeling.

TOPIC 02 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

Why is the solvent background conductivity correction (κ_sat - κ_w) critical, and what are the sources of solvent background conductance?

In conductometric investigations of sparingly soluble salts, the solvent background conductivity correction is of paramount importance because the electrical conductance contributed by the dissolved salt is of the exact same order of magnitude as the intrinsic background conductance of the solvent water itself. Failure to perform rigorous background subtraction introduces catastrophic systematic errors exceeding 5% to 50% into the calculated solubility product K_sp.



Consider the quantitative numbers for saturated silver chloride at 25 °C:

- Saturated solution specific conductivity: κ_sat ≈ 1.890 μS·cm⁻¹

- Net conductivity of dissolved Ag⁺ and Cl⁻: κ_AgCl ≈ 1.839 μS·cm⁻¹

- Solvent background conductivity: κ_w ≈ 0.051 μS·cm⁻¹



If the experimenter were to neglect the solvent background conductivity and use κ_sat directly instead of (κ_sat - κ_w):



s_uncorrected = (1000 × 1.890 × 10⁻⁶) / 138.25 = 1.367 × 10⁻⁵ M

K_sp,uncorrected = (1.367 × 10⁻⁵)² = 1.869 × 10⁻¹⁰ M²



Because K_sp is proportional to the square of conductivity (K_sp ∝ κ²), an error of only +2.7% in conductivity propagates into a +5.6% error in K_sp. For even less soluble salts such as silver bromide (AgBr, s ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁷ M, κ_AgBr ≈ 0.09 μS/cm) or silver iodide (AgI, s ≈ 1 × 10⁻⁸ M, κ_AgI ≈ 0.001 μS/cm), the solvent background completely dwarfs the salt signal, making rigorous subtraction and solvent purification indispensable.



There are two primary physical and chemical sources of solvent background conductivity κ_w in laboratory water:



1. Self-Ionization of Water (The Ultimate Physical Limit):

Pure liquid water undergoes auto-protolysis:



2 H₂O(l) ⇄ H₃O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) (K_w = 1.008 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25.0 °C)



At 25 °C, the equilibrium concentrations are [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.004 × 10⁻⁷ M. Using the limiting ionic conductivities λ°(H⁺) = 349.8 S·cm²·mol⁻¹ and λ°(OH⁻) = 198.6 S·cm²·mol⁻¹:



κ_ideal_water = (1 / 1000) · [ (1.004 × 10⁻⁷) × 349.8 + (1.004 × 10⁻⁷) × 198.6 ] = 5.50 × 10⁻⁸ S·cm⁻¹ = 0.055 μS·cm⁻¹



This represents the theoretical lower limit of electrical conductivity for 100% chemically pure water (corresponding to a maximum resistivity ρ = 1 / κ = 18.18 MΩ·cm).



2. Atmospheric Carbon Dioxide Dissolution:

When ultrapure water is exposed to ambient air, atmospheric carbon dioxide (P_CO2 ≈ 420 ppm) dissolves rapidly according to Henry's law and hydrolyzes to form carbonic acid:



CO₂(g) ⇄ CO₂(aq)

CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇄ H₂CO₃(aq) ⇄ H⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq) (K_a1 = 4.45 × 10⁻⁷)



In unsealed or non-degassed water, dissolved CO₂ generates [H⁺] ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ M (pH ≈ 5.6), increasing the background conductivity from 0.055 μS/cm up to 0.8 to 1.2 μS/cm! Such high contamination would destroy conductometric AgCl measurements.



Therefore, experimental protocol requires: (a) boiling and purging ultrapure Milli-Q water with high-purity nitrogen gas to expel dissolved CO₂; (b) measuring the solvent baseline conductivity κ_w in the thermostatic cell immediately prior to adding the salt; and (c) subtracting κ_w(T) at every individual measurement temperature.

TOPIC 03 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

How does the van 't Hoff equation describe the temperature dependence of K_sp, and how is the enthalpy of dissolution extracted?

The thermodynamic temperature dependence of any chemical equilibrium constant—including the solubility product K_sp—is rigorously described by the van 't Hoff equation, derived from the fundamental Gibbs-Helmholtz relation of classical thermodynamics. The standard Gibbs free energy change for the dissolution of a sparingly soluble salt:



AgCl(s) ⇄ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)



is related to the thermodynamic equilibrium constant K_sp by:



ΔG°_sol = - R · T · ln(K_sp)



From the fundamental thermodynamic definition of Gibbs free energy in terms of standard enthalpy of solution ΔH°_sol and standard entropy of solution ΔS°_sol:



ΔG°_sol = ΔH°_sol - T · ΔS°_sol



Equating the two expressions:



- R · T · ln(K_sp) = ΔH°_sol - T · ΔS°_sol



Dividing both sides by (- R · T) yields the integrated van 't Hoff equation:



ln(K_sp) = - (ΔH°_sol / R) · (1 / T) + (ΔS°_sol / R)



Over moderate temperature intervals (such as 20 °C to 45 °C, where ΔT = 25 K), the standard enthalpy change ΔH°_sol and standard entropy change ΔS°_sol are essentially temperature-independent because the difference in heat capacities between the solvated ions and crystalline solid (ΔC_p° = C_p°(Ag⁺) + C_p°(Cl⁻) - C_p°(AgCl)) is small (~15 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹).



Consequently, when the natural logarithm of the experimental solubility product, ln(K_sp), is plotted against the reciprocal absolute temperature, 1/T (or scaled as 1000/T for computational convenience):



ln(K_sp) = - [ ΔH°_sol / (1000 · R) ] · (1000 / T) + (ΔS°_sol / R)



the resulting van 't Hoff plot forms a strict straight line:



y = m · x + b



where the abscissa is x = 1000 / T (in K⁻¹), the ordinate is y = ln(K_sp), the slope is m = - ΔH°_sol / (1000 · R), and the Y-intercept is b = ΔS°_sol / R.



From the empirical slope m determined via ordinary linear least-squares regression, the standard enthalpy of dissolution is directly isolated:



ΔH°_sol = - m · 1000 · R (in J·mol⁻¹, or divided by 1000 to yield kJ·mol⁻¹)



and the standard entropy of dissolution is extracted from the Y-intercept:



ΔS°_sol = b · R (in J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)



For silver chloride, dissolution is an endothermic process (ΔH°_sol = +65.50 kJ·mol⁻¹). Breaking the stable AgCl rocksalt ionic crystal lattice requires substantial energy (lattice enthalpy ΔH_latt = +916 kJ·mol⁻¹), which is only partially compensated by the exothermic hydration enthalpies of the gaseous silver and chloride ions (ΔH_hyd(Ag⁺) = -473 kJ·mol⁻¹, ΔH_hyd(Cl⁻) = -377 kJ·mol⁻¹, net ΔH_hyd = -850 kJ·mol⁻¹):



ΔH°_sol = ΔH_latt + ΔH_hyd = 916 - 850 = +66 kJ·mol⁻¹



Because dissolution is endothermic (ΔH°_sol > 0), Le Chatelier's principle dictates that raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium to the right, favoring dissolution. Molar solubility s and solubility product K_sp increase significantly with temperature: K_sp rises from 1.13 × 10⁻¹⁰ at 20 °C to 1.77 × 10⁻¹⁰ at 25 °C, and reaches 9.32 × 10⁻¹⁰ at 45 °C—an eight-fold increase across 25 °C! This massive temperature dependence manifests as a steep negative slope in the van 't Hoff plot (m = -7.877 K).

TOPIC 04 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

How are the limiting ionic conductivities λ°(Ag⁺) and λ°(Cl⁻) adjusted for temperature across 20 °C to 45 °C?

To calculate the molar solubility s = (1000 · κ_AgCl) / Λ_m°(T) accurately across a range of temperatures (20.0 °C to 45.0 °C), the limiting molar conductivity of silver chloride Λ_m° cannot be treated as a constant; it must be corrected for its intrinsic temperature dependence.



As established by Kohlrausch and Walden, limiting ionic conductivities increase substantially with temperature because the dynamic shear viscosity of liquid water η decreases rapidly with temperature (following an exponential Arrhenius relation with activation energy E_visc ≈ 15.0 kJ·mol⁻¹). Over the experimental temperature interval 20 °C to 45 °C, the temperature dependence of limiting ionic conductivities is accurately modeled using linear temperature coefficients α_25:



λ°_i(T) = λ°_i(25) · [ 1 + α_i · (T - 25.0) ]



For the constituent ions of silver chloride:

- Silver cation (Ag⁺): λ°(25 °C) = 61.90 S·cm²·mol⁻¹; temperature coefficient α(Ag⁺) = 0.0229 K⁻¹ (+2.29% per °C)

- Chloride anion (Cl⁻): λ°(25 °C) = 76.35 S·cm²·mol⁻¹; temperature coefficient α(Cl⁻) = 0.0216 K⁻¹ (+2.16% per °C)



The limiting molar conductivity of silver chloride is the sum:



Λ_m°(25 °C) = 61.90 + 76.35 = 138.25 S·cm²·mol⁻¹



Combining the ionic coefficients yields an effective composite temperature coefficient for AgCl of α_comp = 0.0220 K⁻¹ (+2.20% per °C):



Λ_m°(T) = 138.25 · [ 1 + 0.0220 · (T - 25.0) ]



Evaluating Λ_m°(T) at our six experimental temperatures:

- At 20.0 °C (T - 25 = -5 K): Λ_m° = 138.25 × [1 - 0.1100] = 123.04 S·cm²·mol⁻¹

- At 25.0 °C (T - 25 = 0 K): Λ_m° = 138.25 S·cm²·mol⁻¹

- At 30.0 °C (T - 25 = +5 K): Λ_m° = 138.25 × [1 + 0.1100] = 153.46 S·cm²·mol⁻¹

- At 35.0 °C (T - 25 = +10 K): Λ_m° = 138.25 × [1 + 0.2200] = 168.67 S·cm²·mol⁻¹

- At 40.0 °C (T - 25 = +15 K): Λ_m° = 138.25 × [1 + 0.3300] = 183.87 S·cm²·mol⁻¹

- At 45.0 °C (T - 25 = +20 K): Λ_m° = 138.25 × [1 + 0.4400] = 199.08 S·cm²·mol⁻¹



Between 20 °C and 45 °C, Λ_m° increases by more than 61%! If an experimenter were to erroneously use the 25 °C value (138.25) at 45 °C, the calculated solubility would be overestimated by 44%, resulting in an error exceeding 100% in K_sp. Applying the exact temperature-corrected Λ_m°(T) ensures that the observed increase in specific conductivity κ reflects the true thermodynamic shift in solubility equilibrium.

TOPIC 05 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

How do the silver halides (AgCl, AgBr, AgI) compare in solubility product and covalent character?

The silver halide series—silver fluoride (AgF), silver chloride (AgCl), silver bromide (AgBr), and silver iodide (AgI)—exhibits one of the most celebrated periodic trends in inorganic and physical chemistry, illustrating the profound transition from ionic to covalent bonding and its dramatic impact on thermodynamic solubility.



1. Silver Fluoride (AgF):

Fluoride is a small, hard, non-polarizable halide anion (Fajans' rules). The bonding in AgF is predominantly ionic, and the high hydration enthalpy of fluoride (ΔH_hyd = -507 kJ·mol⁻¹) easily overcomes the lattice energy. Consequently, AgF is exceptionally soluble in water: its solubility is ~14 M (1800 g/L) at 25 °C!



2. Silver Chloride (AgCl):

Chloride possesses a larger ionic radius (181 pm) and higher polarizability. The polarizable d¹⁰ electron shell of the Ag⁺ cation induces significant mutual covalent polarization with the chloride electron cloud. This covalent character stabilizes the crystal lattice, rendering AgCl sparingly soluble:

- K_sp(25 °C) = 1.77 × 10⁻¹⁰ M²

- Molar solubility s = 1.33 × 10⁻⁵ M (1.91 mg/L)

- pK_sp = 9.75



3. Silver Bromide (AgBr):

Bromide has an even larger radius (196 pm) and significantly higher polarizability. Covalent orbital overlap between Ag⁺ 4d/5s orbitals and Br⁻ 4p orbitals increases dramatically. The salt exhibits a pale yellow color due to metal-to-ligand charge transfer, and its solubility drops by nearly three orders of magnitude:

- K_sp(25 °C) = 5.35 × 10⁻¹³ M²

- Molar solubility s = 7.31 × 10⁻⁷ M (0.137 mg/L)

- pK_sp = 12.27



4. Silver Iodide (AgI):

Iodide is massive (220 pm) and soft, with an extraordinarily polarizable electron cloud. In AgI, bonding is predominantly covalent, adopting a zincblende or wurtzite tetrahedral network rather than the octahedral rocksalt lattice of AgCl. The intense covalent stabilization drives solubility to ultra-trace levels:

- K_sp(25 °C) = 8.52 × 10⁻¹⁷ M²

- Molar solubility s = 9.23 × 10⁻⁹ M (0.0021 mg/L = 2.1 μg/L)

- pK_sp = 16.07



This colossal drop in solubility across seven orders of magnitude (from K_sp = 10⁻¹⁰ for AgCl down to 10⁻¹⁷ for AgI) directly reflects Fajans' rules of polarization: soft polarizable cations paired with soft polarizable anions yield intense covalent lattice stabilization. In conductometry, while AgCl is easily measured on standard micro-Siemens bridges (κ ≈ 1.89 μS/cm), AgI produces conductivities (< 0.002 μS/cm) that fall below the background conductivity of water, demonstrating why AgCl is the gold-standard benchmark for conductometric solubility investigations.

TOPIC 06 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

What common experimental artifacts can corrupt conductometric K_sp determinations, and how are they eliminated?

Achieving analytical accuracy in conductometric solubility product determinations requires rigorous elimination of four major experimental artifacts:



1. Incomplete Removal of Soluble Ionic Precursors:

Silver chloride is synthesized by precipitating AgNO₃ with KCl. If the precipitate is not thoroughly washed, trace residues of soluble ions (K⁺, NO₃⁻, excess Ag⁺ or Cl⁻) will remain adsorbed on the crystal surfaces. Because a concentration of only 10⁻⁴ M KNO₃ would contribute ~14 μS/cm of conductance (ten times the entire AgCl signal!), unwashed precipitate will yield catastrophically inflated apparent solubilities. Elimination: The AgCl precipitate must be digested in hot water and washed by centrifugation at least six times with boiling ultrapure water until the conductivity of the wash supernatant matches that of pure water.



2. Photolytic Decomposition of Silver Chloride:

Silver halides are famously photosensitive. Exposure to ambient room light (especially ultraviolet and blue wavelengths) triggers photolytic reduction:



2 AgCl(s) + hν → 2 Ag(s) + Cl₂(g)



Photolysis produces colloidal metallic silver particles (imparting a purple-gray discoloration to the solid) and releases free chloride and hypochlorite ions into solution, artificially increasing conductance. Elimination: Precipitation, washing, and measurement must be conducted in amber glassware or light-shielded vessels under dim red photographic darkroom illumination.



3. Non-Equilibrium Dissolution and Particle Size Effects (Ostwald Ripening):

Dissolution of a sparingly soluble salt is a heterogeneous surface-limited process. If sufficient equilibration time is not provided, the solution will remain undersaturated. Furthermore, according to the Ostwald-Freundlich equation, colloidal nanoparticles (< 100 nm) possess elevated surface free energies and exhibit higher solubilities than macroscopic bulk crystals:



ln( s_r / s_bulk ) = (2 · γ_sl · V_m) / (r · R · T)



Elimination: The precipitate must be thermally digested at 70 °C for two hours prior to use (coarsening nanoparticles into stable micro-crystals), and the reaction mixture must be stirred at 300 RPM for at least 20 minutes at each temperature plateau to guarantee thermodynamic equilibrium.



4. Cell Polarization and Alternating Current Frequency:

Using direct current or improper AC bridge frequencies causes capacitive double-layer polarization at the platinum electrodes. Elimination: The dip cell must be platinized with platinum black (increasing real surface area 1000-fold) and excited with high-frequency audio AC (1.0 kHz), with automatic phase-angle quadrature nulling.

TOPIC 07 · FORMAL ANALYSIS

How are ΔH°_sol, ΔS°_sol, and ΔG°_sol extracted via linear regression, and how do their uncertainties propagate?

The quantitative thermodynamic analysis of temperature-dependent solubility product data requires rigorous linear least-squares regression followed by full covariance error propagation. For N experimental temperature coordinates (T_i, κ_sat,i):



1. Data Reduction and Coordinate Mapping:

At each temperature T_i (in Kelvin), the net salt conductivity is obtained by subtracting the solvent background:



κ_AgCl,i = κ_sat,i - κ_w,i



The molar solubility s_i and solubility product K_sp,i are calculated using the temperature-corrected limiting molar conductivity Λ_m°(T_i):



s_i = (1000 · κ_AgCl,i) / Λ_m°(T_i)

K_sp,i = s_i²



The coordinates for the linear van 't Hoff plot are:



x_i = 1000.0 / T_i (in K⁻¹)

y_i = ln(K_sp,i) (dimensionless)



2. Ordinary Least-Squares Fit:

The data are fitted to the straight line y = m · x + b using standard closed-form regression formulas:



m = [ N ∑(x_i y_i) - (∑x_i)(∑y_i) ] / [ N ∑(x_i²) - (∑x_i)² ]

b = [ ∑y_i - m ∑x_i ] / N



3. Extraction of Thermodynamic State Functions:

From the slope m and intercept b, the standard enthalpy, entropy, and Gibbs free energy of solution at 25 °C (298.15 K) are computed:



ΔH°_sol = - m · 1000 · R / 1000 = - m · R (in kJ·mol⁻¹)

ΔS°_sol = b · R (in J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)

ΔG°_sol(298.15) = ΔH°_sol - (298.15 · ΔS°_sol / 1000) = - R · (298.15) · ln(K_sp,298.15) (in kJ·mol⁻¹)



4. Uncertainty Propagation and Confidence Intervals:

The standard error of the slope s_m and intercept s_b are derived from the residual variance s_yx²:



s_yx = √[ ∑(y_i - (m x_i + b))² / (N - 2) ]

s_m = s_yx / √[ ∑(x_i - x_mean)² ]

s_b = s_yx · √[ ∑(x_i²) / ( N ∑(x_i²) - (∑x_i)² ) ]



The standard uncertainty in the enthalpy of dissolution is:



u(ΔH°_sol) = s_m · R



and the expanded uncertainty at the 95% confidence level (coverage factor k = 2) is:



U(ΔH°_sol) = 2 · u(ΔH°_sol)



For our standardized AgCl dataset spanning six temperatures (20.0 °C to 45.0 °C):

- Fitted slope: m = -7.877 ± 0.015 K

- Fitted intercept: b = 3.966 ± 0.048

- Coefficient of determination: R² = 0.999999

- Standard enthalpy of solution: ΔH°_sol = 65.49 ± 0.12 kJ·mol⁻¹ (matching benchmark 65.50 kJ·mol⁻¹ within 0.009%)

- Standard entropy of solution: ΔS°_sol = +32.98 ± 0.40 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹

- Standard Gibbs free energy at 25 °C: ΔG°_sol = +55.66 ± 0.15 kJ·mol⁻¹

- Extrapolated solubility product at 25 °C: K_sp = 1.771 × 10⁻¹⁰ M² (pK_sp = 9.75)



These results provide authoritative, highly precise verification of the fundamental thermodynamics of electrolyte dissolution.

Connect the theory to experimental observations.

The thermodynamics of sparingly soluble salt dissolution is governed by the van 't Hoff relationship: ln(K_sp) = -(ΔH°_sol / R)(1/T) + ΔS°_sol / R. For silver chloride, dissolution is endothermic (ΔH°_sol = +65.50 kJ·mol⁻¹) due to the high crystal lattice enthalpy (916 kJ/mol) relative to ion hydration enthalpies (-850 kJ/mol). Because the saturated solution is at virtually infinite dilution (ionic strength I ≈ 1.3 × 10⁻⁵ M, activity coefficient γ_± > 0.995), molar conductivity equals the limiting molar conductivity Λ_m°(T) = 138.25 [1 + 0.0220(T - 25.0)] S·cm²·mol⁻¹. Saturated solution conductivity κ_sat increases from 1.352 μS/cm at 20 °C to 6.148 μS/cm at 45 °C. After subtracting the ultrapure water background κ_w, solubility product values track the van 't Hoff equation with R² > 0.99999, yielding an experimental enthalpy of dissolution agreeing within 0.01% of the certified literature benchmark.
