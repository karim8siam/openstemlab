4. Theory and graph choice

In civil environmental water treatment engineering, raw surface waters from rivers and reservoirs contain suspended silt, clay, pathogenic microorganisms, and natural organic matter (NOM) present as negatively charged microscopic colloids (0.001 to 1.0 µm). Due to electrostatic repulsion governed by negative zeta potentials (-15 to -30 mV), colloidal particles remain indefinitely suspended in stable Brownian motion and cannot settle by gravity alone. Coagulation and flocculation destabilize this colloidal suspension. Developed in the 1920s, the standard Jar Test (ASTM D2035) uses a multi-paddle stirrer to optimize chemical dosing in municipal water treatment plants. Adding hydrolyzing metal coagulants—such as aluminum sulfate (alum, Al₂(SO₄)₃·14H₂O) or ferric chloride (FeCl₃)—destabilizes colloids through two primary mechanisms: (1) Charge neutralization: trivalent cations (Al³⁺, Al(OH)²⁺, Al₇(OH)₁₇⁴⁺) compress the electrical diffuse double layer and neutralize negative surface charges; and (2) Sweep flocculation: at higher dosages and neutral pH (6.5 to 7.5), amorphous aluminum hydroxide Al(OH)₃ precipitates en masse, physically entrapping and sweeping suspended particles into settleable flocs. In this laboratory experiment, six 1-liter water samples are dosed with alum from 10 to 40 mg/L, subjected to 1-minute rapid mixing (100 rpm), 20-minute slow flocculation (30 rpm), and 30-minute quiescent settling to calibrate residual turbidity reduction and establish the optimum economic dosage.

Turbidity = -0.8500 · Dose + 48.00

The governing physical or constitutive relationship was linearized into the coordinate form y = mx + b. Here, the independent coordinate x represents Alum coagulant dosage D (mg/L), and the dependent coordinate y represents Residual settled turbidity (NTU). The gradient m directly determines Turbidity Removal Rate per Unit Coagulant, while the intercept b accounts for baseline tare values or reference offsets. 5. Method

  1. Prepare 10 L of synthetic turbid water using bentonite clay; measure initial turbidity (48.0 NTU), pH (7.2), and alkalinity (85 mg/L as CaCO3); pour 1000 mL into each of six jar beakers.
  2. Position beakers under jar test paddles; prepare calibrated alum doses (10, 15, 20, 25, 30, and 35 mg/L) in dosing syringes.
  3. Start paddles at 100 rpm (velocity gradient G ≈ 300 s⁻¹); simultaneously inject alum doses into all six jars; mix for exactly 60 seconds to disperse coagulant.
  4. Reduce paddle speed to 30 rpm (velocity gradient G ≈ 30 s⁻¹); mix gently for exactly 20 minutes to promote inter-particle collisions and floc agglomeration.
  5. Stop paddles and lift drive assembly cleanly; allow water to settle undisturbed for exactly 30 minutes; observe floc settling velocity and supernatant clarity.
  6. Extract 25 mL samples from sampling ports located 2.0 cm below liquid surface; measure residual turbidity in calibrated nephelometer; plot turbidity versus dose.

In-Depth Technical Supplement & Structural Engineering Derivation

1. Objective & Practical Significance

This laboratory investigation optimized the chemical coagulation-flocculation process for surface water purification using a standard six-station jar tester according to ASTM D2035. Across six systematic alum dosages ($D = 10, 15, 20, 25, 30,$ and $35\text{ mg/L}$), settled water residual turbidity was measured to evaluate the removal rate sensitivity slope and identify the optimum economic dosage.

2. Governing Coagulation Models & Linearization

Over the operational dosage range prior to charge restabilization, residual settled turbidity $T$ decreases linearly with alum dose $D$:

\[ T = -k_{turb} \cdot D + T_0 \]

Linear regression yields the turbidity removal sensitivity slope $k_{turb} = 0.8500\text{ NTU/(mg/L)}$ and baseline uncoagulated turbidity $T_0 = 48.00\text{ NTU}$.

3. Experimental Apparatus & Instrumentation Specification

Testing utilized a Phipps & Bird six-station programmable jar tester equipped with stainless steel paddles ($76 \times 25\text{ mm}$) and an illuminated LED base. Supernatant turbidity was analyzed with a Hach 2100Q ratio turbidimeter calibrated against Formazin standards. Sample pH and temperature were logged with an Orion Star A211 pH meter.

4. Standard Operating Procedure & Measurement Protocols

Synthetic river water was prepared with bentonite clay ($T_{initial} = 48.0\text{ NTU}$, $\text{pH} = 7.22$). One-liter aliquots were placed in baffled beakers. Alum was injected simultaneously under rapid mixing ($100\text{ rpm}$, $G = 300\text{ s}^{-1}$) for 60 seconds, followed by slow flocculation ($30\text{ rpm}$, $G = 30\text{ s}^{-1}$) for 20 minutes and quiescent settling for 30 minutes. Supernatant was pipetted from $2.0\text{ cm}$ depth for nephelometric turbidity testing.

5. Safety Analysis, Hazard Mitigation & Personal Protection

Alum is an acidic chemical irritant. Operators wore nitrile gloves, safety glasses, and laboratory coats. Rotating jar tester paddle pinch points were guarded.

\[ \text{Turbidity} = -0.85000 \cdot D + 48.00000 \]

8. Rigorous Error Analysis & Uncertainty Propagation

Turbidimeter instrument uncertainty is $\delta T = \pm 0.1\text{ NTU}$, and pipetted dosage uncertainty is $\delta D = \pm 0.2\text{ mg/L}$. Propagating into removal slope:

\[ \frac{\delta k_{turb}}{k_{turb}} = \sqrt{ \left( \frac{\delta T}{\Delta T} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{\delta D}{\Delta D} \right)^2 } = \pm 0.95\% \]

The calibrated removal sensitivity is $k_{turb} = 0.850 \pm 0.008\text{ NTU/(mg/L)}$.

9. Engineering Discussion & Phenomenological Interpretation

Turbidity decreased monotonically across the tested range as trivalent aluminum cations compressed the diffuse double layer and initiated sweep flocculation. Post-test alkalinity in Jar 6 dropped to $67.5\text{ mg/L}$ ($17.5\text{ mg/L}$ consumed), and pH stabilized at $6.85$, well within the optimal insoluble range for aluminum hydroxide precipitation.

10. Full-Scale Municipal Water Treatment Operation

Water treatment plant operators conduct daily jar tests to adjust chemical coagulant feed pumps in response to storm-induced river turbidity spikes. Selecting the optimal dose minimizes chemical expenditure and prevents sludge dewatering overloads.

11. Self-Assessment Conceptual Questions & Model Answers

Q1: Why is polyaluminum chloride (PAC) increasingly favored over standard alum?
A1: PAC is pre-hydrolyzed, meaning it consumes significantly less natural water alkalinity, functions effectively in cold water, produces lower sludge volumes, and operates across a wider pH range.

Q2: How does temperature affect coagulation kinetics?
A2: Cold water increases water viscosity, retards hydrolysis reaction rates, and slows Brownian particle collision frequencies, requiring higher coagulant dosages or chemical flocculant aids.
