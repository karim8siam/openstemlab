Civil engineering mechanics & structural foundations of Photoelastic Stress Concentration.

In structural and bridge engineering, holes, cutouts, notches, and re-entrant corners (such as bolt holes, access manholes, and weld copes) disrupt the uniform flow of internal stress trajectories, concentrating stresses into localized peaks several times greater than the nominal average stress. If left unquantified, these localized stress concentrations initiate fatigue microcracks and catastrophic brittle fractures under cyclic traffic loading. Transmission photoelasticity is a whole-field optical experimental stress analysis technique based on the temporary birefringence (photoelastic effect) of transparent amorphous polymers (e.g. polycarbonate or epoxy). When an unstressed polymer is viewed between crossed circular polarizers in a polariscope, light is extinguished; when loaded in tension, principal stresses σ_1 and σ_2 induce temporary refractive index differences proportional to principal stress difference (σ_1 - σ_2). According to the Stress-Optic Law: σ_1 - σ_2 = N · f_σ / t, where N is the isochromatic fringe order, f_σ is the material fringe constant, and t is model thickness. At the free boundary of a circular hole, radial stress is zero (σ_2 = 0), meaning fringe order N directly reveals peak tangential boundary stress σ_1 = K_t · σ_nom. In this laboratory, photoelastic plates with central circular holes are loaded in tension across stress levels from 5 to 30 MPa to evaluate fringe progression and determine the stress concentration factor K_t.

N = 0.2450 · σ_nom

1. Optics of Birefringence & The Photoelastic Effect

Amorphous transparent polymers such as polycarbonate and epoxy resins are optically isotropic when unstressed, possessing a uniform refractive index $n_0$. When subjected to an in-plane mechanical stress field $(\sigma_1, \sigma_2)$, secondary molecular bonds align along principal stress trajectories, inducing optical anisotropy (temporary birefringence). According to Maxwell's Stress-Optic Law, the induced principal refractive indices $n_1$ and $n_2$ scale linearly with principal stresses:

\[ n_1 - n_0 = c_1 \sigma_1 + c_2 \sigma_2, \qquad n_2 - n_0 = c_1 \sigma_2 + c_2 \sigma_1 \]

Subtracting these expressions yields the differential refractive index:

\[ n_1 - n_2 = (c_1 - c_2)(\sigma_1 - \sigma_2) = C (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2) \]

where $C$ is the stress-optic coefficient of the material.

2. Transmission Through a Circular Polariscope

When monochromatic light of wavelength $\lambda$ passes through a stressed model of thickness $t$, the two orthogonally polarized light waves emerge with a relative optical path difference $\Delta$ and phase retardance $\delta$:

\[ \Delta = t (n_1 - n_2) = C t (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2), \qquad \delta = \frac{2\pi \Delta}{\lambda} = \frac{2\pi C t}{\lambda} (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2) \]

In a dark-field circular polariscope (polarizer and analyzer crossed at $90^\circ$, with two quarter-wave plates set at $45^\circ$), the transmitted light intensity $I$ is:

\[ I = I_0 \sin^2\left( \frac{\delta}{2} \right) = I_0 \sin^2\left[ \frac{\pi C t}{\lambda} (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2) \right] \]

Complete extinction (dark isochromatic fringes) occurs whenever phase retardance is an integer multiple of $2\pi$:

\[ \frac{\pi C t}{\lambda} (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2) = N \pi \implies \sigma_1 - \sigma_2 = \frac{N \lambda}{C t} = \frac{N f_\sigma}{t} \]

where $N = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$ is the isochromatic fringe order, and $f_\sigma = \lambda / C$ is the material fringe constant.

3. Kirsch Solution & Stress Concentration Factor $K_t$

At the traction-free boundary of a circular hole ($r = R$), the radial boundary stress vanishes identically: $\sigma_r = \sigma_2 = 0$. The Stress-Optic Law directly provides the peak tangential boundary stress $\sigma_1$:

\[ \sigma_1 = \frac{N f_\sigma}{t} \]

The elastic stress concentration factor $K_t$ is defined as peak boundary stress divided by nominal gross stress $\sigma_{nom}$:

\[ K_t = \frac{\sigma_{peak}}{\sigma_{nom}} = \frac{N f_\sigma / t}{\sigma_{nom}} \implies N = \left[ \frac{K_t t}{f_\sigma} \right] \sigma_{nom} = k_{fringe} \cdot \sigma_{nom} \]

For model thickness $t = 6.0\text{ mm}$, material fringe value $f_\sigma = 60.0\text{ N/mm/fringe}$, and finite-width corrected concentration factor $K_t = 2.450$:

\[ k_{fringe} = \frac{2.450 \times 6.0\text{ mm}}{60.0\text{ N/mm/fringe}} = 0.2450\text{ fringes/MPa} \]

Viva voce questions & analytical theory

State the fundamental Stress-Optic Law for two-dimensional transmission photoelasticity.

The Stress-Optic Law states: sigma_1 - sigma_2 = N · f_sigma / t, where sigma_1 and sigma_2 are in-plane principal stresses, N is the isochromatic fringe order, f_sigma is the material fringe value, and t is model thickness.

How does the Kirsch theoretical solution explain stress concentration around a circular hole in an infinite plate?

G. Kirsch (1898) proved using elasticity theory that under uniaxial tensile stress sigma_0, the tangential boundary stress around a circular hole is sigma_theta = sigma_0 (1 - 2 cos 2theta); at the transverse equator (theta = 90°), peak stress reaches sigma_max = 3 sigma_0, yielding K_t = 3.0.

What is the physical difference between isochromatic fringes and isoclinic fringes?

Isochromatic fringes represent contours of constant principal stress difference (sigma_1 - sigma_2, or maximum shear stress); isoclinic fringes represent loci of points where principal stress directions align with the polarizer transmission axis.

Why are quarter-wave plates inserted into the polariscope to create a 'circular' polariscope?

Quarter-wave plates convert linearly polarized light into circularly polarized light, which eliminates all direction-dependent isoclinic fringes from the field of view, leaving only pure isochromatic stress-magnitude fringes.

How does finite plate width (w) modify the infinite-plate Kirsch factor K_t = 3.0?

In a plate of finite width w with hole diameter d, adjacent boundaries confine stress trajectories; finite-width correction charts (Peterson) modify K_t as a function of d/w, yielding net-section stress concentration factors between 2.2 and 2.6.

Why is photoelastic stress analysis valuable compared to numerical Finite Element Analysis (FEA)?

Photoelasticity provides physical, whole-field optical verification of actual stress gradients without relying on meshing assumptions, singularity approximations, or boundary condition simplifications.

Experimental precautions and laboratory safety

  • Machine holes and notches using sharp, coolant-flooded cutters at low feed rates to prevent thermal annealing and residual machining stresses.
  • Ensure quarter-wave plate fast axes are oriented at precisely 45° to polarizer transmission axes to achieve true circular polarization.
  • Avoid holding specimens with bare warm hands prior to testing to prevent localized thermal birefringence fringe artifacts.
  • Operate within the proportional elastic optical limit of the polycarbonate material to avoid time-dependent optical viscoelastic creep.

Theoretical assumptions & boundary condition limits

  • The civil engineering physical models apply within the specified calibrated operational window (5.0 to 30.0 MPa).
  • Assumes homogeneous, isotropic continuum material behavior without unmodeled micro-fracture anomalies.
  • Continuous sensor readings reflect ordinary least-squares regression across successive observations.
  • Extreme inelastic yielding or cyclic degradation requires non-linear constitutive continuum damage mechanics.
