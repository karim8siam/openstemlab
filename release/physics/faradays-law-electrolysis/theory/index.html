01Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis and Modern Atomic Theory

Michael Faraday's groundbreaking 1834 experimental investigations of electrolysis established the empirical foundation of quantitative electrochemistry and provided the earliest quantitative evidence that electricity is quantized into discrete elementary units of charge. Faraday formulated two fundamental laws: (1) First Law of Electrolysis: The mass m of any substance liberated or deposited at an electrode during electrolysis is directly proportional to the total electrical charge Q = I · t that passes through the electrolyte: m ∝ Q ⟹ m = Z · Q. The constant of proportionality Z is defined as the electrochemical equivalent (ECE) of the substance. (2) Second Law of Electrolysis: When the same quantity of electric charge Q is passed through different chemical electrolytes in series, the masses of the various substances liberated are directly proportional to their chemical equivalent weights (the ratio of atomic weight M to ionic valence n): m ∝ (M / n). Combining both laws into a unified relationship yields m = (M / n F) · Q, where F is Faraday's constant (the electric charge carried by one mole of electrons, F ≈ 96485 C/mol). When combined with the discovery of the electron and Millikan's oil drop experiment (elementary charge e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C), Faraday's constant directly yields Avogadro's number: N_A = F / e.

m = Z \cdot Q = \left(\frac{M}{n F}\right) Q = \left(\frac{M}{n F}\right) I \cdot t, \quad F = e \cdot N_A \approx 96485.3 \; \text{C mol}^{-1}
How did Faraday's laws of electrolysis provide early evidence for the discrete, quantized nature of electrical charge decades before the discovery of the electron?

Because the deposition of exactly one mole of a monovalent element (like Ag⁺) always requires exactly 96485 C of charge, while one mole of a divalent element (like Cu²⁺) requires exactly twice that charge (2 × 96485 C), it proved that electricity is carried by individual microscopic particles, each transferring a fixed, identical integer unit of electric charge.

02Electrode Kinetics and Voltameter Chemical Reactions

In a copper voltameter, electrical conduction through the external circuit is metallic (electron drift), whereas conduction through the liquid phase is electrolytic (migration of hydrated cupric ions Cu²⁺ and sulfate ions SO₄²⁻). At the anode (positive electrode), metallic copper undergoes oxidation, shedding valence electrons into the external wiring and dissolving into the solution: Cu(s) ⟶ Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ (E° = +0.34 V vs SHE). At the cathode (negative electrode), incoming electrons from the external circuit reduce aqueous cupric ions, depositing neutral crystalline copper atoms onto the cathode plate: Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⟶ Cu(s). Consequently, the concentration of the copper sulfate electrolyte solution remains virtually constant throughout electrolysis, because copper dissolves from the anode at the exact same molar rate at which it deposits onto the cathode. The addition of dilute sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) serves two critical functions: it increases the ionic conductivity of the bath, reducing cell internal resistance and Joule heating, and it suppresses the hydrolysis of copper ions, preventing the precipitation of insoluble cuprous oxide (Cu₂O) or basic copper carbonates.

\text{Anode: } \text{Cu}(s) \longrightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^-, \quad \text{Cathode: } \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{Cu}(s)
Why is a small volume of ethyl alcohol added to the copper sulfate electrolyte solution in a laboratory voltameter?

Freshly deposited electrolytic copper consists of microcrystalline, high-surface-area granules that are chemically vulnerable to atmospheric oxidation. Alcohol acts as an antioxidant reducing agent in the bath, preventing dissolved atmospheric oxygen from oxidizing the fresh copper deposit into cuprous oxide (Cu₂O).

03Current Density and Structural Quality of the Deposit

The physical structure and adherence of the deposited copper layer depend critically on the cathodic current density, defined as the electrolysis current divided by the immersed surface area of the cathode: J = I / A (in A/cm² or mA/cm²). If the current density is too high (J > 25 mA/cm²), cupric ions in the immediate boundary layer adjacent to the cathode are depleted faster than they can be replenished by liquid diffusion (mass-transport limitation). To maintain the current, the cathode potential swings negative until hydrogen ions are co-reduced, producing hydrogen gas bubbles: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⟶ H₂(g). These bubbles agitate the deposit, causing the copper to grow as loose, spongy, dark-brown dendritic powder that easily flakes off during washing, producing a large negative mass error. Conversely, if current density is too low (J < 5 mA/cm²), side reactions involving the formation of monovalent cuprous ions (Cu⁺) become non-negligible, causing more mass to deposit per Coulomb than predicted for Cu²⁺. Maintaining current density strictly within the optimal range of 10 to 20 mA/cm² yields a hard, smooth, bright salmon-pink metallic deposit that adheres tenaciously to the plate.

J_{\text{cathode}} = \frac{I}{2 \cdot w \cdot h} \approx 15.0 \; \text{mA cm}^{-2} \quad (10 \le J \le 20 \; \text{mA cm}^{-2})
If a rectangular cathode plate measuring 5 cm by 6 cm is immersed to a depth of 4 cm in the voltameter, what current I should be set to achieve an optimal current density of 15 mA/cm²?

The cathode plate deposits copper on both front and back faces. Active area is A = 2 × (5 cm × 4 cm) = 40 cm². To achieve J = 15 mA/cm², the current must be I = J × A = 15 mA/cm² × 40 cm² = 600 mA = 0.600 A.

04Linear Regression of Deposited Mass Against Total Charge

The quantitative evaluation of the electrochemical equivalent Z relies on plotting the deposited mass m against total charge Q across six distinct trials. In standard slope-intercept form, the relationship is m = Z · Q + b, where independent variable x = Q (in Coulombs), dependent variable y = m (in grams), slope m_slope = Z (in g/C), and intercept b is ideally 0.0000 g. Applying ordinary least-squares regression across all data points averages out digital balance rounding and minor handling fluctuations. The slope directly yields the electrochemical equivalent Z_est. Dividing the known molar mass of copper (M = 63.546 g/mol) by 2 · Z_est immediately isolates the experimental Faraday constant: F_est = M / (2 · Z_est). Furthermore, any statistically significant non-zero vertical intercept b provides diagnostic insight: a positive intercept (b > 0) indicates incomplete removal of electrolyte salts or surface oxidation during drying, while a negative intercept (b < 0) indicates slight loss of copper powder during vigorous water rinsing.

Z = m_{\text{slope}} = \frac{\sum (Q_i - \bar{Q})(m_i - \bar{m})}{\sum (Q_i - \bar{Q})^2}, \quad F = \frac{M_{\text{Cu}}}{2 \cdot Z}, \quad N_A = \frac{F}{e}
If a student's linear fit of m vs Q yields a positive vertical intercept b = +0.0025 g, what physical error during the procedure most likely produced this offset?

A positive intercept indicates a constant extra mass added to every measurement regardless of charge, typically caused by residual dried copper sulfate salts left on the plate due to inadequate rinsing with distilled water, or surface oxidation from overheating in the drying oven.

05Silver vs. Copper Voltameters in Primary Electrical Metrology

Historically, before the modern 2019 SI redefinition based on fixed fundamental constants, the international standard ampere was formally defined in terms of electrolysis: The ampere is that constant current which, when passed through an aqueous solution of silver nitrate, deposits silver at the rate of 0.00111800 grams per second. The silver voltameter served as the primary international standard for current because silver is monovalent (Ag⁺, n = 1), has a high atomic mass (M = 107.868 g/mol), and exhibits an exceptionally large electrochemical equivalent: Z_Ag = 1.11800 mg/C (3.4 times larger than copper). This large mass gain maximized weighing resolution on chemical balances. However, silver nitrate is chemically hazardous, photosensitive, expensive, and forms fragile needle-like crystals that detach easily. Consequently, for university physics laboratories and industrial calibration, the copper voltameter is the universal choice: copper is inexpensive, robust, forms smooth adherent coatings, and avoids toxic heavy-metal handling while still providing four-decimal precision.

Z_{\text{Ag}} = \frac{107.868 \times 10^{-3}}{1 \times 96485.3} = 1.1180 \times 10^{-6} \; \text{kg C}^{-1} = 1.1180 \; \text{mg C}^{-1} \approx 3.395 \cdot Z_{\text{Cu}}
If a copper voltameter and a silver voltameter are connected in series in the same circuit, which plate will gain more mass, and what is the exact theoretical ratio of their mass gains?

Because they are in series, exactly the same charge Q passes through both. By Faraday's second law, m_Ag / m_Cu = (M_Ag / n_Ag) / (M_Cu / n_Cu) = (107.868 / 1) / (63.546 / 2) = 107.868 / 31.773 = 3.395. The silver cathode gains exactly 3.395 times more mass than the copper cathode.

06Anion and Cation Transport Numbers (Hittorf Migration)

Although copper is the sole species deposited at the cathode and dissolved at the anode, electrical current inside the liquid is carried simultaneously by both positive cupric ions (Cu²⁺) migrating toward the cathode and negative sulfate ions (SO₄²⁻) migrating toward the anode. The fraction of total current carried by each ionic species is defined as its transport number (or transference number): t₊ = I₊ / I_total and t₋ = I₋ / I_total, where t₊ + t₋ ≡ 1. Because ionic mobility depends on hydrodynamic ionic radius and hydration shell volume, sulfate ions in aqueous solution have slightly higher mobility than hydrated cupric ions, resulting in transport numbers of approximately t₊(Cu²⁺) ≈ 0.36 and t₋(SO₄²⁻) ≈ 0.64. As a result of this unequal mobility, concentration gradients develop near the electrodes (Hittorf effect): the electrolyte near the anode becomes concentrated in CuSO₄, while the electrolyte near the cathode becomes diluted. Natural fluid convection and mechanical stirring counteract this concentration gradient, ensuring steady, uniform deposition.

t_+ = \frac{u_+}{u_+ + u_-} \approx 0.36, \quad t_- = \frac{u_-}{u_+ + u_-} \approx 0.64, \quad t_+ + t_- = 1
Does the fact that cupric ions carry only 36% of the current in the solution violate Faraday's first law that m = (M/2F) · Q?

No. Faraday's law governs the total chemical conversion at the electrode interfaces, where every 2 electrons that pass through the external wire must reduce exactly one Cu²⁺ ion at the cathode surface. Transport numbers describe how charge is shared by ions migrating through the bulk solution; any deficit in Cu²⁺ arrival is compensated by SO₄²⁻ ions migrating away from the cathode.

07Experimental Error Budget: Handling, Rinsing, and Oxidation

Achieving four-decimal accuracy (diff < 0.1%) in a copper voltameter requires meticulous laboratory technique to eliminate systematic handling errors. The primary physical failure modes include: (1) Surface oxidation: If the freshly washed cathode is dried at excessive temperatures (> 80 °C) or left exposed to moist air, copper reacts with oxygen to form cuprous oxide (2Cu + ½O₂ ⟶ Cu₂O), increasing apparent mass by up to 0.5%. Rinsing with high-purity ethanol removes water and allows rapid drying at low temperature. (2) Mechanical abrasion: Rubbing or wiping the cathode with filter paper dislodges fragile copper microcrystals. The plate must be dipped, never wiped. (3) Electrolyte entrapment: If copper sulfate solution is not completely washed away with distilled water, residual salt crystals dry on the plate, adding false mass. (4) Current drift: If current is allowed to sag as the rheostat warms, using nominal current produces systematic error in Q; integrating current digitally eliminates this drift.

\Delta m_{\text{meas}} = \Delta m_{\text{Cu}} + \Delta m_{\text{oxide}} + \Delta m_{\text{salt}} - \Delta m_{\text{loss}}
Why must the cathode be immersed in alcohol immediately after being washed with distilled water?

Alcohol has high volatility and miscibility with water; dipping the cathode washes away residual water droplets and allows the plate to dry in seconds at room temperature without requiring prolonged heating that would cause thermal oxidation.
