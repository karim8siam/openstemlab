Simulation assumptions and limits

This deterministic model assumes a perfectly aligned transmission grating, monochromatic parallel illumination and exact groove spacing. It computes peak positions and rounds the reduced angle to 0.01°. It does not predict intensity, blaze efficiency, missing orders, slit profiles, spectral overlap, aperture clipping or telescope resolution. Availability of orders 1 to 5 in this lesson does not guarantee their practical visibility in every apparatus. The ordinary fit omits angle-dependent vertical weights and shared grating-calibration uncertainty. The generator uses wavelength 550 nm and density 300 lines/mm, giving d = 10⁻³/300 m. For orders 1 to 5, theta = arcsin(m lambda/d) is rounded to 0.01 degrees. The stored y value is sin(theta_deg × pi/180), x is the integer order and the estimated wavelength is fitted gradient times d. The fifth-order sine is 0.825, so it is geometrically permitted. The reference wavelength is a chosen teaching parameter rather than an identification of a particular atomic emission line. The displayed raw angle represents half a symmetric left/right separation; independent telescope readings are not generated.

Know your apparatus.

Transmission grating and holder

Provide parallel equally spaced grooves with density 300 lines per millimetre; hold the grating perpendicular to the incident beam.

Monochromatic source and collimator

Send a narrow, approximately parallel beam of one wavelength toward the grating.

Spectrometer telescope and angular scale

Centre left and right images of the same spectral line and determine their angular separation.

Levelled rotating table

Keep the grating vertical and permit alignment of its normal with the incoming beam.

FOLLOW THE METHOD

A reason behind every step.

Determine the wavelength of monochromatic light from the angles of successive diffraction orders produced by a transmission grating at normal incidence. Suggested independent settings: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 order.

1

Prepare and align

Focus the eyepiece crosshairs, focus the telescope for a distant image and adjust the collimator for a sharp narrow slit. Level the grating and set its normal along the incident beam.

Why this step matters

The simple grating equation used here assumes normal incidence and a collimated beam.

2

Record the spacing

Read the specified groove density and convert 300 lines/mm into d = 10⁻³/300 m. Identify the central undeviated image as order zero.

Why this step matters

Correct spacing and an unambiguous zero prevent unit and order errors.

3

Choose a diffraction order

Select a positive order from 1 to 5 in the virtual apparatus. In a physical spectrometer locate the same monochromatic line in the corresponding orders on both sides.

Why this step matters

Pairing different orders or spectral lines produces an invalid half-separation.

4

Measure and record the angle

Centre the left and right images and compute half their angular separation after accounting for any scale wrap. The simulator returns this reduced angle directly, rounded to 0.01°. Record it before changing order.

Why this step matters

A common angular zero offset cancels in a symmetric pair; the raw virtual angle is not the full left-to-right separation.

5

Collect the order series

Take readings for orders 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. Calculate sin(theta) using the degree setting or convert each angle into radians first. Fit sin(theta) = s m + b.

Why this step matters

Using the sine transformation preserves the exact angular relation across the full range.

6

Calculate and assess wavelength

Multiply the slope by grating spacing, then convert metres to nanometres by multiplying by 10⁹. Check an individual row with lambda = d sin(theta)/m and discuss calibration, alignment and model omissions.

Why this step matters

The graph result and a manual row calculation provide independent arithmetic checks.
